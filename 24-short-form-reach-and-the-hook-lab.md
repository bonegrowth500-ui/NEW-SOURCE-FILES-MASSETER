# 24 · Short-Form: Reach and the Hook Lab
*Part VI — The Ecosystem*

**The shift:** from *"Short-form is for going viral"* to *"Short-form is the early reach engine and a noisy lab, judged by eligible-adult yield and confirmed in long-form."*

Two of your shorts go up on the same Tuesday. The first answers whether mewing works, and by Friday it shows [~400k] views. Follows jump by [~2,000], and the comments fill with rating requests, several from boys who say they're 15. [Three] adults walk through your door, the owned path that starts with your self-assessment. The second opens on a 27-year-old who has spent a year on [his routine] and can't tell if any of it works. It gets [~9k] engaged views, sends [seven] adults through your door, and [one] books a fit conversation, the free first call that ends in one recommendation.

Your dashboard calls the first one the win. Your calendar says it was the second. The first found the category's youngest viewers, who engage fastest and can't buy, and the second found a man in the middle of his stall. Neither result says much about next Tuesday, because one week can't separate a framing that works from one that got lucky.

This module runs short-form as two things at once. In the early months it's your main reach engine, judged by the eligible adults it sends you, the legal adults who could pay without new credit. All along it's a lab for framings. Nothing there counts until it wins twice and holds up in long-form.

## 1. The Early Reach Engine and the Default Platform Set

In your first four months, short-form is the main way strangers find you. Its feeds show each piece to people who have never heard of you from the first week, and its worth is counted in eligible adults at your door.

### A new channel's reach comes from the feed

Long-form compounds through search and suggested traffic, and a new channel has almost none of either. Its early pieces reach mostly people who already know you. Short-form feeds work the other way round. Each piece is shown first to a small group of strangers and widened when they stay, so a short posted in week one can reach thousands of people with no audience behind it.

Reach binds first, and month 3 is decided by conversations and the routes that produce them. So short-form is one of the labeled sources in the Founding Sprint, your first conversations without an audience, each booking tagged with its route (Module 9). The early design week gives it about 3.5 hours for 4–7 native pieces a week, its largest content line.

### The door is the scoreboard

A short view is worth far less than a long-form view. Plan on roughly 0.1–1 raw lead per 1,000 engaged short-form views, against 2–5 for long-form early on. Only 30–70% of raw leads are eligible adults, and packaging moves that share more than the choice of platform does. In the samples that exist, most of this category's hardcore audience is under 18, and rating and comparison content pulls them in fastest. So views and follows, read alone, are the numbers most likely to mislead you. The one that doesn't is eligible-adult yield: eligible leads per 1,000 engaged views, by source.

Two habits raise it. The first is adult-situation framing, the short-form end of the Age-Up Dial, the packaging choices that lower the minors' share (Module 23). A short opens on the 27-year-old who has guessed for a year, or the man whose headshot is about to go on the company site. It never opens on an idealized jaw, a rating, or two faces compared.

The second habit is profile routing. A Short can link one long-form piece on your channel, so each links the video that pays its point off. Every profile's one link goes to your self-assessment, whose age fork sends anyone under 18 to the education lane, public content and an orthodontist referral, before anything is stored.

### Shorts first, Reels second, TikTok on proof

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Shorts only | Your editing budget covers one native pass per piece | No Reels feed your Instagram router, so fewer adults reach you by DM |
| Shorts, then Reels | Early, with long-form running and Instagram as your router | A second native pass per piece |
| Shorts, Reels, and TikTok | A labeled trial matches Reels' eligible adults per editing hour over about 30 door completions, and your budget covers a third pass | A third pass, the longest route to your door, and no feed into long-form or your router |

**Default: Shorts first, Reels second.** Shorts feed your long-form channel. Few viewers follow a Short's link, but the ones who do meet your decision-stage pieces and your pitch, on the platform that reaches more US adults than any other and nearly all of those aged 18–29. Reels feed your Instagram router, on a platform that reaches about half of US adults. Men who won't comment in public will send a keyword there in private, and it returns your door link. TikTok reaches a little over a third of US adults and feeds neither owned asset, so the adults it sends to your door are its whole output.

Add it only when a labeled trial of pieces that already won their pairs sends eligible adults at least as cheaply per editing hour as Reels do, over about 30 door completions. Whatever the set, it answers to the Platform Count Rule: run only as many platforms as your editing budget serves, and drop one before long-form slips (Module 25).

**Shorts feed long-form, Reels feed the router, and TikTok earns its place on yield.**

### Minor exposure is weighed honestly

None of the three is an adult feed. Teens use all of them heavily, and YouTube reaches more of them each day than any other platform, so Shorts first rests on what happens after the view. A Shorts viewer can walk into long-form, where decision-stage topics thin out the youngest. YouTube also limits how often it repeats videos that compare or idealize physical features to teens, and your packaging never makes those anyway. On every platform, packaging and the door's age fork do the protecting, since no setting keeps a 15-year-old out of your comments.

**When the signals disagree.** A TikTok trial piece runs to [40 times] your usual views and adds [~3,000] follows in a weekend. The platform's age data puts [most] of its viewers under 18, and its door label shows [four] completions, [one] of them eligible. Views say move your week to TikTok, and yield says the trial is failing. Read the framing first. If it leaned toward a rating, a comparison, or an idealized jaw, take the piece down whatever it earned; if it was an adult situation that found a young pocket, it can stay.

Then judge the platform on the trial's own terms, eligible leads per editing hour over about 30 door completions, and never on a weekend. Followers are future distribution, so a young follow spike can tilt who sees your next pieces. That's a reason to feed it no sequels. The framing enters your lab with no wins, since views were never its job's number. Hide the rating requests under your published comment policy. Anyone who says he's under 18 gets the public education-lane answer, with no DM and no route to the door.

## 2. The Shorts Job Selector

Every short gets one job before it's made, and it's judged only by that job's number. The number every feed shows first counts everyone, and the youngest viewers, who can't buy, pile into it fastest.

### One leaderboard pulls every piece younger

Put every short on one leaderboard of views, and the winners are the pieces the youngest viewers engage with, since they engage first. The pieces built to send adults to your door look like losers and get dropped. The number you steer by chooses what you make next, so a views leaderboard slowly hands your packaging to 15-year-olds.

Views are also the least stable count you have. Some platforms now count a view from a piece's first frame, while their analytics keep an engaged view for people who stayed. So count every short-form rate per 1,000 engaged views, by source, and date a break whenever a platform changes what it counts. That's Denominator Discipline, a denominator you can hold still under every rate (Module 12).

### Four jobs, each with its own number

A long-form piece carries two jobs at once, and the Two-Job Scorecard judges it on reach and on belief by eligible-adult yield. A short has seconds, so it carries one:

| Job | What the piece does | Judge it by | Its one ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reach | Puts an adult situation in front of strangers | Adult share of viewers first, then long-form clicks and follows per 1,000 engaged views | The next piece, or a follow |
| Lead | Gives a man with a live question his next step | Door completions per 1,000 engaged views (plan ~0.1–1), then eligible leads by label | The door |
| Proof | Shows your standard working: how you capture, your dated log, a sample review, a consented clip | Profile visits, follows, and long-form clicks per 1,000 engaged views | The long-form piece behind it, or the door |
| Offer | Tells a warm viewer what you sell, who it's for and isn't, the price, and where | Offer-page visits from the piece | The offer page, on surfaces followers see first |

Reach, lead, and proof pieces sit on the Stranger rung of the Warmth Ladder, the rule that matches each asset's ask to its viewer's warmth (Module 18). So they ask only for the next piece, a follow, or the door. The offer job belongs to viewers who have already come back, so it lives on Stories and your offer page, never in a feed that tests every piece on strangers, many of them minors. Even there, a follow counts as coming back only when adult-framed pieces earned it, which is one more reason a young follow spike is a cost.

**One job per short, one number per job.**

That's the **Shorts Job Selector**: name each short's job before you make it, and judge it by that job's number. It keeps a lead piece from being killed for low views, and a spike from being mistaken for demand.

### One point makes four pieces

Take one defensible point: photos taken under different light can't be compared. As a reach piece, it opens on eight months of mirror photos, each under a different light, and points to the next piece. As proof, it shows your published Capture Standard, the matched conditions every capture follows (Module 6), and the date you published it, then points to the long-form piece behind it. As a lead piece, the same opening ends on the door: "If you're an adult and can't tell what's moved, the free self-assessment in my profile sorts out where you are."

The offer piece runs only where viewers have already come back: on Stories, which reach your followers first, and on your offer page. It names the Program, 12 weeks of written review with captures at weeks 0, 6, and 12, taken the same way every time. It says who it's for, adults who can't tell what's working, and who it isn't for: anyone under 18, and anyone who wants a promise about bone. Then it states [$price] and points to the page for the next start, since a date spoken into a piece goes stale.

Tag each piece with its job, link, and rung when you plan the batch, and read each job's number as a count at your weekly review. A rate replaces its planning range only after about 30 events stand behind it, door completions for the lead job. Early, most pieces take the reach or lead job. Proof starts with your own process in month 1 and adds client clips once consent exists, and offer pieces stay rare.

## 3. The Two-Win Rule

Short-form is also a lab for framings, and a single win there is mostly noise. So a framing counts only after it wins twice and then holds in a long-form title test.

### Why one win proves little here

A framing is the situation and promise a piece opens with. "27 and asking if it's too late" and "What can an adult still change?" can carry the same point to different people. In this niche the framing decides who stops scrolling: the adult in the middle of his stall, or the 15-year-old who wants a rating. Short-form makes a cheap, fast lab and a noisy one, and this category adds noise of its own:

- A piece's first audience is a small seed of strangers, many of them under 18, so a win can mean one young pocket liked it.
- Buyers decide in clusters, after payday, a wedding, or a work photo, so a week measures your calendar as much as your framing.
- The bone question pulls views whatever the framing, so a winner on that topic may be the topic winning.
- Counts are small. At roughly 0.1–1 raw lead per 1,000 engaged views, a piece with [5,000] engaged views sends between none and five people to your door, so one extra adult can double its rate.

### A win needs a pair, the job's number, and an adult audience

Pair two framings of the same point in the same week, with the same job and length, so the framing is the only thing that differs. Where a platform lets you trial a piece with non-followers before your followers see it, use that slot for pairs, since it holds the audience steadier. The framing whose piece beats its pair on the job's number wins, as long as its adult share holds at or above the share your recent pieces average. Views, likes, and follows alone never make a win.

A framing that wins twice, on different pieces in different weeks, has earned a long-form test. The second win costs one more batch, and it rules out most one-off causes: a lucky seed, a trend, a week that happened to hold payday.

### Confirmation happens in long-form

A short-form win measures a stranger's thumb in the first second. A long-form title measures an adult deciding to give you part of his evening, in front of an older and warmer audience, so it's an independent test run on the viewers who buy. Put the framing into the title and thumbnail of the next long-form piece whose topic fits. Run it against your current default in your platform's own title and thumbnail test. That test splits impressions between versions and judges them by watch time, so a framing that wins the click and loses the viewer loses the test. "Performed the same" or "inconclusive" counts as a no.

**Win twice in short-form, then once in long-form, or it's still a guess.**

That's the **Two-Win Rule**: a short-form framing counts only after it wins repeatedly and is then confirmed in a long-form test. A confirmed framing becomes a packaging default, and it still answers to yield. If the pieces titled with it draw a younger audience over the next window, it loses its place.

### How noise fools you

| What you saw | What it can be | The check |
|---|---|---|
| One piece at [10 times] your usual views | A young pocket, a trend, a lucky seed | Its adult share, and how its pair did |
| A framing that won on views | The youngest viewers engaging first | The job's number |
| A winner on the bone question | The topic pulling, whatever the framing | A pair on the same topic |
| Three wins you remember | Five losses you don't | A written record of every pair |
| A jump the week a platform recounted views | A new denominator | Engaged views, with the break dated |

Reid *(composite operator)* shows what skipping the checks costs. One of his shorts, framed ["what nobody tells you at 22"], runs to [12 times] his usual views in a weekend. He retitles his next [three] long-form pieces in the same pattern. Their click-through falls, their audience runs younger, and his eligible leads for the month drop by [about a quarter]. The piece had no pair, its adult share sat under [a third], and the framing never won again. The rule would have stopped it at the first check and saved him a month of titles.

### Run the lab inside the batch

Test at most two framings at a time, each in one pair a week, inside the 4–7 pieces you already make. Keep a hook log of every pair: the date, the point, both framings, the job's number, and each piece's adult share. A framing that loses two pairs leaves the lab, and one that wins two goes to the next long-form piece whose topic fits. Test framings, never stakes. Pieces get faster and tighter in pace, never hotter in what they threaten, so no shame variant, rating, or idealized jaw enters a pair, whatever it might win.

## 4. The Native Tax, Derivatives, and Reels Craft

Every platform charges a tax on content that looks imported, so pay it on purpose. Post one clean master of your own work with a light native pass for each platform, and fund full re-cuts only where they've proved they pay.

### Imports get kept out of the feed

Platforms want pieces made for them, and they check. Reposted material and uploads carrying another app's watermark get kept out of the feeds that reach strangers. The major platforms now look for another app's logo and for material that already exists elsewhere, while your own original footage, posted clean, is generally fine. Viewers run the same test faster and scroll past a letterboxed clip or another app's captions as an import.

In this category the check works in your favor. Many of the accounts it catches here are aggregators reposting other people's transformation photos, and never reposting anyone else's footage keeps you visibly apart from them. That cost of belonging is the **Native Tax**: every platform charges for non-native content, so size the adaptation honestly. Three options cover the sizes:

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| One clean master, a light pass per platform | Your own footage, with no other app's logo on it | Its own first line, on-screen text, cover, and caption per platform |
| A full re-cut per platform | That platform's re-cut pieces have beaten the light pass twice on the same job's number | Editing hours out of your 3.5 a week |
| The same file everywhere, watermark and all | Never | Kept out of the feeds that reach strangers |

**Default: one clean master, cross-posted with a light native pass.** The tax that matters is detection plus the imported look. A clean master with its own first line, text, cover, and caption clears both for the least editing. Switch a platform to full re-cuts only when its re-cut pieces beat the light pass twice on the same job's number. That's the Two-Win logic, applied to your editing budget.

### Create once, cut many

Filming happens in batches every two weeks, and one session should feed both surfaces. Before you film, mark the defensible points in each long-form script. Shoot the strongest two or three again as vertical takes while the lights are up. Your editor then cuts derivatives, short clips taken from the long-form piece, and each goes out as one clean master with its pass for each platform. A derivative takes the rung of the surface it's posted to, so a clip cut from a decision-stage pitch loses its price and checkout link before it reaches a feed. Some moments cut well, and some never do:

| Cuts well | Never cuts |
|---|---|
| A core claim, said whole in its fixed words | The pitch, whose price belongs on warm surfaces |
| A capture fact, like light or distance | A before/after segment |
| A stall told back from anonymized patterns | An outcome story without its full count |
| A teardown's steelman and its sharp point, together | A debated point without its caveat |
| A destination line with its perspective line | Anything a commenter under 18 wrote |

An editor can take the cutting from Early, since it's production. Every title, caption, and clip still comes back to you before it's scheduled, because a clip is a claim in your name. The clip brief in this module's templates gives him what he needs to cut safely: the source, the point, its caveat, the job, and the ask. The Platform Count Rule caps the whole pipeline. When your week can't pay a platform's tax, drop the platform before long-form pays it.

**One master per point, a light pass per platform, your approval on every cut.**

### Reels craft for Instagram

Instagram's viewers reach you by a different road. Many check your profile before they follow or tap your link, and the adults who want more tend to write by DM rather than comment. Four parts of the craft follow from that.

**Covers.** The cover is what your profile grid shows, and the grid is where a Reels viewer checks you before following. Make it read as a library of adult decisions, one plain line per cover in the same type and frame. Never use a face pair or your own jaw angle, since your face carries trust and is never evidence for the method. Pin the pieces that route, starting with the Honest Answer, your straight public answer to the bone question.

**Text.** Many viewers watch with the sound off, so the on-screen text carries the one point by itself, in the words he'd use. Claims from your Canon, your 5–7 core claims in fixed words, stay word for word (Module 14). A caveat stays on screen as long as its claim does, and clear of the app's buttons, since a covered caveat is a missing one.

**Pacing.** Cut pauses and repeats, never the caveat. The first line names a situation, the payoff lands in the same piece, and the last line makes one ask. That's the Payoff Test, every curiosity device paid off in the same asset (Intro).

**The send-to-a-friend test.** Instagram weighs sends heavily when it shows a piece to people who don't follow you, so design for the send. Then test it: would an adult forward this to a friend, and would the friend feel recognized rather than rated? A piece that names a situation, like eight months with nothing measured, passes. So does one that arms him against a grift pattern, because forwarding it makes the sender look careful. A piece whose forward says "this is about your face" fails, however many sends it would earn, because the man receiving it never asked.

## 5. The Clip Context Check and the One-Defensible-Point Rule

Every clip you publish is a new claim in your name, so it carries its own caveat or it doesn't ship. And every short is built on one point that's true as it stands.

### A cut sentence is a new claim

A long-form piece earns its claims in order: the question, what the evidence says, how strong it is, and only then the point. Cut a few seconds from it and the point travels alone. "Some adults report their jaw looks different" loses "that's debated, and I don't sell it" and becomes a promise the category's worst channels would make. The feed strips the rest of the context too, and the first frame travels alone as a thumbnail. A clip missing its caveat is the exact screenshot a hostile critic looks for, and the man who sees only the clip never sees the minute that qualified it.

That's the job of the **Clip Context Check**: every extracted clip is an original claim, so restore the caveat or drop it. Find the sentence that qualified the point in the source. If the clip needs it to be true, it goes on screen for as long as the claim does, in words he can read without sound. If it won't fit, the clip doesn't run. Canon claims go out word for word, and your editor flags while you decide.

**Put the caveat back or drop the clip.**

### One true point per short

A short has room for one point, and a viewer carries away one sentence. If that sentence needs a second one to be true, the second is the one he'll lose. So each short follows the one-defensible-point rule, one true point per short piece that needs no caveat. Anything that needs a tier, a count, or an "except" goes to long-form, where it fits, or into a clip that carries it on screen:

| The point | True as stated? | The move |
|---|---|---|
| "Photos taken under different light can't be compared." | Yes, in every case | Ship it |
| "Behavior gets measured every week; appearance gets captured rarely, the same way every time." | Yes: a process fact you control | Canon claim 3, word for word |
| "Some adults see their jaw change." | No: it's debated, and it reads as a promise | Say the true version, that it's debated and you don't sell it, or leave it to long-form |
| "Your stall isn't genetic." | No: nobody can say that about every stall | Claim 2: "Most stalls we see are direction problems: months of real effort with no map and nothing measured. Measuring is how you'd know if yours is." |
| "Mewing works if you stay consistent." | No: a structural claim nobody can support | Never |

### One sharp point at a practice

A short suits *Fight ideas, not people* at its pointed strength, because one defensible point can carry an edge when it's aimed at a practice. Take a short cut from your quarterly teardown of a published ad. Blur the face, remove the handle and watermark, and paraphrase the words:

> **You:** "The man in this ad may well have worked hard, and wanting what he wanted is fair. Now look at the light: overhead in the before, in front of him in the after. You're comparing two lamps. Before you buy a jaw device, ask for dated photos taken the same way. My Capture Standard is in my profile, so hold me to the same test."

Notice the move: the steelman in one line, one sharp point at the practice, then a check he can run on anyone, you included. It repairs Guide, because he learns the test your own proof has to pass: the Honest-Evidence Test, the tells of manufactured proof taught so buyers can judge anyone (Module 16).

> **Theo** *(composite, Burned Struggler)*: "So you're the one honest guy. Convenient."
>
> **You:** "Don't take my word for it. My Capture Standard and my dated log are public, so check them first. If they hold up, the free self-assessment in my profile is the next step."

The line you never say is "Look at that guy's jaw, it's obviously fake," which comments on the features of a man who never consented. Keep the edge on the lamp.

### Client results travel with their counts

Consented client clips put a checkable result in a real man's words. *Name the destination boldly* licenses them in organic short-form, so keeping them off it under-sells the men who earned them. They follow the Proof Portability Gradient, which sets where each kind of proof may travel. Contextualized clips run in organic short-form with range and denominator on screen, and outcome proof never runs in ads (Module 27).

None exists in month 1, since consent comes after the fit window, his full refund in the first 14–21 days. It comes only at a measured peak, a reading where his adherence and at least one marker have reached their thresholds. Process testimonials arrive from about week 7 of your first client. Outcome clips wait for the proof milestone, 10 or more graduates with consented process testimonials, and carry a small-sample label.

Before/after pairs stay in long-form and your site library, where the whole Context Stack, the six items that make a result checkable, fits. A short carries his words, his markers, his context, and his counts, and its first frame shows a situation, never a face:

```
First frame: his situation in words, "[24, shift work, eight months of guessing]"
Claim: "[marker type] reached its threshold by week 12"
What else changed: [weight ±~X kg] · [a new job with a longer commute]
Timeline: baseline [date] · week 12 [date] · markers only, no captures shown
Range: [A–B] across [n] graduates · a small sample
Count: started [N] · exited [n] · graduated [n] · no marker at threshold [k]
His words, under [his initials]: "[For the first time I know what's working and what isn't.]"
Perspective: "Most of how people read you was never about your jaw."
On screen throughout: Observed among clients, not caused.
Ask: the free self-assessment in my profile
```

## 6. Cadence by Stage

Short-form gets its most hours while long-form has no search traffic, then hands them to long-form as it compounds. A long-form view is worth several short-form views at your door, so the hours follow the traffic.

| Stage | Short-form a week | What it's for | Who cuts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | About 3.5 h: 4–7 native pieces | The main reach source, on Shorts and Reels | You film; an editor cuts; you approve |
| Growing | About 1.25 h | The lab feeding long-form, plus derivatives | A freelancer cuts; you approve |
| Scaling | About 0.5 h | Mostly derivatives | Freelancers cut; you approve every clip |

### Early: the batch is the engine

Film every two weeks, in the same session as the long-form piece, and leave with 8–14 masters. Each carries its job and its rung, two framings sit in pairs, and the reach and lead jobs take most of the batch. Month 3 is decided by conversations, so the week-3 source check reaches these hours. If your warm network and replies give fewer than 2 held conversations a week, move about 2 hours a week from long-form into short-form batches and Permission-First Replies. Those are public answers on other threads, with a DM only after he says yes.

The same labels are read again at week 12. Fewer than about 15 held conversations by then means reach is short. Short-form and replies take hours from your slack, up to 25 a week, beside the small adult-only paid test that this shortfall opens at months 3–4. The door, the price, and the offer stay as they are, because a reach problem isn't fixed at the door.

### The hours follow compounding

Once long-form draws its own search and suggested traffic, an hour there buys more adults than an hour of shorts. Plan on 2–5 raw leads per 1,000 engaged long-form views against roughly 0.1–1 for short-form, and long-form adds a clickable path and your pitch. So Growing's design week cuts short-form to about 1.25 hours, where it becomes the lab feeding long-form. Scaling's cuts it to about half an hour of approvals, mostly on derivatives a freelancer cut.

The stage line moves the hours, and your labels check the timing. If short-form still books most of your held conversations when revenue says Growing, you're Early on volume, and the binding constraint decides which moves apply. Keep the Early line until eligible leads pass about 50 a month.

### The long-form queue sets the lab's pace

Two-win framings can arrive faster than long-form can confirm them. Early, a long-form piece comes every other week, so the lab gets about two confirmation tests a month, and Growing's three pieces a month raise that only a little. Keep no more framings in test than your long-form queue can confirm within a month or so. Let a two-win framing wait for a piece whose topic fits, because a framing bent onto the wrong topic breaks the promise its title makes, and the watch-time test scores the broken promise, not the framing.

### The line is protected, and so is the lab

The stage line sits inside the Protected Content Minimum, the content hours client work can never eat (Module 11). Short-form above that line is the third cut when a week runs past 25 hours, after building and extra platforms. A short that spreads is never a reason to add hours or a platform. It's a candidate for the lab, where it needs two wins like any other framing.

**Short-form leads while long-form is young, then hands it the hours.**

## Worked Example: Cole's First Four Weeks of Shorts

Cole *(composite operator)* starts in [January] with door v0, the first working version of his door, plus a founding page, the Honest Answer, and a long-form piece every other week. He plans on Band B, the steady band. Short-form gets its 3.5 hours: [five] pieces a week on Shorts and Reels, filmed in two batch sessions and cut by an editor. Nothing is scheduled until he approves it.

**Week 0. The plan.** He tags [20] pieces before filming: [eight] reach, [eight] lead, [four] proof, and no offer pieces, since almost nobody follows him yet. The proof pieces show his own process: his published pre-commitment, his Capture Standard, what his face does and doesn't prove, and a sample weekly review. No client clips exist, and none can until consent does. Four framings enter the lab, two under test each week: [A] "27 and still guessing", [B] "eight months, nothing measured", [C] "what can an adult still change?", and [D] "same mirror, different light".

**Week 1. The check catches a clip.** His editor's derivative from the Honest Answer cuts "[some adults report their jaw looks different]" and leaves out "debated, and I don't sell it". Cole runs the Clip Context Check and puts the caveat back on screen, inside the same sentence, before he approves it. In the week's lead pair, A beats C on door completions per 1,000 engaged views, with a steady adult share.

**Week 2. The spike.** D's reach piece runs to [~25k] engaged views, about [10 times] his usual. By the platform's data [~70%] of its viewers are under 18, and it sends [one] door completion. Its pair, B on the same point, gets [~3k] engaged views, a steady adult share, and more long-form clicks per 1,000, so B wins. The comments under D hold [six] rating requests and [two] boys who say they're 15. ## Checklist: The Clip Context Check

Run it on every clip before it's scheduled. One no holds the clip.

1. Does the clip's point stay true without the sentence before or after it in the source? → Put that sentence on screen, or drop the clip.
2. Does every caveat, tier, or "debated" from the source stay on screen as long as its claim does? → Put it back, or drop the clip.
3. Is every Canon claim word for word? → Paste it from your Canon.
4. Does any client result carry the whole Context Stack, with its range, denominator, and "observed, not caused" on screen, from at least 10 graduates and labeled a small sample until 30? → Cut the result.
5. Does each client's material match the scope he signed: the surfaces, the name, and words only unless he ticked more? → Cut it.
6. Is the first frame an adult situation, with no face pair, rating, comparison, or idealized jaw? → Re-cut the opening.
7. Does the ask fit a feed's Stranger rung, the next piece, a follow, or the door, with no price and no comment keyword? → Change the ask.
8. Is the clip headed for an ad? → Only process proof goes; pull any client result.

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** Reach binds, and short-form is your main source of it, at about 3.5 hours a week on Shorts and Reels. The default move is the batch: 4–7 tagged pieces, two framings in pairs, and every adult labeled at the door. The trap is spending the hours on polish, one perfect short a week instead of tested ones, which starves the reach and the lab together.

**Growing.** Hours bind for short-form now, since its line drops to about 1.25 while long-form takes three pieces a month. The default move is running it as the lab feeding long-form, with a freelancer cutting derivatives. The trap is keeping Early's native volume out of habit when a long-form hour buys more adults.

**Scaling.** Your approval time binds, because freelancers cut most clips and every one still passes you. The default move is a fixed approval slot with the check run on every clip. The trap is letting a clipper's pace set your claims, since one stripped caveat at volume undoes a year of the Honest Answer.

## Standard Check

- **One defensible point, caveats intact.** Each short makes one point true as stated, and each clip carries its caveat on screen or is dropped, so no cut turns a debated claim into a promise (the line on structural claims).
- **No spike buys a young audience.** No idealized-jaw hook, rating, or comparison enters a pair or a sequel, whatever it earns, and minors in comments get the education lane in public (the line on vulnerability).
- **Results travel with their counts.** Consented clips run in organic short-form with range, denominator, and "observed, not caused" on screen, and never in ads (*Name the destination boldly*, inside the line on fabricated proof).
- **The edge goes on the lamp.** Teardown shorts steelman in a line, blur every face, and aim one sharp point at the practice (*Fight ideas, not people*).
