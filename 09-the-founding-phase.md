# 9 · The Founding Phase
*Part III — Start, Extend, Run, Grow*

**The shift:** from *"I need an audience before I can sell"* to *"I sell through disclosed conversations now, my founding clients are R&D, and every one of them joins the founding group."*

It's the first Monday. You have a camera on a tripod, a channel with no subscribers, and the Honest Answer, your straight public answer to the bone question, filmed and waiting for its edit. Door v0, the first working version of your door, went live at [1 am]. Its age fork, the legal-adult question, works, and its checkout asks every buyer whether he can pay without new credit before any pay button appears. Nobody has walked through it yet.

The tempting plan is to post for six months and sell once there's an audience. In this category, that plan waits on the wrong people. The first viewers to engage skew young, and the men most likely to buy in your first months rarely comment under their own names or tell friends what they're working on. They will answer a message that says plainly what it is, reply to a piece that names their exact stall, and book a call whose purpose and price are on the page.

So the honest questions for day one are where your first five conversations come from, and what you'll change in week 3 if they haven't come. This module answers both. Then it makes your first clients leave the business what it can't build alone: a map of why men stall, a check-in tested on real weeks, and its first proof.

## 1. Why You Sell Before You Have an Audience

You don't need an audience to sell; you need conversations, and for your first three months they come from sources you work by hand.

### Month 3 looks the same in every band

Reach Bands, the four planning bands for how many eligible leads a month your channel reaches by month 9, barely differ at month 3. Across all four, the month-3 plan is roughly 3–18 held conversations in the month and at most about 6 new clients. No channel has had time to pull away yet, and the bands separate only from month 6, once reach compounds. So month 3 is decided by conversations, the one number you can move before reach does. Six months of posting while you wait spends the quarter on a number that isn't moving yet.

**You can't choose your band by month 3, but you can choose your conversations.**

An early audience here also overstates your buyers. The youngest viewers engage first, and in self-selected samples most of the category's hardcore audience is under 18, so a video can do big numbers and send your door mostly fork exits. The adult you want does the opposite. He watches without commenting, and he rarely tells anyone he's working on how he looks, because asking for help with it feels like admitting something. Your audience finds you in public, while your buyer has to be reached quietly, one conversation at a time.

### Early conversations teach what a dashboard can't

Your analytics say what got watched. A held conversation says why a man eight months into [his current routine] still can't tell whether it works, what he has bought, what he measures, and the words he uses for all of it. It also tells you which destination he's after: to be taken seriously, to be at ease in photos, or simply to know. Those answers become your first check-in, your first review template, and the language of your pages. At founding volume you hear them from nearly every adult who reaches you, and no dashboard can tell you what stopped the last man who didn't buy.

### Founding revenue pays for the editor

The Early week runs about 20 hours, and roughly 8.5 of them are the Protected Content Minimum, content hours client work can never eat: long-form every other week, 4–7 native short pieces a week, and the emails after each result. A long-form piece costs about 4–6 of your hours with an editor, and a good deal more without one. Long-form is also where a burned buyer goes to check you before he books, so thinning it costs you the buyers who check. The first founding cash buys the editing that keeps it on schedule, which is why founding clients pay the business's costs before they pay you. Keep your other income through these months, and count a founding month that covers the editor as a month that worked.

## 2. The Founding Sprint: Sources and Speed

Your first conversations come from sources you can name and count. Naming them lets you fix a slow start in week 3, while it's still cheap to fix.

### Unlabeled effort hides the failing source

Most first quarters start as a blend: post, answer whatever comes in, message a few friends, and count the calls at month's end. In this niche the blend fails quietly, because its parts fail in different directions. Early replies are real engagement, yet many come from teenagers or from men asking for a face rating. Messages to friends feel productive, yet this buyer rarely tells a friend he's working on how he looks, so friends are seldom the buyer. When the month ends thin, a blend can't tell you which part to fix.

A held conversation needs three things: an eligible adult, meaning a legal adult who can pay from his own income or savings; a reason to talk this week; and a disclosed way to book. Each source supplies them at its own rate and cost, and two facts about this buyer skew every rate. He hides the pursuit, so the people who know him hear about it last. The youngest viewers engage fastest, so early engagement overstates buyers. That's why every source gets a label, a disclosure, and its own line in your weekly count.

### Six sources, each labeled and disclosed

| Source | What it is, and how it's disclosed | What it costs | What to plan on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm network | Adults you know, messaged once each; the first line says it's a business message | Goodwill, so it's used lightly | With early replies, ~2–8 held a month in months 1–3, front-loaded |
| Early replies | People who reply to or comment on your first pieces; your reply routes them to the door | Reply minutes | Counted with the warm network |
| The founding page | A standing page with every offer, price, and seat count, linked from every early asset | One build, kept current | It converts what the other sources send |
| Native short-form | 4–7 pieces a week; your profile routes to the door | ~3.5 h/week | ~0.1–1 lead per 1,000 views; its job is reach |
| Permission-First Replies | Public answers to adults who engage, asking before any private message | Part of ~2 h/week of replies | Adults who already engaged, routed to the door |
| Speed to lead | A personal reply within hours to every adult who finishes the door | ~1 h/week | 10–20% of eligible leads reach a held conversation |

Six labeled sources, a target of 3–6 held conversations a week, and a check in week 3 that moves hours toward whatever produces: that's the **Founding Sprint**, how you get first conversations without an audience. Its rule fits in a line:

**Label every source, count held conversations by source each week, and move hours in week 3.**

The warm-network source, the adults you already know, is the one to use lightly. Message each adult once, personally, with a first line saying it's a business message, and don't follow up unless he replies. Because this buyer hides the pursuit, the message asks for a forward to an adult who's working on this alone. Plan on about 2–8 held conversations a month from it and early replies together in months 1–3, most of them early, since a network you message once doesn't refill. Nobody under 18 gets the message, however well you know his family.

Early replies and Permission-First Replies, answering the idea in public and asking before anything private, carry attention to the door with no public pitch (Module 25). The only thing you send privately is the door, where the age fork and fit questions do their work. A man whose age you can't tell gets the public answer and nothing private, and anyone who says he's under 18 gets the public answer plus the education lane, your public content and an orthodontist referral. A request to rate a face gets the short Honest Answer and the door, never a rating.

Native short-form is the early reach engine, built on adult situations like the 27-year-old who has guessed for a year (Module 24). Adult framing matters because rating and comparison content pulls the youngest viewers fastest, so judge each piece by the eligible leads it sends. Everything points at the founding page. It names the offers premium first, says who they're for and who they aren't, prints every price beside the next one, and ends in one step, the door. It doesn't change as a start approaches, because a page that heats up near a date is a launch.

Speed to lead, a personal reply within hours, then a booking within 24–48 hours, a reminder, and a held conversation, is this stage's quiet advantage, because a reply from the man he watched is his first evidence the brand isn't another bot. Keep the reply personal, and automate booking and reminders once replies pass about an hour a day (Module 5).

### Count held conversations by source

Aim for 3–6 held conversations a week. That's what the Early week's 4.5 conversation hours carry at about an hour each, all-in. Count five numbers weekly: eligible leads, held conversations, enrollments with cash, pieces published, and check-in completion. Then add the column the Sprint depends on, the source of every held conversation, because a count without sources tells you a week was thin and nothing about why.

Set expectations you can keep. Plan on 0–2 clients in month 1 and 2–6 by month 3, depending on your sources. First cash can slip past month 1 if every source runs slow. A plan that says so in advance is one you won't abandon in week 5, when the channel still looks empty.

### The week-3 source check moves hours

The week-3 source check is a fixed read, at the end of week 3, of what your hand-worked sources produce. If warm network plus replies give fewer than 2 held conversations a week, shift about 2 hours a week from long-form to short-form batches and Permission-First Replies. Week 3 is the moment because those sources front-load: your network has had its one message, and your first pieces have drawn most of the replies they'll draw. Long-form gives up the hours because it pays off over months and a new channel gives it little to compound, while short-form and replies can reach adults this week.

Keep the Honest Answer up, since every other piece points to it. Leave price, paid reach, and new platforms alone, because none of them adds a conversation this month. Hold the shift until your weekly count reaches the 3–6 target, then give long-form its hours back.

## 3. The Dual-Purpose Conversation

In your founding months every conversation does two jobs, learning and deciding, and he's told both before he books.

### Both jobs go on the booking page

Men in this category have met the free strategy call that turns into a pitch halfway through, and some have met its mirror image, a "research interview" that ends in a checkout link. Either one confirms what a burned buyer already believes about the category. So the founding call states both jobs before he picks a slot. You're building the program with its first clients and will ask how he has gone about this, and then you'll look at whether it fits him. It ends in one recommendation: the founding group, the Starter Path, your free sequenced path for anyone who shouldn't buy now, or "don't buy".

The same page shows your public price range and asks the affordability question: "Is this comfortable from your own income or savings, without new credit or buy-now-pay-later?" A no books nothing. He gets the free Starter Path, and the pause route is set, the content-free tag that stops sales sends for 60–90 days. The hour you'd have spent learning he can't pay never happens.

That's the **Dual-Purpose Conversation**: a disclosed fit-and-research call of about 45 minutes, with its purpose, the price, and one recommendation stated before he books. With prep and the written recap it costs about an hour, and the Early week budgets for 4–5 of them.

### Research asks what he did

The research half asks about his past, never his predictions. What a man says he'd pay, or plans to do next month, is a loose guide to what he'll do: people name higher prices for hypothetical offers than they pay for real ones, and intentions turn into action far less often than people expect. What he did is a fact, so ask what he tried and for how long, what he bought, what he measured, when he stopped, and what he hoped a photo would show. Praise for your idea costs him nothing, while a booked date and a payment cost him something, which is why they're the signals.

Those answers do two jobs. They're the excavation Close by Contract needs, the close that agrees its structure first: his stall and goal in his own words (Module 19). They're also the raw material of your stall taxonomy. Tell him how they're used: to build the program, stripped of his name, never quoted without separate consent, and kept out of every marketing tool, because what a man says about his face is the last thing he'd want in an ad audience.

What's out is anything that turns the research into a warm-up for the pitch: questions built to make him want the program ("wouldn't it help to have someone check your week?"), hypotheticals about your prices, and a call booked as research that ends with a link. Each makes the disclosure false after the fact, and each fails the Informed-Client Test, whether he'd still say it served him after seeing exactly what you did (Intro). Questions about photos carry one more rule. If his answers describe distress, or checking that crowds out his day, the research and the sale both stop, and the rest of the call is a referral conversation.

### It ends in one recommendation and an ask

The fit half runs the close you'd run on any call: say his stall and goal back, recommend one thing, state the price once, ask, and resolve each real objection. It ends at a yes with day one booked, a no, or his Decision Date, the day he agrees to decide by, on or before the next start's last day to join. A good-fit adult who helps you learn for 45 minutes and leaves without a recommendation and a price has been failed, however generous the call felt.

The disclosure buys one extra question. After a clear no, you may ask once what made it a no, because you said the call was also research, and you write the answer down without arguing with it. If the answer is "I can't afford it", money talk ends: the Starter Path once, and the pause route. Across 25 conversations, those answers are what you'll read at week 12 if the yeses don't come.

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| One 45-minute call, research then fit | Reach binds and you're under the 3–6 target | About an hour all-in per conversation |
| Research prompts in the booking form, then a 20–30-minute fit call | Held conversations reach the target, or calls start eating content hours | Thinner research, written instead of talked through |
| A fit call only | New conversations stop teaching you new stall patterns | You stop hearing what's changing, so keep one research question on the form |

**Default:** one 45-minute call while reach binds, because the hours are spare and every call is also research at no extra cost. Move the research prompts into the booking form once your count holds at 3–6 a week, since by then the extra minutes cost more than they teach.

## 4. The Founding Group and Founding Private Seats

Every founding client joins one group from the very first, at a price near the real one with the next price stated. One or two founding Private seats, premium seats with fixed deliverables, give the premium buyer a real tier from day one.

### One group from the first client

The founding group is every founding client in one standing group from the first, with monthly entry, a new start every month. No one-to-one product comes before it. A one-to-one founding product would have to be retired, and its price history would show one-to-one selling for less than the group, which no later buyer could square with a premium tier. It also wouldn't test what the business runs on: the standing group, the weekly call, and the monthly start. And your first client never waits for your fifth, because a first cohort that starts once it fills has a date nobody can plan around.

While the group has fewer than about four members, review runs at one-to-one depth, about 30–35 care minutes per client-week all-in. You can afford that because reach binds and minutes are spare, and each deep review teaches you what a good one says. As the group passes about four, the review itself settles toward the 12–20 minutes a client-week of the Program, your 12-week flagship, and the weekly call carries more. Put that on the page, because a client promised near one-to-one attention with no end will read the settling as a cut.

The weekly call runs at its fixed time from week 1, even with one member, under handles and with cameras optional. The second client joins a group that already exists, and the first learns in his first week that membership here means logs kept and captures on schedule.

### The founding price sits near the real price

The founding price is about $1.2–1.5k for 12 weeks, with the next price printed beside it from day one. It sits just under the opening band of $1.5–2.2k: close, because founding clients get the real review, and lower, because they also get a program still being built. Warm sources and process proof you can already show, such as a published pre-commitment and a sample weekly review, push it toward the top of the range, and cold sources push it toward the bottom.

Its stated end is the first scheduled Price Step: dated to a start, announced at least 30 days out, landing low in the opening band, and naming what was added (Module 7). The founding group builds that answer: a tested check-in, written photo conditions, and a sample weekly review drawn from real ones. The seats are a stated number from founding Seat Math, the review minutes your Early week protects divided by one founding client-week. When they fill before the step, say so as plainly as you stated the number: "Founding seats are full; the [April] start opens at $[1,500], with [n] seats."

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Founding price near the real one, next price stated | From the first client | A small gap under the opening band, closed by one scheduled step |
| The opening band from the first client | Your sources are warm and your process proof is already public | Fewer yeses in the months when each one also builds the instrument |
| Free or deep-discount "beta" seats | Never: free transformations traded for before-and-afters are how this category's fake proof gets made | Paid proof no buyer can see, and a price nobody paid |

**Default:** the founding price, because it pays for real review before any proof exists. It's also the lowest price the program will ever carry, so no founding client later watches someone pay less for the same seat. Move to the opening band when the founding seats fill or the step's date arrives, whichever comes first.

### Founding Private seats are the premium tier from day one

The Optimizer, the buyer with more money than time, can arrive in your first week: 25–35, well paid, and wary of being seen in a program. One or two founding Private seats at about $4–6k for 12 weeks give him a real tier while your minutes are spare. They sell on speed, privacy, and fixed deliverables, such as [a 24-hour review turnaround] and [a private weekly call], each covered by your service guarantee. They never sell on better results, since in adjacent fields' trials individual and group formats perform about the same at equal intensity. Every Private page says "we never read or score your face", because precision here means deliverables.

Private needs a call, and his usual way in is the priority tier, the faster paid assessment at about $350–600, credited to the seat. Showing the seat first gives every visitor an honest anchor, a real tier someone buys, and hiding it would fail the buyer it fits. One seat can also double a founding month's cash. Stop at two, and sell the second only while the group's reviews keep their turnaround, because a Private seat that slows them spends the minutes your first proof comes from. The seats open the Premium Lane, the Optimizer's path from the priority tier to Private (Module 13).

### Founding graduates are offered Round Two

Founding graduates reach week 12 from about month 4, and each gets the offer his record supports. One whose record shows measured momentum is offered Round Two, the maintenance block at about $0.8–1.2k. One who moved and holds is told he doesn't need it and hears about the Hold, the measurement subscription, while one paid under the non-response clause, the cash refund when no marker moved despite his effort, hears no offer. His Path and Timeline Card, the one-page path and cost he saw before paying, already priced both, so both need prices before your first founding checkout. The block is designed with the back end (Module 10), and it's offered as a renewal ask at his measured peak (Module 22).

## 5. The R&D Harvest

Founding clients buy a program, and the business gets its instruments, so every founding record has to leave something behind.

### The instruments are built on real weeks

No check-in, photo standard, or review template survives its first real client unchanged. A check-in designed at a desk meets a man whose shifts rotate, and the field he skips in week 3 is the one you thought mattered. Photo conditions copied from a guide meet his bathroom light and a phone held at arm's length. A list of why men stall, written from your own months, meets stalls you never had. Review at one-to-one depth finds all of it quickly, which is why the founding group's depth is research as much as service.

The R&D Harvest is what founding clients must produce. Each part has a version 1 that's done when it has survived the founding group. Say so on the founding page, so every founding client agrees to it before he pays:

| Harvest item | Built from | Version 1 is done when | It becomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stall taxonomy | Every founding record and conversation | Each founding client's stall is named, with its signs and first move | The backbone of templated review, around 20 clients |
| Check-in instrument | His ~10 minutes and your review minutes | A start runs with every field filled most weeks | The Program's check-in |
| Capture Standard | Every retake and the condition that failed | Clients match baseline conditions without a retake | The conditions behind every comparison you publish |
| Process testimonials | Clients whose records show progress | Consented, asked at a measured peak after the exit decision | The count toward the proof milestone |
| The Dated Record's log | Every conversation and client | An entry every month since month 1 | The part of the moat nobody can back-fill |

The stall taxonomy files each founding client's stall under one of the three Stall Verdicts, the honest reasons a stall happens: unmeasured, misdirected, or a lever that doesn't move for him. Each entry holds the pattern's signs in the log, the first move that worked, and how often it has shown up. It stays yours. The paid self-serve tools you build after about 20 graduates carry logs and capture instructions, never the taxonomy's decision rules, because a rule applied without someone reading his record gives a confident wrong verdict.

The check-in and the Capture Standard, matched distance, lens, angle, light, expression, and time for every photo, get versioned the same way. Cut or rewrite a field that goes blank week after week, and keep any rating that dipped before a missed week, since it's your earliest warning of a slide. When a capture needs a retake, write down the condition that failed, whether arm's length, overhead light, or a clenched jaw, and rewrite that step. The version a later client receives has then already met the common mistakes.

### Process testimonials wait for the record

Process testimonials, a client's account of the work itself, are your first proof, and their timing is fixed. Never ask before his fit window, the full-refund period of 14–21 days, has closed. Never ask in the week-6 conversation where his exit right, the pro-rata refund for a client who did the work and wants to stop, is open, because a man deciding whether to leave can't give a free testimonial. The first ask comes at the first measured peak after his exit decision is settled, from about week 7, and only if his record shows progress.

Each one carries separate, revocable written consent and no incentive. It stays about the process: what the weekly review was like, and what he knows now that he didn't. Ten graduates with one is the first of the proof milestone's two conditions, the gate into your proof price band, so the founding group starts the count that later lets your price rise.

### The Dated Record starts with the first client

The Dated Record, your published pre-commitment plus a dated log no one can back-fill, opens in month 1 (Module 3). Its first entries are founding facts: fit declines from the first conversation, check-in completion and turnaround kept from the first client, and the claim rate from the first refund window. Start it at month 6 and the founding months are gone for good. Those are the months a skeptic checks first, because they show what you did while nobody was watching.

## 6. The Month-3 Gate

At week 12 you read two numbers in a fixed order, held conversations and then clients per conversation, because a thin first quarter has two causes that need opposite fixes.

### Volume is read first

Conversion can't be read on a handful of conversations, and a reach problem can pass for a conversion problem when the few you held came from your warmest sources. So volume goes first. Fewer than about 15 held conversations by week 12 means your sources didn't produce, whatever your close rate looks like. The fix is sources: run the week-3 shift harder and add short-form batches and replies. In Band A, where the channel never breaks out, add the default paid test at months 3–4.

That test spends about $300–1,000 a month pushing pieces that already brought in eligible adults, once your pages meet the claims rules. It's judged against a holdout and your maximum affordable cost per eligible lead, the most an eligible lead is worth paying for (Module 27). It needs proven pieces, so if nothing has produced leads on its own yet, the pieces come first.

### Then conversion

Once you've held 25 or more, conversion can be read. Fewer than 3 clients from 25 or more means the conversations happen and the yeses don't, so the offer or the position needs fixing before you build anything further: no paid reach, no new platform, no new rung. Your research answers tell you which. When most no's came from men who wanted a promise about bone or a face rating, the position is drawing the wrong buyers. When good-fit adults said no to the container, its price, or the minutes it asks, the offer doesn't fit the buyers you're drawing.

Read in that order, the two legs make the **Month-3 Gate**. You set it before the quarter starts, so a slow month can't talk you into the wrong fix:

**Under about 15 held by week 12, fix sources; under 3 clients from 25 or more, fix the offer or position.**

### When to keep going

| By week 12 | The read | The move |
|---|---|---|
| Fewer than ~15 held | The volume leg fires: reach | Fix sources; the Band A paid test once pages pass |
| 15 or more held, 3+ clients | Pass | Keep going as planned |
| 15–24 held, fewer than 3 clients | Too few to read conversion | Keep going; check that every call ended in an ask; read again at 25 |
| 25+ held, fewer than 3 clients | The conversion leg fires | Fix the offer or position before building further |

Keep going means the plan stays as written: the founding price steps on its date, Round Two and the Hold open at the first graduation, and nothing else changes on fewer than about 30 events, because at founding volume a ratio swings by chance. Even a fired conversion leg can be bad luck at the bottom of the 15–35% an offer without proof should hold, which is why the objection log decides what you fix. The gate also can't place you in a band. It reads sources you worked by hand, and reach decides your band from month 6.

**When the signals disagree.** Reid *(composite operator)* reaches week 12 with [31] held conversations and [1] client. His notes are full of praise, [six] men said they'd join "next month", and his weekly count has sat inside the 3–6 target since week 5. Every warm signal says the conversations work, and his instinct says more of them, or a lower price, will turn them into clients. The conversion leg has fired, and praise is the one signal that costs a buyer nothing.

So he checks his own calls first. [Twelve] of the 31 heard one recommendation, a price, and an ask, and the rest ran long on research and ended at "let me know". The first fix is the ask, so every call from here ends at a yes, a no, or a Decision Date. Then his objection log sorts the no's: [nine] wanted a promise about bone, traced to hooks promising a new jawline, which is a position fix. He leaves price, paid reach, and a second platform alone, and reads the leg again after the next 25.

## Scripts: The Warm-Network Message, a Permission-First Reply, and the Dual-Purpose Outline

**The warm-network message.** Sent once, personally, to each adult you know; never to anyone under 18, never to a group chat.

> **You:** "Hi [name], this is a business message, so feel free to skip it. I've started [Brand], a 12-week coaching program for adults working on how they come across who can't tell what's working. I review each man's log in writing every week and I'm straight about what can't change for an adult, so it isn't for anyone who wants a promise about bone. My first [4] clients pay a founding $[1,350]; from [April] it's $[1,500]. If you know an adult at this on his own, you could pass on this page: [link]. No need to reply, and I won't follow up."

The first line discloses, and the ask is a forward, because this buyer rarely tells friends what he's working on. If someone asks "Are you saying I need this?", answer: "Not at all. It went to everyone I know, and I'd never guess about anyone's face." Never write "saw your photos and thought of you", which aims the message at his face. A no, or silence, ends it, and a friend who wants in walks the door at the same price as anyone.

**A Permission-First Reply.** A commenter who has mentioned his warehouse shifts asks whether it's too late at 24.

> **You:** "Straight answer: there's no good evidence that habits change an adult's bone, and I don't sell that. Some things are debated. Your habits, body composition, posture, grooming, and how you're photographed can change and be measured. Which are you working on?"
>
> **Dan** *(composite, Struggler)*: "Posture and photos. Eight months in and I can't tell if any of it works."
>
> **You:** "That's usually a measurement problem more than an effort one. Mind if I DM you a free self-assessment that sorts out where you are? No is a fine answer."

The public answer serves every reader, and the permission question moves one adult toward the door with no pitch in public. A yes gets the door link and nothing else, since the door checks age and fit first. Never offer "send me a side profile and I'll rate it". A no ends it, and any sign he's under 18 means no DM at all.

**The dual-purpose outline.** About 45 minutes, after a booking page that showed the price range and asked the affordability question.

> **You:** "Here's how this goes. About 45 minutes. First I'm learning: [Brand] is new, I'm building it with its first clients, and I'll ask how you've gone about this. Your answers shape the program and never appear anywhere with your name. Then we'll see whether it fits, and I'll recommend one thing: the founding group, the free Starter Path, or not buying. At the end I'll ask for a yes or a no. Fair?"

The first third is research on what he tried, bought, and measured, with no hypotheticals. Then the stake and the recommendation:

> **You:** "What happens if the next eight months look like the last eight?"
>
> **Dan:** "I keep at it and still don't know if any of it does anything."
>
> **You:** "Then I'd recommend the founding group from [February 3]: your log reviewed in writing within [48] hours every week, one live call, a week-6 read, and a week-12 re-assessment. It's $[1,350] for 12 weeks. Do you want to start on the [3rd]?"
> *[Stop. Let him answer.]*

The research doubled as the excavation, so the stake is in his words, and the price came once, after the deliverables: Close by Contract with nothing hidden in its first half. A yes books day one before you hang up. If he says "Can I think about it?", probe once ("What would you be weighing?"), answer it, and set a Decision Date before the [February] start's last day to join. Never open with "this is just research, no sales" when the call ends in a recommendation. At a clear no, ask once what made it a no, write it down, and stop; "I can't afford it" gets the Starter Path once and the pause route.

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** This module is the plan. Reach binds, so labeled sources, the Dual-Purpose Conversation, and the founding group are the default until the Month-3 Gate passes. The trap is a quieter way of waiting for an audience: posting daily while the warm-network message sits unsent and door completions wait a day for a reply.

**Growing.** Conversion and selling hours bind, and the founding method becomes how you open each new offer. Round Two or the Hold gets a small founding cohort of its own: disclosed as first, priced near its real price with the next one stated, and harvested for its own instrument. The trap is a "founding" price on the core Program after it has sold at full price, which is a discount with a story attached.

**Scaling.** Care minutes bind, and a waiting list leaves a founding price nothing to do. A new tier such as Program Async runs as a pilot through the standing door: capped seats at its real price, a review date set in advance, and a written decision to keep, change, or drop it. The trap is a pilot that turns into a launch, with a waitlist page, a countdown, and a push to fill it.

## Standard Check

- **Disclosed in the first line.** The warm-network message says it's a business message, and the Dual-Purpose Conversation puts its research job, the price range, and the affordability question on the booking page (*Sell directly*). A research call that ends in a pitch would fail the Informed-Client Test's ceiling.
- **Founding scarcity with its math shown.** Seats are a stated number from founding Seat Math, and the founding price ends on a dated step announced 30+ days out (*Use real dates*). Neither leans on urgency, which keeps both clear of the line on fake scarcity.
- **Stop rules apply to friends.** A friend's "I can't afford it" ends money talk, he gets no discount and no second message, and nobody under 18 is contacted (the line on vulnerability).
- **Testimonials after the exit decision.** The first process-testimonial ask waits for a measured peak after the week-6 exit decision, so no ask leans on his refund rights (*Name the destination boldly*, at its default).
