# 9 · The Founding Phase
*Part III — Start, Extend, Run, Grow*

**The shift:** from *"I need an audience before I can sell"* to *"I sell through disclosed conversations now, my founding clients are R&D, and every one of them joins the founding group."*

It's the first Monday. You have a camera on a tripod and a channel with no subscribers. The Honest Answer, your straight public answer to the bone question, is filmed and waiting for its edit. Door v0, the first working version of your door, went live at [1 am], and nobody has walked through it yet.

The tempting plan is to post for six months and sell once there's an audience. In this category that plan spends the months that decide your first quarter waiting for people who mostly won't buy. Your first clients will come from conversations you start by hand: a disclosed message, a reply under a piece, a call whose purpose and price are on the page.

So the honest questions for day one are where your first five conversations come from, and what you'll change in week 3 if they haven't come. This module answers both. Then it turns your first clients into the business's first instruments: a map of why men stall, a check-in tested on real weeks, and its first proof.

## 1. Why You Sell Before You Have an Audience

You don't need an audience to sell; you need conversations, and for your first three months they come from sources you work by hand.

### Month 3 looks the same in every band

Reach Bands, the four planning bands for how many eligible leads a month your channel reaches by month 9, barely differ at month 3. Across all four, month 3 plans on roughly 3–18 held conversations and at most about 6 new clients. The bands separate only from month 6, once reach starts to compound. So month 3 is decided by conversations, the one number you can move before reach does. Posting for six months while you wait spends the quarter on a number that isn't moving yet.

**You can't choose your band by month 3, but you can choose your conversations.**

### Early conversations teach what a dashboard can't

A view arrives with no age, no budget, and no history. A held conversation arrives with all three. It tells you why a man eight months into [his current routine] still can't tell whether it works, what he has bought, and what he measures. It also tells you which destination he's after: to be taken seriously, to be at ease in photos, or simply to know. Those answers become your first check-in, your first review template, and the words your pages use.

They also show you where your close breaks while a break costs one buyer. The founding call has two traps this category sets. The bone question arrives mid-call and pulls you into an argument, or the research runs so long that the price arrives at the very end. Heard back in your notes in week 4, either flaw costs you one buyer, and left until month 9 it costs a month.

### Founding revenue pays for the editor

The Early week runs about 20 hours, and roughly 8.5 of them are the Protected Content Minimum, content hours client work can never eat. A long-form piece costs about 4–6 of your hours with an editor, and a good deal more without one. Long-form is also where a skeptical buyer checks you before he books, so a thin month there costs you exactly the buyers who check.

The first edits, the Honest Answer's included, come out of the reserve you hold before month 1, about 2–3 months of costs. Founding cash refills it, which is why founding clients pay the business's costs before they pay you, and why you keep your other income through these months. A founding month that covers the editor is a month that worked.

## 2. The Founding Sprint: Sources and Speed

Your first conversations come from routes you can name, and every held conversation carries one label: the route that produced its booking.

### Two facts about this buyer decide how each route behaves

He hides the pursuit. Asking for help with how he looks feels like admitting something, so he watches without commenting and rarely tells a friend. And the youngest viewers engage first: in self-selected samples most of the category's hardcore audience is under 18. Together they explain why routes differ. Friends are seldom the buyer but can forward to him, replies under your pieces arrive fast and skew young, and short pieces built on adult situations, like the 27-year-old who has guessed for a year, reach the man who won't comment (Module 24).

### Every held conversation carries one label

The label is the route that produced the booking. You read it from the tagged link he booked through, or from one booking-form question, "Where did you find me?", when the link carries no tag. Every other touch goes in a notes field. A man who watched [three] shorts and then booked from a friend's forward is labeled warm network, with "shorts ×3" in his notes, so the count stays at one label per conversation.

| Label | The route that produced the booking | What it costs | What to plan on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm network | Your disclosed message to an adult you know, or his forward of it | Goodwill, so it's used lightly | With replies, ~2–8 held a month in months 1–3, front-loaded |
| Replies | Your reply to him under your own pieces | Reply minutes | Counted with the warm network; fades as your first pieces age |
| Permission-First Replies | Your public answer on someone else's thread, then a DM he said yes to | Part of ~2 h/week of replies | Renews, because new threads appear every day |
| Short-form | Your profile link, after a native short | ~3.5 h/week for 4–7 pieces | ~0.1–1 lead per 1,000 views; its job is reach |
| Long-form and search | A long video's link, or a search for your name | ~2.5 h/week | Slowest early; the route skeptics check you through |

Two parts of the Sprint carry no label, because every booking passes through both. The founding page is where a warm reader sees the offers, and its one next step is the door, your owned path from first click to one recommendation, where every short's profile link also sends him. Speed to lead, a personal reply within hours, then a booking within 24–48 hours, a reminder, and a held conversation, turns 10–20% of eligible leads into held conversations while it runs (Module 5).

Five labels, one per held conversation, two shared parts, a target of 3–6 held conversations a week, and a check in week 3 that moves hours between labels: that's the **Founding Sprint**, first conversations without an audience. Its rule fits in a line:

**One label per held conversation, counted weekly, with hours moved in week 3.**

### The warm network is used lightly

Message each adult you know once, personally, with a first line saying it's a business message, and don't follow up unless he replies. The message asks for a forward, since friends are seldom the buyer. Nobody under 18 gets it, however well you know his family.

> **You:** "Hi [name], this is a business message, so feel free to skip it. I've started [Brand], a 12-week coaching program for adults working on how they come across who can't tell what's working. I review each client's log in writing every week, and I'm straight about what can't change for an adult, so it isn't for anyone who wants a promise about bone. My first [4] clients pay a founding price of $[1,500]; from the [March] start it's $[1,650]. If you know an adult at this on his own, you could pass on this page: [link]. No need to reply, and I won't follow up."

It names the offer, who it's for and who it isn't, the price, and the next price with its start, then asks only for a forward. If someone replies "Are you saying I need this?", answer: "Not at all. It went to everyone I know over 18, and I'd never guess about anyone's face." Never write "saw your photos and thought of you", which aims the message at his face. A no, or silence, ends it, and a friend who wants in walks the door at the same price as anyone.

### Replies route to the door

Replies under your pieces and Permission-First Replies, answering the idea in public and asking before anything private, carry attention to the door with no pitch in public (Module 25). Anyone whose age you can't tell gets the public answer and nothing private. Anyone who says he's under 18 gets the education lane, your public content plus an orthodontist referral. Here a commenter who has mentioned his warehouse shifts asks whether it's too late at 24:

> **You:** "Straight answer: there's no good evidence that habits change an adult's bone, and I don't sell that. Some things are debated. Your habits, body composition, posture, grooming, and how you're photographed can change and be measured. Which are you working on?"
>
> **Dan** *(composite, Struggler)*: "Posture and photos. Eight months in and I can't tell if any of it works."
>
> **You:** "That's usually a measurement problem more than an effort one. Mind if I DM you a free self-assessment that sorts out where you are? No is a fine answer."

The public answer serves every reader, and the permission question moves one adult toward the door. A yes gets the door link and nothing else, since the door checks age and fit first. If he pushes for the answer right there, keep it public: "The general version is in [the Honest Answer]. Yours depends on your record." Never offer "send me a side profile and I'll rate it". A no ends it.

### Three numbers frame the weekly count

The count sits beside three numbers that do different jobs. The target, 3–6 held conversations a week, is what you aim for. Your band's month-3 waypoint, roughly 3–18 a month, is what you plan on. The week-12 floor, about 15 held in the first 12 weeks, is the line you can't stay under without changing sources.

The Early week's 4.5 conversation hours hold 4–5 calls at about an hour each, all-in, so a sixth comes out of the week's slack. Plan on 0–2 clients in month 1 and 2–6 by month 3, depending on your labels, and know that first cash can slip past month 1 if every label runs slow. A plan that says so in advance is one you won't abandon in week 5.

### The week-3 source check moves hours

The week-3 source check reads two labels at the end of week 3: warm network and replies. If together they give fewer than 2 held conversations a week, shift about 2 hours a week from long-form to short-form batches and Permission-First Replies. Week 3 is the moment because both labels front-load: your network has had its one message, and replies fade as your first pieces age. Permission-First Replies renew and short-form builds reach, while long-form pays off over months a new channel doesn't have yet.

Keep the Honest Answer up, since every other piece points to it. Leave price, paid reach, and new platforms alone, because none of them adds a conversation this month. Hold the shift until the weekly count reaches the 3–6 target, then give long-form its hours back.

## 3. The Dual-Purpose Conversation

In your founding months every conversation does two jobs, learning and deciding, and he's told both before he books.

### Both jobs go on the booking page

Men in this category have met the free strategy call that turns into a pitch halfway through. Some have met its mirror image, a "research interview" that ends in a checkout link. Either confirms what a burned buyer already believes about the category, so the founding call states both jobs before he picks a slot. You're building the program with its first clients and will ask how he has gone about this; then you'll see whether it fits him. It ends in one recommendation: the founding group, a founding Private seat, the Starter Path, your free sequenced path for anyone who shouldn't buy now, "don't buy", or a referral.

The same page shows your public price range and asks the affordability question: "Is this comfortable from your own income or savings, without new credit or buy-now-pay-later?" A no books nothing. He gets the free Starter Path, and the pause route is set, the tag that stops sales sends for 60–90 days.

That's the **Dual-Purpose Conversation**: a disclosed fit-and-research call of about 45 minutes, with its purpose, the price, and one recommendation stated before he books. With prep and the written recap it costs about an hour all-in.

### Research asks what he did

The research half asks about his past, never his predictions. What a man says he'd pay, or plans to do next month, is a loose guide to what he'll do. People name higher prices for hypothetical offers than they pay for real ones, and intentions turn into action less often than people expect. So ask what he tried and for how long, what he bought, what he measured, and when he stopped. Praise for your idea costs him nothing, while a booked date and a payment cost him something, so those are the signals.

What he bought belongs in your notes as history. It never appears in the recommendation or the price, because a price argued from money he has already lost is the recovery pitch grifters build on sunk spend. Tell him how his answers are used: to build the program, stripped of his name, never quoted without his consent, and never in a marketing tool. Ask before you record the call, since replaying it later is how you find where your close broke.

What's out is anything that turns the research into a warm-up for the pitch: questions built to make him want the program ("wouldn't it help to have someone check your week?"), hypotheticals about your prices, and a research call that ends with a link. Each fails the Informed-Client Test, whether he'd still say it served him after seeing exactly what you did (Intro). Questions about photos carry one more rule. If his answers describe distress, or checking that crowds out his day, the sale stops and the call becomes a referral conversation.

### It ends in one recommendation and an ask

The fit half runs the close you'd run on any call: say his stall and goal back, recommend one thing, state the price once, ask, and resolve each real objection. Close by Contract, the close that agrees its structure first, carries it (Module 19). It ends at a yes with day one booked, a no, or his Decision Date, the day he agrees to decide by, before the next start's last day to join. A good-fit adult who helps you learn for 45 minutes and leaves without a recommendation and a price has been failed.

The disclosure buys one extra question. After a clear no, you may ask once what made it a no, because you said the call was also research, and you write the answer down without arguing with it. After distress or a fit-check pause there's no such question, only the referral or the pause. If the answer is "I can't afford it", money talk ends: the Starter Path once, and the pause route.

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| One 45-minute call, research then fit | Reach binds and your calls fit the Early week's 4.5-hour line | About an hour all-in per conversation |
| Research prompts in the booking form, then a 20–30-minute fit call | Calls pass 4–5 a week, beyond that line | Thinner research, written instead of talked through |
| A fit call only | New conversations stop teaching you new stall patterns | You stop hearing what's changing, so keep one research question on the form |

**Default:** one 45-minute call while your calls fit the 4.5-hour line, because the hours are spare and every call is also research at no extra cost. Move the research prompts into the booking form once calls pass 4–5 a week, since the extra minutes then come out of content.

## 4. The Founding Group and Founding Private Seats

Every founding client joins one group from the very first, at a price near the real one with a stated end. One or two founding Private seats, premium seats with fixed deliverables, give the Optimizer a real tier from day one.

### Every client joins one group from the first

The founding group is every founding client in one standing group from the first, with monthly entry, a new start every month. No one-to-one product comes before it. That product would have to be retired later, and its price history would show one-to-one selling below the group, which no later buyer could square with a premium tier. It also wouldn't test what the business runs on: the standing group, the weekly call, and the monthly start.

While the group has fewer than about four members, review runs at one-to-one depth, about 30–35 care minutes per client-week all-in. That depth is where you learn this category's quiet signals: a comparison habit creeping back into his notes, a capture shot under a new light, a blocker that repeats week after week. Reach binds and minutes are spare, so you can afford it. Past about four members, the review itself settles toward the 12–20 minutes of the Program, your 12-week flagship, and the weekly call carries more, as the page says from day one.

The weekly call runs at its fixed time from week 1, even with one member, under handles and with cameras optional. Your first client never waits for your fifth, and the second joins a group that already exists.

### The founding price sits near the real price and ends as stated

The founding price is about $1.2–1.5k for 12 weeks, with the next price printed beside it from day one. It sits just under the opening band of $1.5–2.2k. It's close because founding clients get the real review, and lower because they also get a program still being built. Warm sources and process proof you can already show, such as a published pre-commitment, push it toward the top of the range, and cold sources push it toward the bottom.

**A founding price has a stated end, a seat count or a start date, and it's never extended.**

It ends at whichever comes first: its seats filling, or the dated start where the first scheduled Price Step lands, announced at least 30 days out, low in the opening band, and naming what was added (Module 7). The founding group builds that answer: a tested check-in, a written Capture Standard, the matched distance, light, and angle for every photo, and a sample weekly review drawn from real ones. The seat count comes from founding Seat Math, the review minutes your Early week protects divided by one founding client-week. Extending the price to rescue a slow start would turn a real end into a deadline that moved, which crosses the line on fake scarcity.

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Founding price near the real one, with a stated end | From the first client | A small gap under the opening band, closed by one scheduled step |
| The opening band from the first client | Your sources are warm and your process proof is already public | Fewer yeses in the months when each one also builds the instrument |
| Free or deep-discount "beta" seats | Never: free transformations traded for before-and-afters are how this category's fake proof gets made | Paid proof no buyer can see, and a price nobody paid |

**Default:** the founding price, because it pays for real review before any proof exists. It's also the lowest price the program will ever carry, so no founding client later watches someone pay less for the same seat. Move to the opening band at the founding price's end, whichever comes first.

### Founding Private seats are the premium tier from day one

The Optimizer, the buyer with more money than time, can arrive in your first week: 25–35, well paid, and wary of being seen in a program. One or two founding Private seats at about $4–6k for 12 weeks give him a real tier while your minutes are spare. They sell on speed, privacy, and fixed deliverables, never on better results, since in adjacent fields' trials individual and group formats perform about the same at equal intensity. Every Private page says "we never read or score your face", because precision here means deliverables.

Build the seat async-first, because the Early week gives Private about half an hour a week. Weekly calls would cost about 14–15 hours over 12 weeks. An async-first seat, with [a recorded review at weeks 0, 4, 8, and 12], [written replies within 24 hours on weekdays], and [a 20-minute call at baseline, week 6, and week 12], costs about 7–8, roughly 0.6 hours a week. One such seat fills the line, so sell a second only when Seat Math shows another 0.6 hours a week that nothing else needs.

His way in is the priority tier, the faster paid assessment at about $350–600, credited to the seat, and Private always needs a call. The seat is there for the buyer it fits, who would otherwise wait a week for a free slot he'd have paid to skip. The seats open the Premium Lane, the Optimizer's path from the priority tier to Private (Module 13).

### Founding graduates are offered Round Two

Founding graduates reach week 12 from about month 4, and each gets the offer his record supports. One with measured momentum, adherence at or above his threshold and a marker still improving across its last two readings, is offered Round Two, the maintenance block at about $0.8–1.2k. One who moved and holds is told he doesn't need it, and hears about the Hold, the measurement subscription. One paid under the non-response clause, the cash refund when no marker moved despite his effort, hears no offer.

His Path and Timeline Card, the one-page path and cost he saw before paying, already priced both, so they need prices before your first founding checkout. Your founding graduates are Round Two's first cohort, and the block is designed with the rest of the back end (Module 10).

## 5. The R&D Harvest

Founding clients buy a program, and the business gets its instruments, so every founding record has to leave something behind.

### The instruments are built on real weeks

Few check-ins, photo standards, or review templates survive their first real client unchanged. A check-in designed at a desk meets a man whose shifts rotate, and the field he skips is the one you thought mattered. Photo conditions copied from a guide meet his bathroom light and a phone held at arm's length. A list of why men stall, written from your own months, meets stalls you never had. One-to-one review finds all of it fast, which is why the founding group's depth is research as much as service.

The R&D Harvest is what founding clients must produce, each part frozen at version 1 once it survives the founding group. Say so on the founding page, with his consent for his record, stripped of his name, to shape it. The last row belongs to the Dated Record, your published pre-commitment plus a dated log no one can back-fill (Module 3):

| Harvest item | Built from | Version 1 is done when | It becomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stall taxonomy | Founding records, plus research answers from every held conversation | Each founding client's stall is named, with its signs and first move | The backbone of templated review, around 20 clients |
| Check-in instrument | His ~10 minutes and your review minutes | A start runs with every field filled most weeks | The Program's check-in |
| Capture Standard | Every retake and the condition that failed | Clients match baseline conditions without a retake | The conditions behind every comparison you publish |
| Review template | Your best founding reviews | One real week, anonymized, stands as your sample weekly review | The sample now; templated review at about 20 clients |
| Process testimonials | Clients whose records show progress | Consented, asked at a measured peak after the exit decision | The count toward the proof milestone |
| The Dated Record's log | Every conversation and client | An entry every month since month 1 | The part of the moat nobody can back-fill |

Each taxonomy entry is a few lines, with the method in your own files:

```
Pattern: [name, in plain words] · Verdict: [unmeasured | misdirected | lever not moving]
Signs in the log: [sign] · [sign] · First move: [one adjustment]
Seen in: [n] founding records · [n] research answers · Version 1, [date]
```

The verdict line uses the three Stall Verdicts, the honest reasons a stall happens. Research answers from men who didn't buy count too, stripped of names, since a stall doesn't need a purchase to be real. The taxonomy stays yours: the paid self-serve tools you build after about 20 graduates carry logs and capture instructions, never its decision rules. A rule applied without someone reading his record gives a confident wrong verdict.

Version the check-in and the Capture Standard from what the founding weeks show. Cut or rewrite a field that goes blank week after week, and keep any rating that dipped before a missed week, since it's your earliest warning of a slide. When a capture needs a retake, note the condition that failed, whether arm's length, overhead light, or a clenched jaw, and rewrite that step. When a version survives a full start, freeze it, and name it in the founding step as what was added.

### Process testimonials wait for the record

Process testimonials, a client's account of the work itself, are your first proof, and their timing is fixed (Module 22). Never ask before his fit window, the full-refund period of 14–21 days, has closed. Never ask in the week-6 conversation where his exit right, the pro-rata refund for a client who did the work and wants to stop, is open, because a man deciding whether to leave can't give a free testimonial. The first ask comes at the first measured peak after his exit decision is settled, from about week 7, and only if his record shows progress.

Each one carries separate, revocable written consent and no incentive, and it stays about the process: what the weekly review was like, and what he knows now that he didn't. Ten graduates with one is the first of the proof milestone's two conditions, the gate into your proof price band. So the founding group starts the count that later lets your price rise.

### The Dated Record starts with the first client

Its first entries are founding facts: fit declines from the first conversation, check-in completion and turnaround kept from the first client, and the claim rate from the first refund window. Start the log at month 6 and the founding months are gone for good. They're the months a skeptic checks first, because they show what you did while nobody was watching.

## 6. The Month-3 Gate

At week 12 you read two numbers in a fixed order, held conversations and then clients from them, because a thin first quarter has two causes that need opposite fixes.

### The gate reads your work, not your band

Month 3 can't tell you which band you're in, since month 3 is decided by conversations and the bands separate only from month 6. What it can tell you is whether the conversations happened and whether they turned into clients. Each answer has its own fix, so the gate asks those two questions, in that order, about the quarter you just worked.

### Volume is read first

A thin count can pass for a conversion problem, because the few conversations you held came from adults near you, and warm conversations close at rates cold ones won't repeat. So volume goes first. Fewer than about 15 held conversations by week 12 means reach is the problem, whatever your close rate looks like, and it takes two fixes together.

The first is sources: run the week-3 shift again, and give Permission-First Replies and short-form the hours your week can spare up to 25. The second is the default paid test at months 3–4, once your pages meet the claims rules: about $300–1,000 a month pushing pieces that already brought in eligible adults, judged against a holdout and the most an eligible lead is worth paying for (Module 27). It needs proven pieces, so if nothing has produced an eligible lead on its own yet, the pieces come first.

### Conversion is read at 25 conversations

Fewer than 3 clients from 25 or more held conversations means the conversations happen and the yeses don't, so the offer or the position needs fixing before you build anything further. Work the fix in order. First, replay the calls you recorded with consent against Close by Contract and your objection log, the running list of every no in his words. Did each call reach a summary, one recommendation, the price once, and an ask? Then check where the no's point: whether your position was clear before the call, whether the Path and Timeline Card surprised him (Module 6), and whether the price sits well under the cash ceiling, about 1–1.25 months of the core buyer's take-home.

| What the replays and the log show | It points at | The one change |
|---|---|---|
| Calls with no summary, no recommendation, or no ask | The conversation | Every call ends at a yes, a no, or a Decision Date |
| No's from men who wanted a promise about bone, or a rating | Position | The Honest Answer and the Outcome Map go on the booking page, before the call |
| Surprise at the months, the check-ins, or the total cost | The Path and Timeline Card | The Card goes out with the booking confirmation |
| Good-fit adults who call the price too high | Value, rarely the price, which sits well under the ceiling | Hold the price; show the delivery and your own installments at cost |
| "I can't afford it" | A stop rule, never an objection | The Starter Path once and the pause route; nothing to fix |

Change one thing at a time, and let what buyers said lead. No rate is readable at month 3, because a ratio needs about 30 events and two windows before it can move you (Module 12). So a fix is judged on the next [ten] calls' replays, never on their percentage.

Read in that order, the two legs make the **Month-3 Gate**. You set it before the quarter starts, so a slow month can't talk you into the wrong fix:

**Under about 15 held by week 12, fix sources; under 3 clients from 25 or more, fix the offer or position, one change at a time.**

### The founding price ends as stated in every row

| By week 12 | The read | The move | The founding price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fewer than ~15 held | Volume: reach | The source shift and the paid test, together | Ends as stated |
| 15–24 held, fewer than 3 clients | Too few to read conversion | Replay the calls; read again at 25 | Ends as stated |
| 25+ held, fewer than 3 clients | Conversion | The fix order, one change at a time | Ends as stated |
| 15+ held, 3+ clients | Pass | Keep going as planned | Ends as stated |

Keep going means the plan stays as written: Round Two and the Hold open at the first graduation, and nothing else changes on fewer than about 30 events. One short can fill a week with rating requests and swing every ratio you have. That's why the gate reads the quarter and the replays, never a hot week.

**When the signals disagree.** Reid *(composite operator)* reaches week 12 with [31] held conversations and [1] client. He chased views with a [broad self-improvement] series and skipped the labels, so he can't see that the series booked most of his calls. His notes are full of praise, [six] men said they'd join "next month", and his count has sat inside the 3–6 target since week 5. His instinct says more calls, or a lower founding price, will turn them into clients. The conversion leg has fired, though, and praise is the one signal that costs a buyer nothing.

So he works the fix order. Replaying the [22] calls he recorded with consent, he finds [19] ended without a recommendation and an ask, so the first change is the ask. The [six] "next month" men each get one message naming the [March] start's last day to join, and no second. His log holds the next change: most no's came from men who liked the broad series and wanted free tips, a position fix he'll make once [ten] calls with a real ask have been read. The founding price ends on its date, and he buys no paid reach.

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** This module is the plan. Reach binds, so labeled sources, the Dual-Purpose Conversation, and the founding group are the default until the Month-3 Gate passes, and founding graduates become Round Two's first cohort. The trap is a quieter way of waiting for an audience: posting daily while the warm-network message sits unsent and door completions wait a day for a reply.

**Growing.** Conversion and selling hours bind, and the founding method opens each new offer the same way. First buyers hear they're first, the price is real or a founding price with a stated end, and their weeks become the offer's instrument. Round Two was the first, founded on your founding graduates, and the paid overflow assessment usually comes next. The trap is a "founding" price on the core Program after it has sold at full price, a discount with a story attached.

**Scaling.** Care minutes bind, and a waiting list leaves a founding price nothing to do. A new tier such as Program Async, the same review without the live call, runs as a pilot through the standing door, with capped seats at its real price and a review date set in advance. The trap is a pilot that turns into a launch, with a waitlist page, a countdown, and a push to fill it.

## Standard Check

- **Disclosed in the first line.** The warm-network message says it's a business message, and the Dual-Purpose Conversation puts its research job, the price range, and the affordability question on the booking page (*Sell directly*). A research call that ends in a pitch would fail the Informed-Client Test's ceiling.
- **Founding scarcity with its math shown.** Seats are a stated number from founding Seat Math, and the founding price ends at its seat count or its date, never extended (*Use real dates*). Neither leans on urgency, which keeps both clear of the line on fake scarcity.
- **Stop rules apply to friends.** A friend's "I can't afford it" ends money talk, he gets no discount and no second message, and nobody under 18 is contacted (the line on vulnerability).
- **Research that never turns into a pitch.** What he bought stays in the notes, never in the recommendation or the price, and the first testimonial ask waits for a measured peak after his exit decision (the line on vulnerability; *Name the destination boldly*).
