# 9 · The Founding Phase
*Part III — Start, Extend, Run, Grow*

**The shift:** from *"I need an audience before I can sell"* to *"I sell through disclosed conversations now, my founding clients are R&D, and every one of them joins the founding group."*

It's the first Monday. You have a camera on a tripod and a channel with no subscribers. The Honest Answer, your straight public answer to the bone question, is filmed and waiting for its edit. Door v0, the first working version of your door, went live at [1 am], and nobody has walked through it yet.

The tempting plan is to post for six months and sell once there's an audience. In this category, that plan waits on the wrong people. The first viewers to engage skew young. The men most likely to buy early rarely comment under their own names or tell friends what they're working on. What they will do is answer a message that says plainly what it is, or book a call whose purpose and price are on the page.

So the honest questions for day one are where your first five conversations come from, and what you'll change in week 3 if they haven't come. This module answers both. Then it turns your first clients into the business's first instruments: a map of why men stall, a check-in tested on real weeks, and its first proof.

## 1. Why You Sell Before You Have an Audience

You don't need an audience to sell; you need conversations, and for your first three months they come from sources you work by hand.

### Month 3 looks the same in every band

Reach Bands, the four planning bands for how many eligible leads a month your channel reaches by month 9, barely differ at month 3. Across all four, month 3 plans on roughly 3–18 held conversations and at most about 6 new clients. The bands separate only from month 6, once reach starts to compound. So month 3 is decided by conversations, the one number you can move before reach does. Posting for six months while you wait spends the quarter on a number that isn't moving yet.

**You can't choose your band by month 3, but you can choose your conversations.**

An early audience here also overstates your buyers. The youngest viewers engage first. In self-selected samples most of the category's hardcore audience is under 18, so a big video can send your door mostly fork exits. Meanwhile the adult you want watches without commenting and rarely tells anyone he's working on how he looks, because asking for help with it feels like admitting something. He has to be reached quietly, one conversation at a time.

### Early conversations teach what a dashboard can't

Your analytics say what got watched. A held conversation says why a man eight months into [his current routine] still can't tell whether it works. It tells you what he bought, what he measures, and which destination he's after: to be taken seriously, to be at ease in photos, or simply to know. Those answers become your first check-in, your first review template, and the language of your pages. No dashboard can tell you what stopped the last man who didn't buy.

Early conversations also show you where your close breaks while the stakes are small. A call that loses the thread at the price, or never reaches a recommendation, costs you one buyer in week 4. The same flaw running at month 9 volume costs you a month. At founding volume you can hear every call back in your notes and fix the flaw by name.

### Founding revenue pays for the editor

The Early week runs about 20 hours, and roughly 8.5 of them are the Protected Content Minimum, content hours client work can never eat. A long-form piece costs about 4–6 of your hours with an editor, and a good deal more without one. Long-form is also where a burned buyer checks you before he books, so thinning it costs you exactly the buyers who check.

So the first founding cash buys the editing. Founding clients pay the business's costs before they pay you, which is why you keep your other income through these months. A founding month that covers the editor is a month that worked.

## 2. The Founding Sprint: Sources and Speed

Your first conversations come from sources you can name and count. Naming them lets you fix a slow start in week 3, while it's still cheap to fix.

### Unlabeled effort hides the failing source

Most first quarters start as a blend: post, answer whatever comes in, message a few friends, and count calls at month's end. In this niche the blend fails quietly, because its parts fail in different directions. Early replies are real engagement, yet many come from teenagers or from men asking for a face rating. Messages to friends feel productive, yet this buyer rarely tells a friend what he's working on. When the month ends thin, a blend can't tell you which part to fix.

A held conversation needs an eligible adult, a legal adult who can pay from his own income or savings. It also needs a reason to talk this week and a disclosed way to book. Each source supplies these at its own rate, and two facts about this buyer skew every rate. He hides the pursuit, so the people who know him hear last, and the youngest viewers engage fastest, so early engagement overstates buyers. That's why every source gets a label, a disclosure, and its own line in your weekly count.

### Six sources, each labeled and disclosed

| Source | What it is, and how it's disclosed | What it costs | What to plan on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm network | Adults you know, messaged once each; the first line says it's a business message | Goodwill, so it's used lightly | With early replies, ~2–8 held a month in months 1–3, front-loaded |
| Early replies | People who reply to or comment on your first pieces; your reply routes them to the door | Reply minutes | Counted with the warm network |
| The founding page | A standing page with every offer, price, and seat count, linked from every early asset | One build, kept current | It converts what the other sources send |
| Native short-form | 4–7 pieces a week; your profile routes to the door | ~3.5 h/week | ~0.1–1 lead per 1,000 views; its job is reach |
| Permission-First Replies | Public answers to adults who engage, asking before any private message | Part of ~2 h/week of replies | Adults who already engaged, routed to the door |
| Speed to lead | A personal reply within hours to every adult who finishes the door | ~1 h/week | 10–20% of eligible leads reach a held conversation |

Six labeled sources, 3–6 held conversations a week, and a week-3 check that moves hours toward whatever produces: that's the **Founding Sprint**, first conversations without an audience. Its rule fits in a line:

**Label every source, count held conversations by source each week, and move hours in week 3.**

### The warm network is used lightly

The warm-network source, the adults you already know, is finite and front-loaded. You message each adult once, personally, and say in the first line that it's a business message. Because this buyer hides the pursuit, the message asks for a forward, and you don't follow up. With early replies, plan on about 2–8 held conversations a month in months 1–3, most of them early, since a network messaged once doesn't refill. Nobody under 18 gets it, however well you know his family.

> **You:** "Hi [name], this is a business message, so feel free to skip it. I've started [Brand], a 12-week coaching program for adults working on how they come across who can't tell what's working. I review each man's log in writing every week, and I'm straight about what can't change for an adult, so it isn't for anyone who wants a promise about bone. My first [4] clients pay a founding price of $[1,350]; from [April] it's $[1,500]. If you know an adult at this on his own, you could pass on this page: [link]. No need to reply, and I won't follow up."

The message names the offer, who it's for and who it isn't, the price, and the next price, and then asks only for a forward. If someone replies "Are you saying I need this?", answer: "Not at all. It went to everyone I know, and I'd never guess about anyone's face." Never write "saw your photos and thought of you", which aims the message at his face. A no, or silence, ends it, and a friend who wants in walks the door at the same price as anyone.

### Replies route to the door

Early replies and Permission-First Replies, answering the idea in public and asking before anything private, carry attention to the door (Module 25). The only thing you send privately is the door, where the age fork and fit questions do their work. A man whose age you can't tell gets the public answer and nothing private. Anyone who says he's under 18 gets the public answer and the education lane, your public content plus an orthodontist referral. Here a commenter who has mentioned his warehouse shifts asks whether it's too late at 24:

> **You:** "Straight answer: there's no good evidence that habits change an adult's bone, and I don't sell that. Some things are debated. Your habits, body composition, posture, grooming, and how you're photographed can change and be measured. Which are you working on?"
>
> **Dan** *(composite, Struggler)*: "Posture and photos. Eight months in and I can't tell if any of it works."
>
> **You:** "That's usually a measurement problem more than an effort one. Mind if I DM you a free self-assessment that sorts out where you are? No is a fine answer."

The public answer serves every reader, and the permission question moves one adult toward the door with no pitch in public. A yes gets the door link and nothing else, since the door checks age and fit first. If he pushes for the answer right there, keep it public: "The general version is in [the Honest Answer]. Yours depends on your record." Never offer "send me a side profile and I'll rate it", which sells the rating this category runs on. A no ends it.

### Short-form finds him, and speed books him

Native short-form is the early reach engine, built on adult situations like the 27-year-old who has guessed for a year (Module 24). Adult framing matters because rating and comparison content pulls the youngest viewers fastest, so judge each piece by the eligible leads it sends. Everything points at the founding page, which names the offers premium first, says who they're for and who they aren't, and prints every price beside the next. It doesn't change as a start approaches, because a page that heats up near a date is a launch.

Speed to lead is the early stage's quiet advantage: a personal reply within hours, a booking within 24–48 hours, a reminder, and a held conversation. A reply from the man he watched is his first evidence the brand isn't another bot. Keep it personal, and automate booking and reminders once replies pass about an hour a day (Module 5).

### Count held conversations by source

Aim for 3–6 held conversations a week, what the Early week's 4.5 conversation hours carry at about an hour each. Count five numbers weekly: eligible leads, held conversations, enrollments with cash, pieces published, and check-in completion. Then add the column the Sprint depends on: the source of every held conversation. A count without sources tells you a week was thin, and nothing about why.

Set expectations you can keep. Plan on 0–2 clients in month 1 and 2–6 by month 3, depending on your sources. First cash can slip past month 1 if every source runs slow. A plan that says so in advance is one you won't abandon in week 5.

### The week-3 source check moves hours

The week-3 source check is a fixed read of what your hand-worked sources produce by the end of week 3. If warm network plus replies give fewer than 2 held conversations a week, shift about 2 hours a week from long-form to short-form batches and Permission-First Replies. Week 3 is the moment because those sources front-load: your network has had its one message, and your first pieces have drawn most of their replies. Long-form gives up the hours because it pays off over months, while short-form and replies can reach adults this week.

Keep the Honest Answer up, since every other piece points to it. Leave price, paid reach, and new platforms alone, because none of them adds a conversation this month. Hold the shift until your weekly count reaches the 3–6 target, then give long-form its hours back.

## 3. The Dual-Purpose Conversation

In your founding months every conversation does two jobs, learning and deciding, and he's told both before he books.

### Both jobs go on the booking page

Men in this category have met the free strategy call that turns into a pitch halfway through. Some have met its mirror image, a "research interview" that ends in a checkout link. Either confirms what a burned buyer already believes about the category, so the founding call states both jobs before he picks a slot. You're building the program with its first clients and will ask how he has gone about this; then you'll see whether it fits him. It ends in one recommendation: the founding group, the Starter Path, your free sequenced path for anyone who shouldn't buy now, or "don't buy".

The same page shows your public price range and asks the affordability question: "Is this comfortable from your own income or savings, without new credit or buy-now-pay-later?" A no books nothing. He gets the free Starter Path, and the pause route is set, the tag that stops sales sends for 60–90 days.

That's the **Dual-Purpose Conversation**: a disclosed fit-and-research call of about 45 minutes, with its purpose, the price, and one recommendation stated before he books. With prep and the written recap it costs about an hour, and the Early week budgets for 4–5 of them.

### Research asks what he did

The research half asks about his past, never his predictions. What a man says he'd pay, or plans to do next month, is a loose guide to what he'll do. People name higher prices for hypothetical offers than they pay for real ones, and intentions turn into action less often than people expect. So ask what he tried and for how long, what he bought and measured, when he stopped, and what he hoped a photo would show. Praise for your idea costs him nothing, while a booked date and a payment cost him something, so those are the signals.

Those answers do two jobs. They're the excavation Close by Contract needs, the close that agrees its structure first: his stall and goal in his own words (Module 19). They're also the raw material of your stall taxonomy. Tell him how they're used: to build the program, stripped of his name, never quoted without his consent, and never in a marketing tool.

What's out is anything that turns the research into a warm-up for the pitch. That covers questions built to make him want the program ("wouldn't it help to have someone check your week?"), hypotheticals about your prices, and a research call that ends with a link. Each fails the Informed-Client Test, whether he'd still say it served him after seeing exactly what you did (Intro). Questions about photos carry one more rule. If his answers describe distress, or checking that crowds out his day, the sale stops and the call becomes a referral conversation.

### It ends in one recommendation and an ask

The fit half runs the close you'd run on any call: say his stall and goal back, recommend one thing, state the price once, ask, and resolve each real objection. It ends at a yes with day one booked, a no, or his Decision Date, the day he agrees to decide by, before the next start's last day to join. A good-fit adult who helps you learn for 45 minutes and leaves without a recommendation and a price has been failed.

The disclosure buys one extra question. After a clear no, you may ask once what made it a no, because you said the call was also research. Write the answer down without arguing with it. If it's "I can't afford it", money talk ends: the Starter Path once, and the pause route. Across 25 conversations, those answers are what you'll read at week 12 if the yeses don't come.

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| One 45-minute call, research then fit | Reach binds and you're under the 3–6 target | About an hour all-in per conversation |
| Research prompts in the booking form, then a 20–30-minute fit call | Held conversations reach the target, or calls start eating content hours | Thinner research, written instead of talked through |
| A fit call only | New conversations stop teaching you new stall patterns | You stop hearing what's changing, so keep one research question on the form |

**Default:** one 45-minute call while reach binds, because the hours are spare and every call is also research at no extra cost. Move the research prompts into the booking form once your count holds at 3–6 a week, since then the extra minutes cost more than they teach.

## 4. The Founding Group and Founding Private Seats

Every founding client joins one group from the very first, at a price near the real one with the next price stated. One or two founding Private seats, premium seats with fixed deliverables, give the premium buyer a real tier from day one.

### One group from the first client

The founding group is every founding client in one standing group from the first, with monthly entry, a new start every month. No one-to-one product comes before it. That product would have to be retired later, and its price history would show one-to-one selling below the group, which no later buyer could square with a premium tier. It also wouldn't test what the business runs on: the standing group, the weekly call, and the monthly start.

While the group has fewer than about four members, review runs at one-to-one depth, about 30–35 care minutes per client-week all-in. Reach binds and minutes are spare, so you can afford it, and each deep review teaches you what a good one says. Past about four members, the review itself settles toward the 12–20 minutes of the Program, your 12-week flagship, and the weekly call carries more. Put that on the page, because a client promised near one-to-one attention with no end will read the settling as a cut.

The weekly call runs at its fixed time from week 1, even with one member, under handles and with cameras optional. Your first client never waits for your fifth, and the second joins a group that already exists. Both learn from week 1 that membership here means logs kept and captures on schedule.

### The founding price sits near the real price

The founding price is about $1.2–1.5k for 12 weeks, with the next price printed beside it from day one. It sits just under the opening band of $1.5–2.2k. It's close because founding clients get the real review, and lower because they also get a program still being built. Warm sources and process proof you can already show, such as a published pre-commitment, push it toward the top of the range, and cold sources push it toward the bottom.

Its stated end is the first scheduled Price Step: dated to a start, announced at least 30 days out, landing low in the opening band, and naming what was added (Module 7). The founding group builds that answer: a tested check-in, written photo conditions, and a sample weekly review drawn from real ones. The seats are a stated number from founding Seat Math, the review minutes your Early week protects divided by one founding client-week. When they fill before the step, say it as plainly as the number: "Founding seats are full; the [April] start opens at $[1,500], with [n] seats."

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Founding price near the real one, next price stated | From the first client | A small gap under the opening band, closed by one scheduled step |
| The opening band from the first client | Your sources are warm and your process proof is already public | Fewer yeses in the months when each one also builds the instrument |
| Free or deep-discount "beta" seats | Never: free transformations traded for before-and-afters are how this category's fake proof gets made | Paid proof no buyer can see, and a price nobody paid |

**Default:** the founding price, because it pays for real review before any proof exists. It's also the lowest price the program will ever carry, so no founding client later watches someone pay less for the same seat. Move to the opening band when the founding seats fill or the step's date arrives, whichever comes first.

### Founding Private seats are the premium tier from day one

The Optimizer, the buyer with more money than time, can arrive in your first week: 25–35, well paid, and wary of being seen in a program. One or two founding Private seats at about $4–6k for 12 weeks give him a real tier while your minutes are spare. They sell on speed, privacy, and fixed deliverables, such as [a 24-hour review turnaround] and [a private weekly call], each covered by your service guarantee. They never sell on better results, since in adjacent fields' trials individual and group formats perform about the same at equal intensity. Every Private page says "we never read or score your face", because precision here means deliverables.

Private needs a call, and his usual way in is the priority tier, the faster paid assessment at about $350–600, credited to the seat. Showing the seat first gives every visitor an honest anchor, a real tier someone buys, and hiding it would fail the buyer it fits. One seat can also double a founding month's cash.

Stop at two, and sell the second only while the group's reviews keep their turnaround. A seat that slows them spends the minutes your first proof comes from. The seats open the Premium Lane, the Optimizer's path from the priority tier to Private (Module 13).

### Founding graduates are offered Round Two

Founding graduates reach week 12 from about month 4, and each gets the offer his record supports. One whose record shows measured momentum is offered Round Two, the maintenance block at about $0.8–1.2k. One who moved and holds is told he doesn't need it, and hears about the Hold, the measurement subscription.

One paid under the non-response clause, the cash refund when no marker moved despite his effort, hears no offer. His Path and Timeline Card, the one-page path and cost he saw before paying, priced both offers, so they need prices before your first founding checkout. The block is designed with the back end (Module 10) and offered as a renewal ask at his measured peak (Module 22).

## 5. The R&D Harvest

Founding clients buy a program, and the business gets its instruments, so every founding record has to leave something behind.

### The instruments are built on real weeks

No check-in, photo standard, or review template survives its first real client unchanged. A check-in designed at a desk meets a man whose shifts rotate, and the field he skips is the one you thought mattered. Photo conditions copied from a guide meet his bathroom light and a phone held at arm's length. A list of why men stall, written from your own months, meets stalls you never had. One-to-one review finds all of it fast, which is why the founding group's depth is research as much as service.

The R&D Harvest is what founding clients must produce. Each part has a version 1, done when it has survived the founding group. Say so on the founding page, so every founding client agrees to it before he pays:

| Harvest item | Built from | Version 1 is done when | It becomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stall taxonomy | Every founding record and conversation | Each founding client's stall is named, with its signs and first move | The backbone of templated review, around 20 clients |
| Check-in instrument | His ~10 minutes and your review minutes | A start runs with every field filled most weeks | The Program's check-in |
| Capture Standard | Every retake and the condition that failed | Clients match baseline conditions without a retake | The conditions behind every comparison you publish |
| Process testimonials | Clients whose records show progress | Consented, asked at a measured peak after the exit decision | The count toward the proof milestone |
| The Dated Record's log | Every conversation and client | An entry every month since month 1 | The part of the moat nobody can back-fill |

The harvest asks little of him: the check-in he'd fill anyway, two questions in his ordinary check-ins, and his consent. Ask what was hard to log, and which line of a review changed what he did. Nothing from his record is used beyond his own review without that consent.

The stall taxonomy files each founding client's stall under one of the three Stall Verdicts, the honest reasons a stall happens. They are unmeasured, misdirected, or a lever that doesn't move for him. Each entry holds the pattern's signs in the log, the first move that worked, and how often it has shown up. The taxonomy stays yours: the paid self-serve tools you build after about 20 graduates carry logs and capture instructions, never its decision rules. A rule applied without someone reading his record gives a confident wrong verdict.

The check-in and the Capture Standard, matched distance, lens, angle, light, expression, and time for every photo, get versioned the same way. Cut or rewrite a field that goes blank week after week. Keep any rating that dipped before a missed week, since it's your earliest warning of a slide. When a capture needs a retake, note the condition that failed, whether arm's length, overhead light, or a clenched jaw, and rewrite that step.

### Process testimonials wait for the record

Process testimonials, a client's account of the work itself, are your first proof, and their timing is fixed. Never ask before his fit window, the full-refund period of 14–21 days, has closed. Never ask in the week-6 conversation where his exit right, the pro-rata refund for a client who did the work and wants to stop, is open. A man deciding whether to leave can't give a free testimonial. The first ask comes at the first measured peak after his exit decision is settled, from about week 7, and only if his record shows progress.

Each one carries separate, revocable written consent and no incentive. It stays about the process: what the weekly review was like, and what he knows now that he didn't. Ten graduates with one is the first of the proof milestone's two conditions, the gate into your proof price band. So the founding group starts the count that later lets your price rise.

### The Dated Record starts with the first client

The Dated Record, your published pre-commitment plus a dated log no one can back-fill, opens in month 1 (Module 3). Its first entries are founding facts: fit declines from the first conversation, and check-in completion and turnaround kept from the first client. The claim rate joins from the first refund window. Start the log at month 6 and the founding months are gone for good, though they're the months a skeptic checks first.

## 6. The Month-3 Gate

At week 12 you read two numbers in a fixed order, held conversations and then clients per conversation. A thin first quarter has two causes, and they need opposite fixes.

### Volume is read first

Conversion can't be read on a handful of conversations. A reach problem can also pass for a conversion problem when the few you held came from your warmest sources. So volume goes first: fewer than about 15 held conversations by week 12 means your sources didn't produce, whatever your close rate looks like. The fix is sources, starting with the week-3 shift, run harder. In Band A, where the channel never breaks out, add the default paid test at months 3–4.

That test spends about $300–1,000 a month pushing pieces that already brought in eligible adults, once your pages meet the claims rules. It's judged against a holdout and your maximum affordable cost per eligible lead, the most an eligible lead is worth paying for (Module 27). It needs proven pieces, so if nothing has produced leads on its own yet, the pieces come first.

### Then conversion

Fewer than 3 clients from 25 or more held conversations means the conversations happen and the yeses don't. Then the offer or the position needs fixing before you build anything further: no paid reach, no new platform, no new rung. Your research answers tell you which. When most no's came from men who wanted a promise about bone or a face rating, the position is drawing the wrong buyers. When good-fit adults said no to the container, its price, or the minutes it asks, the offer doesn't fit the buyers you're drawing.

Read in that order, the two legs make the **Month-3 Gate**. You set it before the quarter starts, so a slow month can't talk you into the wrong fix:

**Under about 15 held by week 12, fix sources; under 3 clients from 25 or more, fix the offer or position.**

### When to keep going

| By week 12 | The read | The move |
|---|---|---|
| Fewer than ~15 held | The volume leg fires: reach | Fix sources; the Band A paid test once pages pass |
| 15 or more held, 3+ clients | Pass | Keep going as planned |
| 15–24 held, fewer than 3 clients | Too few to read conversion | Keep going; check that every call ended in an ask; read again at 25 |
| 25+ held, fewer than 3 clients | The conversion leg fires | Fix the offer or position before building further |

Keep going means the plan stays as written. The founding price steps on its date, Round Two and the Hold open at the first graduation, and nothing else changes on fewer than about 30 events. At founding volume, a ratio swings by chance. Even a fired conversion leg can be bad luck at the bottom of the 15–35% an offer without proof should hold, so let the objection log decide what you fix. The gate also can't place you in a band, because reach decides the band from month 6.

**When the signals disagree.** Reid *(composite operator)* reaches week 12 with [31] held conversations and [1] client. His notes are full of praise, and [six] men said they'd join "next month". His weekly count has sat inside the 3–6 target since week 5. His instinct says more conversations, or a lower price, will turn them into clients. The conversion leg has fired, though, and praise is the one signal that costs a buyer nothing.

So he checks his own calls first. [Twelve] of the 31 heard one recommendation, a price, and an ask, and the rest ran long on research and ended at "let me know". The first fix is the ask, so every call from here ends at a yes, a no, or a Decision Date. Then his objection log sorts the no's: [nine] wanted a promise about bone, traced to hooks promising a new jawline, a position fix. He leaves price, paid reach, and a second platform alone, and reads the leg again after the next 25.

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** This module is the plan. Reach binds, so labeled sources, the Dual-Purpose Conversation, and the founding group are the default until the Month-3 Gate passes. The trap is a quieter way of waiting for an audience: posting daily while the warm-network message sits unsent and door completions wait a day for a reply.

**Growing.** Conversion and selling hours bind, and the founding method becomes how you open a new offer. Round Two gets a small founding cohort of its own: disclosed as first, priced near its real price with the next one stated, and harvested for its own instrument. The trap is a "founding" price on the core Program after it has sold at full price, which is a discount with a story attached.

**Scaling.** Care minutes bind, and a waiting list leaves a founding price nothing to do. A new tier such as Program Async runs as a pilot through the standing door, with capped seats at its real price and a review date set in advance. The trap is a pilot that turns into a launch, with a waitlist page, a countdown, and a push to fill it.

## Standard Check

- **Disclosed in the first line.** The warm-network message says it's a business message, and the Dual-Purpose Conversation puts its research job, the price range, and the affordability question on the booking page (*Sell directly*). A research call that ends in a pitch would fail the Informed-Client Test's ceiling.
- **Founding scarcity with its math shown.** Seats are a stated number from founding Seat Math, and the founding price ends on a dated step announced 30+ days out (*Use real dates*). Neither leans on urgency, which keeps both clear of the line on fake scarcity.
- **Stop rules apply to friends.** A friend's "I can't afford it" ends money talk, he gets no discount and no second message, and nobody under 18 is contacted (the line on vulnerability).
- **Testimonials after the exit decision.** The first process-testimonial ask waits for a measured peak after the week-6 exit decision, so no ask leans on his refund rights (*Name the destination boldly*, at its default).
