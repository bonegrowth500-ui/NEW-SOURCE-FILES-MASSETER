# 5 · The Door
*Part II — The Offer System*

**The shift:** from *"Free discovery calls for everyone, or a link to checkout"* to *"One door that checks age, answers fast, caps free calls, checks fit and affordability before any payment, and ends in one recommendation."*

Reid (composite operator) has [20] free calls on his calendar this week, booked from a link under every video. By Friday he knows what the link couldn't tell him. [Two] callers were minors, and he found out [ten minutes] in. Most of the adults were no-fit. One wanted a promise about bone, and another could only pay with a buy-now-pay-later app. One man spent his call describing how many times a day he checks his profile in photos, and Reid, unsure what else to do, pitched him. On Thursday, a man who had told Reid he couldn't afford it got the automated email announcing next month's price step.

Reid isn't careless. His door is a calendar link, so every screening decision happens live, inside the most expensive hour he has. His email tool never hears what his calls learned. This module builds the other kind of door. It checks age before anything is kept and fit and money before anything is paid, answers adults within hours, and ends every path in one recommendation. Even the email system obeys what a buyer said.

## 1. Why One Owned Door

The door is where eligibility, fit, speed, and the sale meet, so it has to be one door, on a site you own, that every buyer passes through before money moves.

The two doors most operators start with fail here in opposite directions. A calendar link offering free calls to everyone screens nothing until the call, so it learns a caller's age, state of mind, and money only after the hour is spent. A checkout link screens nothing at all. A 16-year-old with a parent's card, a man who checks his profile many times a day, and a buyer stretching on buy-now-pay-later all pay the same way. Those payments tend to come back as refunds, disputes, or harm.

### Checks run cheapest first

One door fixes both by running its checks in order of cost. The age question costs him a click, the fit and money questions a minute, and a conversation costs you an hour. Asked in that order, they spend your live hours only on adults who passed everything cheaper. The order protects him too: a minor leaves before he types anything worth keeping, and a man in distress meets help before he meets an offer.

The door has to be owned. The age fork, the door's legal-adult question, must come before any field that stores data, and platform lead forms ask for an email first. Answers about how a man feels about his face should never pass through an ad platform's forms or pixels.

Your own site also keeps the source tag, so you can count eligible adults, legal adults who could pay from their own income or savings, by the content that sent them, and see which pieces mostly draw minors to the fork. It has to be one door as well, because checks that differ by channel make the weakest channel the loophole. Put together, every buyer walks the same path:

**age fork → result → reply within hours → a conversation or a written plan → fit and money checks → one recommendation.**

That's the **Door**, the one owned path from first click to one recommendation. Every discovery asset routes to its self-assessment, and every warm route that skips the self-assessment lands on a checkout carrying the same checks. Build its first version before your first paid step. After that, change one setting at a time and judge it on at least 30 events, because one busy week says almost nothing.

### It sells a decision, never a face rating

The category's cheapest product is a verdict on a face, and it fails here three ways. Apps and one-off "analyses" already sell it cheaply. A reading of his structure is exactly what the House Standard, the rules this playbook sells by, rules out. And a score feeds the checking your fit questions exist to catch. No app supplies judgment: why this man stalled, what he should change first, whether coaching fits him, and whom he should see instead. That's harder to copy, and it's the decision he's stuck on.

So the rule is Sell the Decision, Not a Face Rating: every step delivers a judgment about what he should do next, and no step reads, rates, or scores his face. It keeps you clear of medical opinions too, because a decision about habits and presentation doesn't need one.

## 2. The Self-Assessment and Speed to Lead

The self-assessment is the door's first step, and its job is routing: it sorts who he is and what he needs before anything is sold, then hands every adult to a reply within hours.

### The fork comes before any field that keeps data

The fork asks "Are you a legal adult where you live?" The wording matters, because the age of majority isn't 18 everywhere you sell. Nothing comes before it: no email field, no name, no question about his face. Ask it neutrally, with no "you must be 18 to continue" above it, because a question that announces its right answer teaches a 16-year-old what to click. Then make sure the back button can't change the answer, because a fork he can retake in two seconds is decoration.

A no sends him to the education lane: public content and an orthodontist referral for anyone not yet a legal adult where he lives, with no data kept. The page has no email field, stores no answer, and carries no ad pixel, since a pixel would add a minor to an audience you'd later advertise to. All that remains is an anonymous count of fork exits by source. Jordan *(composite, minor)*, 16, arrives from a short, answers no, and reads your public answer on growing faces beside the referral. The count rises by one, and nothing else about him exists.

Warm checkouts that skip the self-assessment carry the fork as a one-line attestation, "I'm a legal adult where I live", ahead of any payment field. An attestation is a statement rather than proof, and it's the proportionate check for a coaching purchase. Asking for ID would leave you holding documents you'd have to protect, and data you never collect can't leak.

### Answers route, and one item can pause everything

After the fork come [8–10] plain questions. They cover his goal in his words, what he's tried and bought, what he measures, why now, what he values in help, and when he could start. Each answer feeds a tag: his source, his stage, the signals that set his route, and his claim on a free slot in a full week. These are Readiness Tags, the door's labels that decide which sequence he gets (Module 26).

Just before the email field sits the first tier of the **Fit Check**, a plain-language check for signals that buying could hurt him. It's one unscored item: is how he feels about his appearance making everyday life hard? It sits there so any pause is written in the same step as his address, and no sequence can start ahead of it.

An endorsed answer shows him where to find help and offers a conversation with you, a referral conversation and never a sales one. He sees no booking button and no offer. The answer also sets the pause route, a content-free tag that stops every sales send for 60–90 days. His fit answers never become tags, and the pause records no reason.

### The result names one next step

He sees his result on the page and gets the full version by emailed link, so confirming his address comes with a reason to click. The result page, his first sales conversation with you, has fixed parts. It names his stall as one of the three Stall Verdicts: unmeasured, misdirected, or a lever that doesn't move for him (Module 14).

It then gives the Honest Answer, your public straight answer on what can change at his age, and one step for this week. Then come a button to book a free fit conversation and a note of how often you'll email, with a one-click exit. When free slots are closed to him, the button offers the Decision Assessment, a paid written plan worth its fee without a purchase.

### Speed to lead: replied within hours, booked within 24–48 hours

Speed to lead is a four-step sequence: a personal reply within hours, a booking within 24–48 hours, a reminder, and a held conversation. In other markets' data, contact within the hour far outperforms later contact. Show rates also fall the longer a booking waits, so a reply tomorrow and a slot in a fortnight lose much of what the door earned. With a reply within hours and a booking inside 24–48 hours, plan on 10–20% of eligible leads reaching a held conversation.

The reply matters more here than its minutes suggest. Men in this category have been sold to by bots, auto-DMs, and scripted closers. A reply from the person they watched, naming one thing from their answers, is the first evidence that the brand is what it said. Early on it costs about an hour a week. Past about an hour a day, replies start eating content hours, so template them around one line written for him and automate booking and reminders. The reply still comes from you.

He books first and logs while he waits. The booking comes before any task. Then he gets a free 7-day behavior log, and his baseline capture stays on his own device. That log is Let Him Succeed Before He Pays, mastery before money, and it becomes his Week-Zero Baseline, the first week of his record, if he enrolls (Module 17). Asked for first, the log puts a task between him and the booking just as his interest peaks. Offered after, it gives the wait a use.

### Door v0 goes live in week one

Door v0, the first working version of the door, goes live in week one, because any paid step before it sells without knowing age, fit, or money. It holds the fork, the education-lane page, the questions and distress item, the result, a booking page with its disclosure, and the pause tag, wired into your email tool before its first sequence. It also holds a checkout for the priority tier, the faster, premium version of the assessment, which sells from month 0. That checkout carries the attestation, the Fit Check, and the affordability question, the one fixed sentence about paying from his own money. Templated replies, the async assessment, and the overflow route wait for your numbers.

## 3. The Fit Conversation and the Call Cap

The fit conversation is the door's human step, and it stays free for as long as conversations are what the business is short of.

### It's disclosed, short, and ends in a decision

The fit conversation, the free, disclosed first call that ends in one recommendation, runs 20–30 minutes. In the founding months it runs about 45, as a dual-purpose call that gathers research on the stall as well as deciding. Its booking page says what a skeptic would otherwise suspect. The call is free, you'll ask for a yes or a no, and you never rate a face or give medical opinions. It ends in one recommendation, which may be a program, the Starter Path, the free sequenced path for anyone who shouldn't buy now, or "don't buy".

Disclosure carries more weight here than in most markets. To a man who has met the category's "free strategy call", a pitch discovered halfway through confirms everything he feared. Disclosed up front, the same ask is the structure he agreed to when he booked, which is how Close by Contract, the close that agrees its structure first, opens (Module 19).

What the call delivers is fixed, however you run it: his stall and goal said back in his words, one recommendation, the price stated once, the ask, and a written recap within 24 hours. It ends in a yes or a no. A screened, good-fit buyer who leaves un-asked has been failed as surely as one who was pushed, because he goes back to guessing without hearing what you'd recommend.

### The Call Cap spends free hours where they change a decision

Somewhere past 6–8 held conversations a week, the calendar starts eating the business. The signs arrive together: most weeks above that range, a show rate sliding toward about 60%, more than half of held conversations ending no-fit, and selling hours cutting into your content minimum. In this niche the no-fits have a recognizable shape. One man wants a promise about bone, one can pay only on credit, and one mentions snoring that makes the right answer a referral.

A free hour isn't worth the same to every buyer. It changes the decision of the uncertain buyer, who needs to test you live. He's the burned buyer, who has paid for a promise before, or the one whose answers never settle on a goal. It closes the high-intent buyer, stage "now" with your offer page read, who is ready and wants a person. The buyer who would decide the same way from a written plan loses little by getting one, and he keeps the plan.

That's the logic of the **Call Cap**: free fit conversations stay open up to about 6–8 held a week, reserved by tags for uncertain or high-intent buyers, and the overflow goes to the paid or async Decision Assessment. Below the cap, every eligible adult can book free. Once it binds, free slots show only on results carrying those tags, and every other result offers an assessment delivered within days beside the next open slot. Nobody is refused a decision, only a free hour this week. The priority tier stays open throughout for the buyer who'd rather pay for speed.

### Free, until free hours are the scarce thing

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Free fit conversation, up to the Call Cap | Every stage; below the cap it's simply free | Tags and a weekly count to keep |
| Small credited fee to book (~$25–50) | No-shows keep eating selling hours after bookings land inside 24–48 h with a reminder | Friction, and a paid step, so the Fit Check and the affordability question move to booking |
| Paid Decision Assessment for everyone | As the overflow only | As the whole door, it cuts conversations you need while every eligible lead counts |
| Direct checkout only | As a warm route beside the door | As the only door, uncertain buyers stall or refund, and screening thins |
| Application before any call | Inside the path to Private, the premium seat | Slow, and friction without judgment |

**Default: free, up to the Call Cap.** A fee filters out eligible adults while reach binds, and a burned buyer reads a paid first look as the rating-app pattern he's trying to leave. Switch to the small credited fee only when no-shows keep eating selling hours after bookings already land inside 24–48 hours with a reminder, because lead time and reminders lift show rates without filtering anyone out.

Judge the cap on its economics rather than on how the week feels. A paid overflow thins take-up while it saves hours, and only your numbers say which effect wins. So compare settings, such as the cap's level or the overflow's price, on profit per operator hour per 100 eligible leads, after at least 30 events at each, since a month of solo volume is mostly noise. As care minutes, the review and call time each client takes, start to bind, the cap tightens below 6–8, because every hour it frees goes to review.

## 4. The Fit Check and the Affordability Question

No payment moves until two checks have run: one for signals that buying could hurt him, and one for money he can't comfortably spend.

### Two tiers, and the second runs at every paid step

This category sells to men unhappy with how they look, and some carry a concern that measurement would feed rather than settle. Clinical figures say why you check. Serious appearance concern runs well above the general population's rate among people seeking appearance change, a finding strong in clinics and weak as a transfer to coaching buyers (Module 2). A planning figure says what you'll see. Roughly 5–20% of paid-step applicants show at least one signal, most continue after a conversation, and acute signals are rare. After about 30 applicants, your own aggregate counts replace that range.

The Fit Check's second tier runs at booking of every paid step: either tier of the assessment, a small booking fee, any checkout, a payment plan, a checkout link in a DM. In plain words it asks about checking, what the concern crowds out, avoidance, a booked procedure, recent appearance purchases, and how he'd describe it in a sentence. The check is never scored, never shown back to him as a result, never a label, and never stored in a marketing tool. You read the answers yourself, no freelancer ever sees them, and you keep them only as long as the decision needs them.

### A signal pauses the sale

Any signal starts the signal pause, five fixed protections between the signal and his money. Each closes a different route to a sale he'd regret, and together they cost a good-fit buyer a few days at most:

| Part of the pause | What it protects |
|---|---|
| No payment that day | The decision gets made away from the moment the signal showed |
| Adjusted expectations, in writing | He decides on what the Program, your 12-week flagship, can and can't do for him, in his words |
| At least 72 hours to cool off | Urgency has time to pass, and with monthly starts a good-fit buyer loses nothing |
| No payment plan | A plan eases the stretch for exactly the buyer who should feel the whole decision |
| The fit window starts on day one of delivery | The pause never eats his full-refund window |

The pause holds a human conversation with three plain questions: how often it happens, what it crowds out, and the words he uses for it. It can end three ways: he enrolls with adjusted expectations, takes the Starter Path in its reading-only version, or gets a referral. An insecurity-led buyer usually continues on the Dignity Route, which keeps stakes to time, money from here on, and guessing (Module 15). Checking or fixation routes to a referral and reading-only content, with no capture tools and nothing that tracks his face, because measurement would feed the loop. Acute distress, or "everything is ruined" language, ends the sale.

Serial appearance purchasers get the same pause. A run of recent appearance purchases, each dropped for the next, is a signal on its own, because the pace of buying is the pattern. Tell it apart from a burned buyer. Theo *(composite, Burned Struggler)* bought [a device and a course], stopped, and now checks everything, which is due diligence to support. A man on his [fourth] purchase in [a few months] may be buying relief rather than a plan, and the pause gives you both three days to find out.

### The affordability question runs at every paid step

The second check is one sentence, and its wording never changes, because automation can only honor an answer it reads the same way everywhere. Ask it before every paid step, in every channel:

**"Is this comfortable from your own income or savings, without new credit or buy-now-pay-later?"**

At a checkout it comes after the attestation and before any payment option appears, so a no never meets a plan offer. On a call the words stay the same. A yes lets the Fit Check run. A no ends money talk: he gets the Starter Path once, and the pause route is set. Money that isn't his goes to the Starter Path too.

### The pause route binds your automation

The pause route stops every sales sequence and date send for 60–90 days, then asks his permission again. Three things set it: an endorsed distress item at the door, a "can't afford" at a call, checkout, or plan step, and a fit-check pause. It records no reason, because a stored reason gets used. An email tool that knows who "can't afford" will one day meet a campaign for payment plans.

While the pause runs he gets only what he asked for, such as his result or the Starter Path, and no start announcement, price-step email, sales sequence, or abandoned-checkout email. When it ends, one message asks whether he wants to keep hearing from you. It sells nothing, and silence keeps him out of sales sends.

Every automation you add later has to read the tag, including the ones you build long after you've forgotten why it exists. Check monthly that promotional sends to paused leads stand at zero. Reid's price-step email is what a missing check looks like.

**When the signals disagree.** A buyer has watched [a dozen] of your long-form videos and opened every email. He clicks from a decision-stage video straight to the Program checkout, attests that he's a legal adult, and answers the affordability question: no. Every warmth signal says he's ready; his answer says the money isn't comfortable. The answer decides, because it's the only check that asks about the man carrying the decision rather than his interest in it.

So the checkout ends there, with no plan, no discount, and no "are you sure?". He sees the Starter Path once, with its re-entry point. The pause route silences the checkout's own abandoned-cart email, the automation most likely to cross this stop, because it fires on exactly the buyer who stopped at the question. The back button can't change his answer, for the same reason it can't change the fork. The door stays open for his return, through the same question.

## 5. The Decision Assessment and the Written Plan

The Decision Assessment is the door's paid step, and it's worth selling only if its written plan is worth the fee to a man who never buys anything else.

### One product at two speeds

The standard tier costs $150–250 and becomes the overflow route once the Call Cap binds. The priority tier, the same plan faster with a recorded walkthrough of it, costs about $350–600. It's credited to the Program or to Private, and it's open from month 0 as the entry for the Optimizer, the buyer with income and little time. At volume the standard tier goes async-first: a written plan and a recorded review, with an optional short live slot. A live assessment costs 1–1.5 hours all-in and a templated async one 0.5–0.75, which is why its template sits second in your build queue, after templated review.

It reads his record, never his face. The plan draws on his answers, what he's tried and bought, his free log if he kept one, his goal in his words, and his constraints. His baseline capture stays on his own device, and the plan tells him how to take it so a later comparison means something. The booking page says all of this before he pays: the price, the turnaround, that his face is never read, and that the plan may end in "don't buy".

The fee is credited toward any program he joins, and the credit is held about 90 days, two starts. After a "not now" or a Starter Path recommendation, it's held until he enrolls, for up to about 12 months. State the terms once, in writing, on the plan, and never raise the credit again as a reason to decide. His decision point is the next monthly start or an announced price step, because a credit he has already paid for is never a deadline. If the plan wasn't useful, the plan-usefulness refund returns his fee on a short written request (Module 7).

### Six parts, each worth the fee alone

The written plan is the assessment's deliverable, and it has six parts. Each must pass one test, because most assessments end without a purchase and the plan is all those buyers keep: would a man who never buys still keep it?

| Part | What it holds | Why he keeps it without buying |
|---|---|---|
| The verdict, with reasons | Unmeasured, misdirected, or "this lever doesn't move for you", argued from his record | It ends "is it me?" with a cause he can check, and it's allowed to say stop |
| A measurement setup | What to log weekly, when to capture, what not to measure | Every month he spends alone from here becomes evidence |
| What to stop spending on | Named purchases and habits that can't show him anything | Money and months back, the plainest return a non-buyer gets |
| The levers that matter | His goal sorted into changeable, debated, never claimed, and referral | The category's noise cut to a short list he can act on |
| One recommendation | A program, Private, the Starter Path, or "don't buy", with the price once | The decision he paid for, instead of a menu |
| A re-check date | A dated moment to read his own record, and what to look at | His next decision point, which he owns whether or not he buys |

The verdict carries the most weight, and the third verdict makes the other two believable. A plan that always finds a fixable cause is a pitch with a fee attached. One that can say "this lever doesn't move for you, stop paying for it" is judgment. The stop-spending part does the same work with money, since a plan that tells him to spend less is hard to mistake for a sales page. It never lists clinical care; anything clinical goes on the plan's "worth seeing" line.

The levers come from the Outcome Map, the brand's straight answer to the bone question by age and evidence tier (Module 3). Debated items appear at their tier, and bone never appears as a lever. Here is the full template, ready to fill:

```
[Brand] · Decision Assessment · Written plan for [first name] · [date]

1. What you told us
   Goal, in your words: "[his words]"
   Tried: [routine] for [months] · Bought: [items]
   Constraints: [minutes a day] · [schedule] · [budget from your own income or savings]
2. The verdict: [unmeasured | misdirected | this lever doesn't move for you]
   Why, from your record: [reason] · [reason] · [reason]
3. Your measurement setup (it runs without us)
   Weekly: log [habit block A] and [habit block B]; review on [day]
   Captures on your own device, to the attached conditions:
   baseline [date] · about week 6 [date] · week 12 [date] · none in between
4. Stop spending on: [item], because [reason] · [item], because [reason]
5. The levers that matter for your goal
   Changeable and measurable, ranked: [lever] · [lever] · [lever]
   Debated, at its evidence tier: [item]
   Not something habits change: [item] · Worth seeing [professional] about: [item]
6. One recommendation: [offer] from [start date] · [public price] · [credit] applied
   Why, in your words: [reason]
   Before you pay: the Path and Timeline Card and the Expectation Document
7. Re-check date: [date]. Look at [log count], [capture], and one question: [question]

Credit terms: [stated once]. Refunded if this plan wasn't useful: [how to ask].
We never read or score your face.
```

### Samples and a published mix let a skeptic check the plan

The verification kit is two samples a skeptic can read before buying: the sample written plan built here and the sample weekly review built with the Program. Both are labeled as composites, posted on your Verify Page, where skeptics look you up, and sent to any buyer who asks what he'd get. For a burned buyer the kit is his due diligence: Theo reads the sample plan before he books anything, because he wants the deliverable before the promise. Write yours from a composite and label it as a sample at the top, because a real client's plan is private and an unlabeled one reads as proof.

After about 30 assessments, publish the recommendation mix in aggregate: how often the plan said a program, Private, the Starter Path, a referral, or "don't buy". A man paying to be told whether to buy suspects the answer is always "buy". The count is the one answer to that suspicion he can check. It goes into your dated public log beside your fit declines, on the schedule you committed to in month 1.

## 6. The One Recommendation

Every path through the door ends in one recommendation, because a man who came to stop guessing and leaves with a menu has been handed his guessing back.

### Four recommendations, each a real destination

| Recommendation | When it's the one | What goes with it |
|---|---|---|
| The Program, or at Scaling Program Async, its tier without the live call | An eligible adult with no open signal, a goal in the changeable column, and the [minutes a day] to do the work | The next monthly start, the price once, the Card and the Expectation Document |
| Private | The Optimizer who needs speed, privacy, and fixed deliverables, while your minutes are spare or once the seat earns what a Program seat does per care hour | A call first, and the same checks |
| The Starter Path | Not now, not a fit, "can't afford", or money that isn't his | Handed over once, with its re-entry point |
| "Don't buy", or a referral | The lever doesn't move for him; snoring, jaw pain, bite, or growth questions; checking or fixation | The reason in writing, and who to see |

Program Async is the real tier under the cash ceiling for buyers aged 20–24, about 1–1.25 months of take-home. It matters once the Program's price has climbed to the ceiling for 25–34. Every tier stays visible on your public page, premium first, so he can see what he isn't being offered, and the recommendation is still one. "Don't buy" is the answer that makes the other three credible. It's a destination too: a referral names who to see, and the Starter Path keeps him measuring.

### Before payment, he sees the whole path

Any paid recommendation goes out with the Path and Timeline Card and the Expectation Document, before any payment. Between them they say what 12 weeks reliably deliver, when visible change tends to show, what the full path is likely to cost, what can't move, and how reviews are made (Module 6). At checkout the Card sits above the pay button, because a buyer who learns the full path only after paying becomes the refund request of week three.

### Warm routes skip the conversation, never the checks

Decision-stage assets may pitch with public prices and link straight to a checkout, under the Warmth Ladder, the rule for which asset may ask for what (Module 18). That checkout runs the same checks as the door. Once the door has verified his age and fit, a DM may carry one recommendation and a checkout link, which is *Sell directly* at full strength. An unverified contact gets the door, never a close, and Private needs a call. By Scaling, about 50–70% of enrollments come without a live call. They're carried by the Async Arc, the call's arc run through pages, walkthroughs, emails, and voice notes (Module 20).

The Informed-Client Test's floor, which says under-asking a good-fit buyer fails him too, sets the minimum here. Every good-fit buyer leaves every step knowing the one recommendation, its price, the next real date, and what to do next. His written recap within 24 hours carries all four.

## Worked Example: Dan, Through Cole's Door Twice

Run one buyer through the door at two stages of the same business. Cole (composite operator) is in Band B, the steady band. Dan *(composite, Struggler)*, 24, arrives both times from a long-form video. He has about eight months of [his daily routine] behind him, a [jaw device] in a drawer, nothing measured, and a comparison-photo habit. A second buyer shows what a signal does.

**Month [2], the Early door. The read: reach binds, so this adult is worth a free hour.** Dan finishes the self-assessment at [9 pm] on a Monday. The fork says adult, the distress item is clear, and his tags read "now" and Struggler. His result names his stall as unmeasured and offers a free fit conversation. Cole replies personally at [8 am], quoting Dan's own line about team photos, and offers [two] slots inside 48 hours. Dan books [Wednesday], gets the 7-day log, and keeps his baseline capture on his phone.

After a reminder, the dual-purpose call runs about 45 minutes. It ends in one recommendation: the founding group, every early client in one group with monthly entry, from [the next start] at [~$1.2–1.5k]. Cole states the price once and asks, and Dan says yes. At checkout he attests, answers the question with a yes from savings, and passes the Fit Check. The Card and the Expectation Document arrive before he pays. Cole's minutes: [10] for the reply and about an hour all-in for the call. Left alone: any fee to book, and what the device cost him.

**Month [20], the Scaling door. The read: care minutes bind, so free hours go to buyers a call would change.** Cole's cap now sits at [4] free conversations a week, below the 6–8 ceiling, because review hours come first. This week's slots are gone by [Tuesday]. Dan's answers show a clear goal, stage "now", and no uncertainty. His result offers the next free slot, [nine] days out, or a Decision Assessment within [three working days] at [~$150–250], credited. He picks the assessment because it answers him this week, and clears the attestation, the question, and the Fit Check at booking.

Cole's templated plan reads Dan's [five] logged days and his history. The verdict is unmeasured, and the stop list names another device and the weekly bathroom-light photo. The recommendation is Program Async from [the next start], at [~$2.4–2.8k] with the credit applied. Dan wants weekly review, [his rotating shifts] rule out a fixed live call, and it fits the budget he gave. The door has verified him, so the plan arrives with a checkout link. Dan enrolls [two days] later, after the question, the Fit Check, the Card, and the Document. Cole's minutes: [~40]. Left alone: a call Dan didn't need, and any look at his face.

**Month [20], Thursday. Adrian's pause.** Adrian *(composite, Optimizer)*, 31, a consultant, books the priority tier at [~$350–600] and wants to pay on the spot. The affordability question gets a yes, from income. Then the second tier asks about procedures, and he answers that a consultation is booked for [next month]. That's a signal, so there's no payment that day. On a [20]-minute call Cole asks the three plain questions. It comes up on video calls a few times a week and crowds out nothing. ## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** Reach binds, so every eligible adult who finishes the door earns a personal reply within hours and a free conversation. The priority tier is the only paid assessment open from month 0, for the Optimizer who would rather pay than wait. The trap is adding a fee or a paid overflow because the week feels full. A full week with few held conversations is a reach problem, and friction makes it worse.

**Growing.** The Call Cap starts to bind, and the overflow opens once the bind signs show together. Reserve the free slots by tag and send the rest to the Decision Assessment, judged on the economics after 30 events. The trap is booking first come, first served, so buyers who'd decide the same way in writing take the hours your uncertain buyers needed.

**Scaling.** Care minutes bind, so assessments go async-first, the cap tightens below 6–8, and most enrollments come without a call. The trap is automation outgrowing the pause route. Every sequence, cart email, and date send you add must read the tag, and the monthly check stays at zero.

## Standard Check

- **The pause route in every machine.** A distress item, a "can't afford", or a fit-check pause writes one content-free tag that every sequence, cart email, and date send obeys for 60–90 days, then a re-permission ask that sells nothing. That's the line on vulnerability carried into automation, checked monthly against zero.
- **Checks before money, the fork before data.** The fork keeps nothing from a minor, and a fit signal pauses the sale for a conversation instead of a payment. Both hold the line on vulnerability by design, through plain, unscored questions that never reach a marketing tool.
- **A decision, never a verdict on his face.** The written plan sells judgment (*Sell directly*), may say "don't buy", and never rates his face or offers a medical opinion (the line on structural claims). The published mix and the plan-usefulness refund let a buyer check that it earned its fee.
