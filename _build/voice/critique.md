# Voice sample v1: critique (Step 2.6)

**Scope.** `_build/voice/sample-v1.md` (about 1,570 words: Module 1's opening and Section 3, one Module 19 objection, and Module 1's fixed blocks), read against STYLE v2, SPEC §8, and the House Standard. Numbers were checked against LEDGER v2 and names against FRAMEWORKS.md. About 300 lines of each source were sampled: Source A, the long-form channel mastery file (lines 1–150 and 480–629), and Source B, the 20-module blueprint (lines 1–150 and 3550–3700). Line numbers (L) refer to sample-v1.md.

## Verdict

The register is right and close to lockable. The sample is calm, direct and reasoned, with no hype, shame or fake urgency, and its paragraph, heading and bold discipline is already what 28 modules need. It is **not ready to lock as it stands**. Eleven findings are major, and most of them sit at template level, so each would repeat 28 times:

- a banned word family ("diagnose")
- an over-certain flagship model
- a Belief Chain contradiction in the objection debrief
- house nouns used before they are glossed
- a business section that reads as generic once the house vocabulary is removed
- a contrast-sentence habit that will dominate at book length
- a natural density that lands about 1,500 words short, with nothing in the template saying what fills the gap
- a Standard Check that could be pasted into any module
- no options-plus-default block anywhere
- an objection unit that stops before the ask
- an opening that presents the upside case as the result of one question

The sample also can't lock two template elements it doesn't show: the worked example and a checklist extra. v2 should include both.

## Calibration: what each source teaches

| Metric (prose only)* | Source A | Source B | Sample v1 | Target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Median sentence length | 17–20 words | 8–11 words | 12 words | 14–18 words |
| Sentences of 8 words or fewer | 18–19% | 35–54% | 26% | ≤20% |
| Mean paragraph length | 90–100 words (max ~300) | 14–23 words | 43 words (max 86) | 40–110 words |
| Paragraphs set entirely in bold | 0% | ~90% | labels only | labels only |

*Heuristic sentence split; headings, tables, bullets and blockquotes excluded.*

**Source A works because** it reasons before it instructs. Every framework variant carries a "Reasoning:" line and a "How to choose". It runs examples end to end on a realistic case: the Two-Job Scorecard applied to one real title, a retention-graph autopsy with its drop points, a length decision priced block by block. Its niche detail is specific ("the search bar as a confession": four queries, four different people). It also names its own ceilings ("Now the honest ceiling, because pretending faceless has no downside would be exactly the kind of overclaim this program refuses").

**Avoid from A:**
- paragraphs of 200–300 words
- telling each idea three times: in the section, again in an "average vs. S+" list, again in the closing standard
- value superlatives ("the single most important tension", "the entire competitive advantage", "the rarest signal available")
- aphorism stacks ("Events entertain. Components compound.")
- signposting its own structure ("Hold that idea; Part 5 is almost entirely about…")
- "It is not a keyword box. It is a confession booth." as the default sentence shape

**Source B works because** it is scannable (numbered sections, Key Takeaways), concrete (frequencies and steps), and explicit about applying each idea to the niche ("For your niche:").

**Avoid from B:**
- bold on nearly every paragraph, so bold stops signalling anything
- bullet lists with no reasoning (a five-step DM framework at one line per step)
- single-point numbers and false precision ("5-10 Stories per day", "approximately 1-3 seconds")
- pop neuroscience ("The brain is a prediction machine", "dopamine-trained brain")
- overclaims ("you can change your bone structure") and anger aimed at clinicians
- throat-clearing ("By the end of this module, you will understand…")
- niche grounding added after generic advice instead of built into the reasoning

**Where v1 sits.** It avoids nearly all of B's weaknesses and most of A's. Its drift risks are A's contrast cadence (VOI-6) and flourishes (VOI-12), and B's generic business content dressed in house vocabulary (VOI-5, VOI-13). The one thing it hasn't yet taken from A is what carries length: the case worked end to end (VOI-7).

## Will it hold for 7,000 words? (Dimension 6)

**Yes, if the rules below are locked.** Four features of the sample hold up at length: short reasoned paragraphs, headings that narrate the argument, tables where comparison is the point, and scripts that change the texture.

Four things will fail at length unless there is a rule for them:

1. **Cadence.** At the sample's rate, a module carries about 45 "not X, but Y" turns and dozens of fragment endings (VOI-6). The reader starts hearing the template instead of the argument.
2. **Length.** At the sample's density, a module lands around 5,000 words (roughly 4,600–5,700), under the 6,300 floor (VOI-7). Unless the template names what fills the gap, drafters will fill it with restatement.
3. **Fixed blocks.** As written, the Stage Notes restate the body, the Standard Check is portable, and the Quick Reference lacks the tables he'll come back for (VOI-8, 18, 19). Across 28 modules that adds up to thousands of words of skimmable boilerplate.
4. **Reading a module on its own.** Unglossed house nouns make a module hard to read out of order (VOI-4), and the spec requires modules to be read in order *and* stand alone.

**What the sample can't show.** The worked example (~700 words) and the extras (~650) make up about a fifth of every module. Add both to v2 before locking; rules R15, R16 and R18 give their shape.

## (a) Findings

### VOI-1 · Major · Clinical vocabulary in the playbook's own analysis
*Dimensions 3, 5*

**Evidence.**
- L42 "### What misdiagnosis costs"
- L54 "Diagnose once a month in the Operator Review (Module 13)."
- L100 "most expensive mistakes are misdiagnoses."
- L102 "Diagnose monthly; pull one lever at a time."

**Why it matters.** STYLE bans "diagnose" outright, and FRAMEWORKS naming principle 5 repeats the ban. The ban exists because the operator's own vocabulary leaks into how he talks to clients. A playbook that "diagnoses" constraints teaches him the verb. The build files use the word too (the Module 1 brief says "Diagnose monthly"; MAP's Module 14 entry says "link diagnosis" and "diagnose and repair links"), so drafters will copy it unless the rule explicitly covers business analysis.

**Rewrite.**
- L42 → "### What a misread costs"
- L54 → "Name the binding constraint once a month, in the Operator Review (Module 13)."
- L100 → "…most expensive mistakes are misreads."
- L102 → "Read it monthly; pull one lever at a time."
- Module 14's terms → "finding the broken link" and "find and repair links".

### VOI-2 · Major · The flagship model is over-certain, and the monthly check loses its reason
*Dimensions 5, 1*

**Evidence.**
- L20 "In most businesses it wanders. In this one it moves in a fixed order… always in the same sequence"
- L40 "The last position arrives when…"
- L44 "Most expensive mistakes in this niche are the right move made at the wrong position."

**Why it matters.** As the business grows, the constraint moves forward. It also slides back:
- a price raise can make conversations bind again
- a slow quarter on the channel hands it back to reach
- templated review frees minutes and hands it back as well

An operator taught "fixed order, always" will misread a slide-back as failure. The sample's own instruction to check monthly (L54) only makes sense if the constraint can move back, but the text never says so, so the instruction arrives without its reason. "In most businesses it wanders" is an unsupported comparison. And "most expensive mistakes" competes with the House Standard's own point that one overclaim is the costliest thing in this category.

**Rewrite (L20–24).**
> A constraint is whatever stops the next dollar of profit. In this business it moves in a predictable order, because each part feeds the next: no conversations without reach, no care to deliver until conversations turn into clients. Widen the narrowest pipe and the next one becomes the limit. So as you grow, the binding constraint moves forward through three positions:
>
> **reach → conversations → care minutes.**
>
> That's the **Constraint Sequence**. Knowing where you sit in it comes before any tactic, because the right move at one position is the wrong move at the next. It can also slide back: a price raise can hand the constraint back to conversations, and a quiet quarter on the channel can hand it back to reach. That's why you read it every month.

- L40 → "The third position arrives when…"
- L44 → "Outside the House Standard's lines, most expensive mistakes here are the right move made at the wrong position."

### VOI-3 · Major · The objection names two different "actually broken" links
*Dimensions 1, 4*

**Evidence.**
- L60 "It's actually a broken **Range** link (Module 14)"
- L72 "moved the conversation to the link that's actually broken: **Cause**."

**Why it matters.** The objection map in THESES assigns "adults can't change" to the Range link. The script makes two moves: it repairs Range with the honest answer, then follows the real objection to Cause. That is the Real-Objection Sort at work, and it is the most teachable move in the excerpt. As written, though, the reader gets two contradictory verdicts and no name for the move. Belief Chain debriefs will recur dozens of times across Modules 14–22, and each must name exactly one link per move.

**Rewrite.**
- L60 → "This objection sounds like a statement about bone. It's a **Range** objection (Module 14): he doesn't believe anything is left to move at his age, so every other part of your offer sounds like a trick."
- L72 → "Notice the two moves. The Honest Answer repaired Range: some things are set, some can move, and he can check which is which. Then you followed the objection behind the objection to **Cause**: his stall has a findable cause, and it's 'no measurement', not 'too old'. That's the **Real-Objection Sort** at work: the objection he voiced wasn't the one stopping him."

### VOI-8 · Major · The Standard Check is portable boilerplate
*Dimensions 3, 5*

**Evidence.**
- L91 "Trust is an input. Every tactic in this playbook either spends trust or earns it."
- L92 "Aim it at the situation (the wasted months, the guessing), never at the person."

Both bullets could be pasted unchanged into any of the 28 modules; only L90 touches this module. L90 also overreaches slightly: "which is why you can guarantee it". You can guarantee your inputs. His knowing also depends on his logging, which is what the Collectability Test exists to check.

**Why it matters.** STYLE defines this block as how *the module's tactics* sit against the House Standard. Generic principles repeated in every module become a block the reader learns to skip. Meanwhile, the specific places where this module's tactics could slip go unsaid: pressure when conversations bind, and fake scarcity when care minutes bind.

**Rewrite.**
- **The promise you control.** The End of Guessing is guaranteeable because it's built from your inputs: the plan, the weekly review, the week-6 read, the week-12 re-assessment. Guarantee those loudly. Never let it drift into a promise about his face (the line on structural claims).
- **Where each constraint tempts you.** When conversations bind, the shortcuts are pressure: a second ask after a clear no, a discount, a deadline that moves. The sequence answers with the Door Switch and real dates instead, and the stop rules still win.
- **Scarcity only when it's real.** When care minutes bind, the waiting list is true, so state it as fact ("this start: [6] of [8] seats taken"). Countdown timers and invented seat counts stay out at every stage.

### VOI-9 · Major · Options plus a default is never shown, and one real choice is left open
*Dimensions 1, 6*

**Evidence.**
- L30 "a free or token-priced fit conversation beats a paid assessment"
- L82 "Keep the first conversation free or token-priced."

No options-plus-default block appears anywhere in the sample. L30 also argues against itself: the next sentence says "A fee filters people out", which is an argument against the token fee it just allowed.

**Why it matters.** SPEC §3 makes options plus a default pick the decision style of the whole playbook, and this sample exists to lock the template. "Free or token-priced" hands the reader the choice with no pick and no switch trigger.

**Rewrite (this also locks the house format).**

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Free fit conversation | Reach binds; every eligible lead counts | More no-shows |
| Small credited fee (~$25–50) | No-shows are eating your selling hours | A little friction at booking |
| Paid Decision Assessment ($150–250, credited) | Conversations bind (the Door Switch) | Fewer conversations, which by then is the point |

> **Default: free.** A real fee filters people out, and filtering is the last thing you need while every eligible lead counts. Add the small credited fee only if no-shows start eating your selling hours.

### VOI-10 · Major · The objection unit stops before the decision
*Dimension 5 (too timid)*

**Evidence.**
- The script ends at L70 ("That's the gap. Not your age.") and the unit ends at L76 on what never to say.
- The Pushback Signal is described but not scripted (L74 "Reflect what he said, ask permission to challenge it, then challenge it…").

**Why it matters.** This is the House Standard's floor: a good-fit buyer who talks himself out of the right decision because you never asked was failed. Resolving an objection reopens the decision; it doesn't finish the sale. If the unit teaches the insight but not the return to the recommendation, drafters will copy that shape into every objection in Modules 18–22, and the playbook will under-sell by design. The permission ask and the challenge are the load-bearing lines the reader most needs word for word.

**Rewrite (append after L74).**
> **You:** "Fair. You've heard that from a lot of people. Can I push back on it for a second?"
> **Dan:** "Sure."
> **You:** "You've had [eight months] of mirror checks. What have they told you about what's moving?" *[Stop. Let him answer.]*

> **Then return to the decision.** Once the objection is resolved, go back to where you were: "Given that, does the plan we talked about still fit what you want?" If yes, restate your one recommendation and ask. If he gives a clear no, the ask ends for this conversation.

### VOI-11 · Major · The opening presents the upside case as the result of one question
*Dimensions 5, 1*

**Evidence.**
- L12 "A year later… The other has a waiting list for his next start date, a price that has gone up twice…"
- L14 "Neither is more talented. The second operator kept asking one question"
- L14 "This module gives you that question and the model behind it. … This is the module that tells you which one to pull, and when."

### VOI-15 · Minor · The misread table's last row breaks the table's logic

### VOI-16 · Minor · Terminology and capitalization drift, plus a leaked internal label
*Dimension 3*

**Evidence.**
- **One metric, four names:** "held conversations" (L28), "held calls" (L34), "sales conversations" (L20), "calls" (L36, L40).
- **Inconsistent capitals:** "**The End of Guessing**" in the cheat sheet (L108), but "the end of guessing" at L90 and L96. Also "the premium lane" (L40, L86) against FRAMEWORKS' "The Premium Lane".
- **A synonym for a registered framework:** "the honest map" (L64) and "a map he can check" (L72) stand in for the registered **Honest Answer** / **Outcome Map** (Module 3).
- **A leaked internal label:** "(hard line 2)" at L90. The playbook-facing House Standard calls these "Six lines we never cross" and never calls them "hard lines".

## (b) Voice rules to lock for drafting (20)

1. **Every recommendation carries its reason.** Give the mechanism in the same sentence or the next ("because…", "so…", "which means…"). *Check:* strike the reasoning. If the paragraph still stands as a list of instructions, it has drifted toward Source B; add the reason or cut the instruction.
2. **Paragraphs.** 2–5 sentences and 40–110 words; nothing over 130. One-sentence paragraphs only for a section's opening claim or a formula line, at most one per `##` section.
3. **Sentences.** Median 14–18 words. Every reasoning paragraph holds at least one sentence of 20+ words that carries a causal chain. At most one punchline of under 6 words per paragraph, and never ending two paragraphs in a row. Use colons for "here's what that means" turns; em dashes at most once per section.
4. **Budget for contrasts and refrains.** "Not X, but Y" / "isn't X. It's Y." at most once per ~700 words, reserved for the module's belief shift and objection re-routes. One refrain per module (e.g., "the next dollar"), used at most three times: to plant it, to apply it, and in the Quick Reference.
5. **Introduce frameworks in five beats.**
   - the problem in this niche
   - the mechanism
   - the framework itself: shown before it is named, bolded in Title Case at first use, with a formula line he can say aloud ("reach → conversations → care minutes")
   - a run on one composite case (in the section, 150–250 words, unless the module's worked example is built on it)
   - the operating rule: when, how often, and the default

   Never open a section with a framework's name. Never introduce two new frameworks in one paragraph.
6. **One format for options plus a default.** Wherever a real choice exists: a table (Option / Pick it when / What it costs), then "**Default:** X, because Y. Switch to Z when [ledger trigger]." Never leave "X or Y" undecided in running prose. Models with no real choice get a decision rule instead.
7. **Certainty matches the evidence.** Absolute words about how the business behaves ("always", "never", "fixed", "only") are reserved for House Standard lines, stop rules and the ledger's fixed rule thresholds; "only" in a plain condition ("only if no-shows bite") is fine. Models state the default pattern and name where it breaks ("It can also slide back…").
8. **Conviction comes from consequences, not adjectives.** No value superlatives ("worth more than any", "the single most", "can't buy any other way"). "Actually" and "honestly" at most once per section. Earn force with a mechanism or a chain of consequences ("…a refund, a dispute, and a screenshot").
9. **Optimism comes from the plan.** End positions and sections on what the right move makes possible, stated as a mechanism or a ledger range. Composite outcomes stay inside planning bands A–B unless labeled "the good case".
10. **No house noun before its gloss.** The first time a module uses any named offer, asset, rule or framework, the same sentence carries a 3–10-word gloss. Recaps of another module's framework get at most two sentences, then "(Module N)".
11. **Cross-references and parentheses.**
    - One format: "(Module N)", placed after the gloss.
    - At most one per ~600 words, except in Modules 1 and 28.
    - Never in headings. Never "as we'll see", "covered later" or "see above", and no bridges or previews at the end of a section: the next heading is the transition.
    - Parentheses carry pointers and ranges only; glosses go in the sentence.
12. **The niche swap test.** Every `##` section must fail a swap test: change the niche, and at least one reason, sign, example or cost stops being true. Each section carries at least one niche mechanism:
    - the under-18 share of the audience
    - the category's grift history
    - the bone question
    - slow, confounded evidence
    - photos and capture
    - the stalled Struggler
    - referral triggers
13. **Numbers.** Use LEDGER ranges with what moves them, in units he can act on ("12–20 minutes per client-week; 6–10 once templated"). Single numbers only for fixed process facts and the ledger's fixed rule thresholds, stated as rules ("the Door Switch fires above ~6–8 held conversations a week"). No rhetorical pairs of single numbers. Numbers inside composites are placeholders in brackets or ledger ranges.
14. **One vocabulary list.**
    - The clinical ban covers the playbook's own analysis too: no diagnose, diagnosis, misdiagnosis, treat, protocol (except Plateau Protocol), patient or healing. Use read, misread, name, check, find, trace.
    - One term per concept ("held conversations" is the metric; "call" is only the medium), and no synonyms for registered frameworks.
    - Capitalization follows a style sheet built from the FRAMEWORKS.md name column.
    - House Standard elements are cited by name, never by number.
    - US spelling, and one fixed style for punctuation around quotation marks.
15. **Script format.**
    - Blockquote with bold speaker labels.
    - The buyer's name comes from the fixed cast, labeled *(composite, state)* at first appearance.
    - At most 150 words per block, with load-bearing lines word for word.
    - Italic bracketed stage directions only for silences that matter (*[Stop. Let him answer.]*).
    - Brackets for method content and his personal numbers, describing in plain words what goes there. General outcome categories from the Outcome Map are written out, not bracketed.
16. **Every script gets a debrief, and every sales unit ends at the decision.** The debrief runs up to 100 words: the move, the mechanism, and exactly one framework or Belief Chain link per move. Then comes the scripted pushback variant, the line you never say, and either the return to the recommendation and ask, or the stop rule that ends it. Keep two run-in labels (**Why it works.** / **How to use it.**) and let the rest flow as prose.
17. **A fixed composite cast.** Define the cast once in the Intro: a name per buyer state plus two operator composites. Create the VOICE.md the briefs already cite. Modules never invent new names.
18. **Texture cadence, cases, and hard cases.**
    - No more than ~600 words of unbroken prose. Change texture every 400–600 words: a table, a case, a script, a checklist or a formula line.
    - Rough mix per module: ~60% reasoning prose, 10–15% tables and checklists, 15–20% cases and scripts, 10% or less fixed blocks.
    - One worked example per module (600–800 words): a composite with dated decision points, the default move, the read, the right move, and the outcome as a ledger range or placeholder.
    - One "when the signals disagree" hard case per module.
    - Checklists: 5–8 items, each a yes/no question he can answer from his own records, with the ledger threshold written into the item.
19. **Headings and bold.**
    - `## N. Title Case` for sections; `### Sentence-case headings that read as claims` for subsections ("Reach binds first"); objection headings are the buyer's words in quotes.
    - Each section's first sentence states its claim.
    - Parallel subsections carry parallel parts (mechanism → niche reason → signs → moves).

20. **Fixed blocks: fixed jobs, tighter budgets.**

    - **Standard Check, 100–160 words:** 2–4 bullets, each naming a tactic from this module, the House Standard element it touches, and what keeps it inside the line. No bullet that could move to another module unchanged.
    - **Quick Reference, 200–350 words:** one line, 3–5 takeaways, the module's one or two decision tables, the framework cheat sheet ("Use it to…", owned frameworks only), a "Leans on" line, and "Do this month" (three actions, replacing a separate action-steps block).

## 3. The Constraint Sequence

A constraint is whatever stops the next dollar of profit. In this business it moves in a predictable order, because each part feeds the next: no conversations without reach, no care to deliver until conversations turn into clients. Widen the narrowest pipe and the next one becomes the limit. So as you grow, the binding constraint moves forward through three positions:

**reach → conversations → care minutes.**

That's the **Constraint Sequence**. Knowing where you sit in it comes before any tactic, because the right move at one position is the wrong move at the next. It can also slide back: a price raise can hand the constraint back to conversations, and a quiet quarter on the channel can hand it back to reach. That's why you read it every month.

### Reach binds first

Early on, your problem is arithmetic: too few eligible adults (legal adults who could pay from monthly income without new credit) know you exist, so even a solid close rate produces a thin month. In this category that's harder than it looks. A large share of the most engaged viewers are under 18, and rating and comparison content pulls the youngest fastest, so a video can do big numbers and barely move your eligible pool. The signs: held conversations below the 3–6 a week the early months need, self-assessment completions that land mostly in the under-18 education lane or say "not yet", and an inbox you can clear before lunch.

When reach binds, every spare hour goes to the two things that create conversations: content adults find, and a personal reply to every adult who finishes your self-assessment. That reply is this stage's quiet advantage: answering within hours lifts how many leads book, and it's affordable only while volume is low. Keep the first conversation easy to say yes to.

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Free fit conversation | Reach binds; every eligible lead counts | More no-shows |
| Small credited fee (~$25–50) | No-shows are eating your selling hours | A little friction at booking |
| Paid Decision Assessment ($150–250, credited) | Conversations bind | Fewer conversations, which by then is the point |

**Default: free.** A real fee filters people out, and filtering is the last thing you need while every eligible lead counts.

### Then conversations bind

Somewhere past six to eight held conversations a week, the constraint moves. You have enough people; you don't have enough selling hours, or the wrong people are reaching your calendar. In this niche the wrong people have a recognizable shape: the man who wants a promise about bone you won't make, the one who could only pay on credit, the one whose jaw pain or snoring means the right answer is a referral. The signs: show rates sliding below about 70%, more than half your calls ending no-fit, and weeks where selling eats the hours you set aside for content.

Now the right moves flip. This is when the **Door Switch** earns its place (Module 6): a paid Decision Assessment screens for seriousness before you spend the hour, and turns a free hour into paid judgment. It's when warm buyers should be able to enroll without a call (Module 20). And it's when conversion becomes the number you watch.

### Then care minutes bind

The third position arrives when the Program, your 12-week flagship, is full: roughly 25–45 concurrent clients inside a 20–25-hour week, higher as review gets templated and the group call carries more of the load. Review is the product here (his weekly logs and check-ins, and at the measurement points his photos, taken the same way each time), and it doesn't scale by itself, which is why minutes run out before leads do. The signs: a waiting list, review turnaround creeping toward its limit, and weeks that run past 25 hours unless you pause booking.

From here, more leads and more calls only lengthen the waiting list. The levers that remain raise profit per operator hour. Price comes first: at capacity, a raise that costs you fewer buyers than its percentage flows almost entirely to profit, because the seats still fill. Then the back end: the Hold, a paid alumni room, earns with very few minutes, and Round Two, about twelve more weeks offered at the week-12 measurement, fills a seat without a selling hour. Then review, templated from 12–20 minutes per client-week to 6–10. Once that frees seats, the Premium Lane, a few high-touch seats priced to fixed deliverables, becomes worth opening. This is the good problem: it's where the $25–50k range lives, reached through price and leverage rather than more hours.

### What a misread costs

Outside the House Standard's lines, most expensive mistakes here are the right move made at the wrong position.

| The move | Right when… | Costly when… | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charging for the first conversation | Conversations bind | Reach binds | Fewer conversations while every one counts |
| Adding a platform | Reach binds *and* the editing budget can serve it | Care minutes bind | Thinner content, slower review, fewer renewals |
| Pushing for more leads | Reach binds | Care minutes bind | A waiting list you can't serve, and worse care for the clients you have |
| Raising price | Conversations or care minutes bind, with proof | Reach binds and nobody knows you yet | The few eligible adults you have walk away |
| Templating review | Care minutes bind, after ~20 clients have taught you what a good review says | Early, before you've learned it | Your weakest early reviews frozen into the product |

One move is wrong at every position: discounting to close. Buyers learn to wait, and in a category where "70% off, today only" is a grift tell, a discount makes you look like what you're positioned against. A founding price is different: a real price with a stated end.

**When the signals disagree.** Say you're at [~$9–12k] a month on a high price with fewer than ~50 eligible leads a month: Growing on revenue, Early on volume. Go by the constraint. If your calendar has open slots and your inbox clears by lunch, reach binds, and a paid assessment would cost you the few conversations you have.

Name the binding constraint once a month, in the Operator Review (Module 13): a week holds too few conversations to read, and a quarter lets a misread run too long. Ask one question, *which of the three is stopping the next dollar?*, then pull the one lever that answers it and leave the others alone.
