# Critique — buyer

## Verdict
On paper this is the most honest foundation in its category. It has hard lines that don't move, denominators, binding stop rules, no surgery anchors, no affiliate money (D33), public prices and a refusal policy. Each buyer finds something to like: Jordan gets adult-specific stall content and the promise of a straight verdict; Sam gets public prices, a standards page and discreet billing; Alex gets anti-grift framing and a named face accountable for every claim. From the buyer's side, though, the design protects the operator's credibility and capacity better than it answers the buyer's actual question. Three problems stand out. First, no locked document says what an adult can realistically change, in a niche literally named "bone growth": D42 orders it, but nothing delivers it. Second, P9, P7 and adherence-conditional guarantees together make every non-result the client's fault, which is exactly how Alex was burned. Third, the risk reversal a burned buyer needs has not been decided. Jordan would be drawn in, then risks being flagged as "appearance-concerned" by a brand whose marketing names that concern forcefully, and faces installments of about 13–28% of his take-home pay. Sam would be routed into a group cohort with a call-gated premium layer and no photo or data policy. Alex would see the $150–250 paid assessment, closer-style stakes questions, seat-cap copy, decoys and value stacks as the grift he already paid for. He buys only with a collectable guarantee, the right to stop future payments, a published non-responder rate and a plain public answer on adult bone change. Most fixes are additions: an Outcome Map, a three-verdict stall model, a locked guarantee, a privacy rule, a burned-buyer path and an optimizer lane. The rest remove permissions that fail the design's own Informed-Client Test: decoys, value stacks, public commitments, appearance future-pacing and sunk-spend anchors.

## Findings

### BUY-1 [critical] D42, P9, P12, P2, D41, B13, Master thesis (with SPEC §6 niche label)
**Issue:** The foundation never says what an adult buyer can realistically get.
- D42 orders 'name what habits and measurement can change and what they can't', but no thesis, decision or ledger row does it.
- P12's 'canon of calibrated core claims' contains no claims.
- P9's 'real, but bounded' never states the bound.

Meanwhile the niche is literally 'bone growth' coaching. B13 keeps the category's top keyword for search, and D42 forbids claiming adult bone remodeling. 'It's genetics' is treated both as a status-quo belief to move (P2) and as an enemy idea (D41), even though for adult bone the brand concedes it.

Buyer reactions:
- Jordan's one real question ('will anything change at 24, after 14 months of nothing?') goes unanswered.
- Sam cannot judge value without an outcome inventory.
- Alex found the brand by searching the bone keyword. Keyword in, 'measurement and habits' out, reads as the same bait-and-switch that sold him a jaw device.

**Fix:** Lock an Outcome Map in the foundation as a D-row plus a template every module carries. It is claims architecture, not technique. Three columns:
- What adults can change and measure (habits, posture and breathing habits, presentation).
- What is debated (visible effects, each with an evidence tier).
- What the brand never claims for adults (bone remodeling, per D42).

Then:
- Make the adult-bone answer canon claim #1 under P12, publish it on the Verify page, and give the bone keyword a dedicated 'honest answer' asset that answers before it pitches.
- Re-scope D41's enemy to the overgeneralization 'nothing you do matters', not the view that adult bone is largely set.
- The operator's public descriptor names what he delivers (assessment, plan, review, measurement), never 'bone growth'.

### BUY-2 [critical] P9, P7, P16, B10 (vs REGISTRY 'Four Hidden Variables of a Stall'; GAPS P-T3)
**Issue:** Together these form a closed blame loop:
- P9 re-attributes the stall to 'my approach was never measured or reviewed'. It drops the 'individual factors' variable that the registry's own Four Hidden Variables includes.
- P7's 14-day promise ('baseline set, X days logged, first review done') is mostly a promise about the buyer's own effort.
- P16 and B10 make guarantees conditional on adherence.
- No locked document handles plateaus or non-responders, although GAPS P-T3 is a P1 gap.

Net effect: any non-result becomes the client's fault. Jordan hears that 14 months were wasted because he didn't measure. Alex's course came with a tracking sheet and his device had an app; he hears the exact alibi the gurus used ('you weren't consistent'). Applied to every Struggler, 'your stall is fixable' also drifts toward HL2's universal-claim line.

**Fix:** Replace P9 with a three-verdict stall model: unmeasured, misdirected, or the lever doesn't move for this person. Make 'stop, this isn't moving for you' a legitimate outcome of the assessment and of the week-6 and week-12 reviews.

Rewrite P7 so most of the 14-day promise is the operator's:
- a personal written plan;
- reviews returned inside a stated window;
- the pre-agreed measure that would tell both parties to stop.

Add a Non-Response Protocol thesis covering what is said at weeks 6 and 12 when nothing measurable moved, plus the exit and partial-refund path. Publish the non-responder share inside every outcome range.

### BUY-3 [critical] B10, P16, B9; LEDGER B (no guarantee or fit-window row); DECISIONS (no D-row); GAPS B12, P-C8
**Issue:** Risk reversal, the deciding factor for a burned buyer, has not been decided. B10 says only that guarantees cover 'process and controllables'.

The research default is close to uncollectable:
- Fit-window refund minus the assessment fee.
- After at least 10 of 12 check-ins, '6 more weeks free or a partial refund' if habit or measurement markers haven't improved. If habit markers count, the qualifying adherence itself largely satisfies them.
- 'More of what didn't work' is no remedy to Alex.

B9's plan rule (all installments due before delivery ends) leaves all risk after the fit window with the buyer. The dossier's Commit Ritual also closes the fit window by having the client restate his reasons, which is consistency pressure timed to the refund deadline.

**Fix:** Add a D-row and LEDGER rows for:
- a statute-plus fit window (e.g., 14–21 days, full refund of the program fee, claimed through a short conversation);
- an assessment fee refundable if the written plan wasn't useful;
- cancel-forward installments (a client who exits after the fit window stops future payments);
- a service guarantee on the operator's own inputs (missed review windows earn credit);
- a non-response clause with markers fixed in advance and a cash partial refund, not extra weeks.

Run a Collectability Test (could a burned client who did the work actually collect?) and publish the claim rate. Add a rule that consistency devices are never scheduled at, or used against, refund or cancellation rights.

### BUY-4 [major] B4, B5, D18, D29, P13, D32; LEDGER B paid assessment $150–250 with 14–30-day credit
**Issue:** From the buyer's side, the paid assessment looks like the category's grift format. A $150–250 review of your history and photos by an uncredentialed stranger is a paid face audit, and 'fee credited if you enroll within 14–30 days' is the tripwire-and-expiry funnel Alex has already seen.

The assessment's dual role is also undisclosed at purchase. It is the sales conversation (stakes segment, one recommendation, price, ask, decision date), yet D32's research-versus-sales labeling is never applied to it. B5 condemns free calls as 'disguised pitches', while the paid version is a pitch the buyer pays for.

Jordan would gladly pay for a straight verdict. He would feel conned if the last 15 minutes of what he bought turned into a close.

**Fix:** On the purchase page:
- Disclose that the session may end with a recommendation for a paid program.
- Once data exists, publish the share of assessments ending in 'DIY plan', 'refer out', 'not now' and 'cohort'.

In delivery:
- Deliver the written plan whatever he decides, usable without buying anything.
- Cap the offer segment (e.g., the last 10 minutes at most, and only if fit is found).
- Make the fee refundable if the plan isn't useful.
- Publish a labeled-composite sample deliverable.
- Lead with history and habits; photos stay optional until enrollment.
- Mention the credit expiry once, in writing, and never in follow-ups.

### BUY-5 [major] Master thesis, B1, B4/B5 vs B12; D18, D26; P5; LEDGER B founding price
**Issue:** The early stage sells 'calibrated judgment' before B12 admits that the stall taxonomy, check-in instrument and capture protocol exist.

B1 says sales minutes bind first 'early'. Before an audience exists, trust and demand bind and minutes are slack. B5's hours-per-enrollment arithmetic doesn't apply yet, and a paid-first door taxes exactly the first and most skeptical buyers.

P5 overreaches. The evidence shows lay guides can deliver a validated protocol well; it does not show that an uncredentialed person's plan is valid, which is precisely Alex's question.

The founding price has no range (LEDGER B: 'honest founding price').

**Fix:** Stage-gate the door:
- Early default: free, labeled founding fit conversations (already kept as a D18 option) or a low, fully refundable founding assessment.
- Switch to the paid assessment when a numeric trigger shows sales minutes binding (e.g., assessment requests above N per week).

Also:
- Ban 'calibrated' language until the operator has a stated number of reviewed cases.
- Restate P5 as 'presence delivers the plan; it doesn't validate it', and pair it with the Outcome Map.
- Ledger a founding price range and a disclosure line: 'you're among my first N clients; here is what that means for you'.

### BUY-6 [major] B6; STANDARD operational rule 'Screen before charge'; LEDGER C screen-positive 12–28%; D18; D50; H6
**Issue:** The screen fails on capacity, accuracy and feel.

- Capacity: if the screen runs in the free door, as D18 specifies, maturity volume is about 470–1,400 leads a month (business SYNTHESIS T14, behind LEDGER C's views). At 30–70% adults and 12–28% flagged, that is about 17–275 flags a month. If 'a flag triggers a conversation' at 15–20 minutes each, that costs about 4–90 hours against 87–108 operator hours.
- Accuracy: with positive predictive value of about one-third to three-quarters, many flags are false positives among core buyers. Jordan's 14 months of daily practice reads as 'preoccupation'; Alex's serial purchases read as a red flag.
- Feel: Sam is asked mental-health-style questions before getting any value. An automated 'we suggest talking to a professional' is a label whatever the intent.
- Loop: the forceful desire-naming the Standard permits (N5, N9, P10) raises the very concern the screen flags.
- Gaming: a free quiz is easiest to game for the people most at risk.

**Fix:** Two tiers.
- Free door: an automated fit check worded around impairment and distress (missing work or social plans, hours per day, feeling unable to stop), not around concern. Results never label. The outcome offers resources plus an opt-in human conversation.
- Paid step: the decisive human judgment happens here.

Rule: a flag is not a decline. Also add:
- a one-line 'why we ask';
- a country-aware referral directory with crisis routing for acute distress;
- a LEDGER row for screen-conversation minutes;
- a buyer-facing name ('fit check', not 'screen').

### BUY-7 [major] B2, D49, D29/D30, P16; LEDGER B Private layer
**Issue:** Sam wants efficient, private expertise. D49 routes the Optimizer into a Private layer that:
- requires the group cohort (D30; LEDGER 'on top of cohort');
- requires a call (D29);
- carries deliberate friction at the premium gate (P16).

B2 sells Private on 'privacy' while structurally placing him in a group. At about 1–1.5 one-to-one hours a month (business SYNTHESIS T4), the layer prices at roughly $550–1,300 per one-to-one hour, and its speed promise has to fit a 20–25-hour week. The One-Recommendation Rule grates on an expert buyer; the dossier's own C10 says it breaks for experts. Rolling monthly starts can leave him waiting weeks.

**Fix:** Give Sam a real lane:
- A Private variant with every group surface optional (recorded stage calls, async review, start any week), or a one-off async priority review (full lever map, fixed turnaround, one follow-up) as the optimizer's entry product.
- A fast lane: skip the free quiz, embed the fit check, and hold one call that is both assessment and enrollment.
- Published response-time commitments sized to the operator's actual working windows.
- All tiers shown side by side with the recommendation, never in place of the choice.

### BUY-8 [major] Missing H-rule (THESES §5); B10 (billing only); P18; E16; SPEC legal flag
**Issue:** There is no privacy or data architecture. Yet the business collects face photos and answers about sleep, jaw pain and appearance concern, and runs on freelancers with access to its tools.

- Sam's first question has no answer: who sees my photos, where are they stored, can I delete them, will they ever appear in your content?
- Nothing stops cohort members sharing face photos in group spaces (comparison risk, HL4).
- Jordan's stigma extends to inbox sender names, not just billing descriptors.
- The screen answers are plausibly consumer health data. Washington's My Health My Data Act, for example, has no size threshold and allows private suits. EU and UK answers are special-category data. Any tool that extracts facial measurements may touch biometric law.

**Fix:** Add H7 Privacy:
- Photos are coach-only: never in group spaces, never visible to freelancers.
- Cohort participation can be pseudonymous and camera-optional.
- Screen and health answers stay out of ESP tags, pixels and freelancer-visible tools, with a short retention period.
- Deletion on request.
- Separate, revocable consent for any marketing or dataset use.
- Discreet sender name and billing.

Add one legal flag to verify before launch: health-data consent laws, GDPR special-category data, and biometric laws if facial measurements are extracted.

### BUY-9 [major] D49, D35/H2, STANDARD N2, D29, E7/E8; GAPS P-S4; REGISTRY Motivation Segments and Maturity States ('Skeptical Evaluator')
**Issue:** The burned buyer is not a segment. D49 keeps the Struggler and the Optimizer, while the REGISTRY still carries skeptic-curious buyers and a Skeptical Evaluator state that no decision routes or calibrates for. This matters because the master thesis says grift fatigue defines the market.

Firmness is uniform, and the permitted closing moves are scripts Alex has already heard from closers:
- 'What does another year cost you?'
- 'Is it the money, the timing, whether it works, or someone you need to ask?'
- Challenging 'stories the buyer tells himself'.

D29's recurring live enrollment sessions resemble the free-webinar-then-pitch format. E7's 60-day revenue metric pushes early asks, and the slow verifier who reads for months and checks the brand on search, without clicking, gets sunset or re-permissioned instead of served.

**Fix:** Add a Burned Struggler state (a sub-state, not a 180 from the sources):
- A door question: 'what have you already bought or tried?'
- A verification-first path: Verify page, sample deliverable, outcome data, and guarantee terms up front.

Calibrate force by segment. For burned buyers, answer 'I need to think about it' with a verification kit and a decision date he chooses; no stakes questioning and no objection sort.

Title enrollment sessions for what they are ('cohort walkthrough + Q&A, price inside'). Give tagged skeptics a slow email lane with a 180-day revenue view.

### BUY-10 [major] B8 (honest anchors); STANDARD HL5
**Issue:** B8 whitelists 'his own sunk spend (devices, apps, courses, months)' as an honest price anchor. For Alex this is the recovery-scam pattern: 'you've already lost $X, so this $2k is reasonable.' It reminds him that the category burned him and asks him to spend again on the same logic. Repeated appearance-fix purchasing can itself signal vulnerability, so this anchor points force at exactly the buyers HL5 protects. It also fails the Informed-Client Test: few buyers would endorse being priced against their own losses.

**Fix:** Remove sunk spend from B8's anchor list. Use prior spend only diagnostically (what was tried and what it taught), never in the same breath as the price. Treat a history of serial purchases as a signal for extra care: no payment plan, and a cooling-off gap between assessment and enrollment.

### BUY-11 [major] STANDARD notch 8 ('tiering/decoys', 'value stacking'); persuasion SYNTHESIS §0 row 'Tiers and decoys'
**Issue:** The Standard permits decoys and value stacking. A decoy exists only to steer the buyer through a mechanism he isn't meant to notice, so it fails the Standard's own governing test. The research synthesis already concluded 'no decoys' (the attraction effect is fragile), but no D-row carries that conclusion. Value stacks ('$4,997 value, yours for $1,997') are the grift signature to Alex and transparent to Sam.

**Fix:** Add a D-row that removes decoys. Redefine value stacking as itemizing real delivery units (reviews, calls, reassessments, turnaround) with no invented 'total value' figure. Tiers must differ by real service intensity (P5).

### BUY-12 [major] STANDARD notch 3 (future-pacing), notch 5; D39 ('confidence in photos' framed boldly); P10
**Issue:** Two permissions slip appearance outcomes past the claim ladder:
- Future-pacing an appearance goal is fantasy induction. It inflates expectations, which B10 treats as a cost ('calibration is margin'), and the design's own research (P2 dossier finding 18) found that positive fantasies reduce effort.
- 'Confidence in photos' is not purely experiential. It depends on the photos changing, so framing it boldly is a visible-change claim made without the context stack.

Naming the desire to be taken seriously is fine. Implying the jaw is why he isn't taken seriously reinforces appearance-contingent self-worth, which raises the very concern the screen flags.

**Fix:** Limit future-pacing to process and knowledge states ('by week 12 you'll know where you stand'). Move 'confidence in photos' under D39's context-stack rule. Add to P10: name the desire, never claim the face is its cause. Pair desire-naming with a line that puts appearance in perspective, in the same asset.

### BUY-13 [major] STANDARD notch 7 ('people who take this seriously', public commitments); P8 (control lexicon); P18; LEDGER D alliance
**Issue:** The identity and vocabulary rules misfire on the buyers they target:
- 'People who take this seriously' implies that Jordan (14 months) and Alex (two courses and a device) failed for lack of seriousness. That insults the most committed buyers.
- 'Public commitments' collide with P18's own finding that buying help for your jaw is kept secret.
- P8 bans 'support' outright, leaving no language for Jordan's real unmet need: to be heard after months of private rumination. Only P5 touches the alliance evidence (LEDGER D, r≈.3); no thesis covers being heard or agreeing on goals.
- 'Protocol' is the grift's own word ('bone-smashing protocol') and sounds clinical.
- A buyer who learned there is a 'Control Lexicon' designed to disguise help-seeking would feel handled.

**Fix:** Base identity on how people decide, not on effort: 'people who want a straight answer', 'people who measure before they believe'.
- Commitments stay private or inside the closed cohort, and are opt-in.
- Narrow P8's ban to therapeutic and medical framing ('healing', 'treatment'), and allow plain validation ('that's frustrating, and it makes sense').
- Replace 'protocol' with 'plan' or 'block'.
- Promote an alliance thesis: he feels heard and the work matches his goals, checked after every call.

### BUY-14 [major] P4 (show the work); LEDGER A (6–10 review minutes per client-week; 65–85% operating margin); persuasion SYNTHESIS open question 4
**Issue:** P4 treats as settled something the research listed as an open question: does showing review minutes raise conversion, or make the service look like labor?

- The honest math is about 72–120 minutes of individual review across 12 weeks for $1.5–2.2k, or roughly $750–1,800 per individual hour before group calls. Alex will do that division.
- 'Where the fee goes', published without the 65–85% operating margin, is selective disclosure.
- 'N seats' copy reads as fake scarcity however true it is, because every grifter's copy says the same.

**Fix:** Downgrade P4 to a hypothesis with a test plan.
- Lead with the deliverable (a sample written review, the turnaround time, the number of reviews and reassessments), not with minutes.
- Any fee breakdown includes the operator's pay and profit.
- State seat caps as plain facts with a public fill history that includes under-filled cohorts. Never write 'X spots left'.

### BUY-15 [major] P11, P5, P17, B11 (no null-result contingency)
**Issue:** The costly signals (refusals, stated limits, ranges, watch-me-think) prove sincerity, not efficacy, and Alex's question is 'does it work?' An honest operator selling something that doesn't move the needle still costs him $2k. The design also assumes its own dataset will be commercially favorable: P17 and B11 build the proof engine and the moat on it, with no plan for honest visible-change ranges that come back small.

**Fix:** Add signals that bear on efficacy:
- Commit in advance to publishing outcome ranges on a fixed schedule whatever they show, non-responders and plateau cases included.
- Publish a measurement protocol a skeptic could replicate.

Write the null-result plan now. The offer's core value must stay worth buying even if visible-change ranges are small: a verdict that stops wasted money and months, habit and presentation levers, and measurements the client owns.

### BUY-16 [major] LEDGER G ('a ~$2.5k program ≈ 4–6% of gross annual pay'); B7 cash ceiling; B9 and LEDGER B plan rules
**Issue:** Affordability uses the wrong denominator, against the design's own Denominator Discipline. Jordan grosses about $45k, roughly $3.0–3.2k a month after tax. The $1.5–2.2k cohort plus a 10–15% plan premium, split into 3–4 installments that must finish before delivery ends, comes to about $410–840 a month. That is roughly 13–28% of his take-home for three months. LEDGER B records that plans lift sales mainly among weak-credit buyers, yet the only safeguard is one affordability question.

**Fix:** Ledger affordability in monthly take-home and discretionary terms. Script the affordability question: 'comfortable from your monthly income, without new credit or buy-now-pay-later?' Add cancel-forward installments, and a shorter or lower-intensity block for buyers at the edge. No plan when the answer signals borrowing.

### BUY-17 [minor] E16 ('every CTA routes to one self-assessment'; results by emailed link) vs D18/D20 (direct enrollment; priced offers in decision-stage content)
**Issue:** The door has two gaps:
- It never says where the age fork and fit check sit for a warm buyer who clicks straight to an offer page. Either Sam is forced back through the quiz, or the screen is bypassed.
- Quiz-then-results-by-email is the most recognizable funnel pattern. Sam resents giving an email and health-adjacent answers before getting any value; Alex reads it as lead capture.

**Fix:** Embed a 3-question fit check (age, red flags, impairment) in checkout for direct enrollers, and skip it for anyone who already passed the self-assessment. Show a meaningful result on the page, and email the full version with the send frequency and one-click exit stated.

### BUY-18 [minor] B11 ('Continuity's real product is the outcome dataset')
**Issue:** A client who read B11 would learn he pays $39–79 a month so the operator can build a marketing moat. That fails the Informed-Client Test. Alex, who reads terms, will find the data-use clause and assume the worst.

**Fix:** Reframe it: for the client, continuity answers 'did it hold?' through periodic review. The dataset is a by-product used only with separate, revocable consent, and aggregate findings are shared back with the alumni who produced them.

### BUY-19 [minor] Internal names in THESES/REGISTRY: 'Sellable adults', 'Sellable-Adult Yield', 'Control Lexicon', 'Defense Meter', 'Down-Path'
**Issue:** The Informed-Client Test covers 'exactly what you did and why', which includes what you call the buyer. These names fail the design's own Hostile-Screenshot Test ('coach calls his audience sellable adults'), and they will leak through freelancers, dashboards and screen-shares.

**Fix:** Add a glass-box naming rule and rename before drafting, e.g., Adult Fit Rate, Plain-Language Rule, Pushback Signal, Starter Path.

### BUY-20 [minor] B7 ('raise on announced dates only when utilization stays high and outcome milestones are met')
**Issue:** Read literally, a price rise is announced first and gated second. If the gate then fails, the operator must break either his own gating rule or a public date (HL3). Alex checks whether announced price rises actually happened.

**Fix:** Fix the sequence: pass the gate, then announce, then raise on the announced date. Never announce a conditional rise.

### BUY-21 [minor] LEDGER B coaching market rate ~$230–300/h (ICF); B8 anchors; business SYNTHESIS open question 6
**Issue:** The anchor uses the wrong reference class. ICF rates describe executive and life coaching. Jordan and Alex compare a $1.5–2.2k, 12-week program with online fitness coaching and $4-a-week apps. The synthesis flagged consumer coaching price bands as unverified, yet the ledger marks the ICF rate EV(M) and B8 uses it as an honest anchor.

**Fix:** Add a verified row for consumer online-coaching monthly prices, and compare the cohort's weekly price against it. Keep the ICF rate internal and out of buyer-facing anchors.

### BUY-22 [minor] H3 / D12 (face-as-proof)
**Issue:** H3 handles explicit self-proof and appearance attacks, but not the implicit halo. On camera in a face niche, a good-looking operator is read as proof whatever he says, and staying silent lets that implied claim stand. Alex will ask outright.

**Fix:** Publish a 'what my face does and doesn't prove' line on the About/Verify page. Ban thumbnails of the operator's profile or jaw angle that work as aspirational before-and-afters.

### BUY-23 [minor] B6 / P11 (declines and refusals published as costly signals)
**Issue:** Publishing declines risks turning screened-out, possibly vulnerable people into marketing. Jordan would not want to be the '31 people we turned away' statistic, and a decline framed around mental health would be a label broadcast in public.

**Fix:** Publish the policy and aggregate fit-decline counts only. Never publish clinical reasons, individual stories or screen-flag counts. Test every refusal post against one question: would the declined person endorse being counted here?

## Proposed theses
- (Replaces P9) A stall has three honest explanations: it was never measured, it was measured but misdirected, or the lever does not move for this person. The assessment earns its fee only if it is willing to return the third verdict and tell the buyer to stop.
- (Sharpens D42 and P12) Bounded agency is a published map, not a slogan. It lists what adults can change, what is debated, and what the brand never claims for adults, each with its evidence tier. The brand's answer to 'can adults grow bone?' is canon claim number one and is repeated wherever the category keyword brings people in.
- (Closes B10) For a buyer who has been burned, risk reversal is the proof. A guarantee counts as a trust signal only if a client who did the work could realistically collect it, and the brand publishes how often clients do.
- (Amends P7) Promise what he can verify in 14 days, and make most of the promise yours: a personal written plan, reviews returned inside a stated window, and a pre-agreed measure that would tell you both to stop.
- (New) Privacy is part of the product. Photos are seen only by the coach, participation can be pseudonymous, billing and sender names are discreet, and any client can delete his data, because most buyers intend to keep this purchase secret.
- (Amends P10) Name the desire, never the cause. The operator may say plainly that the buyer wants to be taken seriously, but never that his face is the reason he isn't.
- (New) Every honest 'no' about structure must travel with an honest 'yes' about something he can move, or calibration curdles into the fatalism the brand exists to answer.
- (Amends P11) Costly signals prove sincerity, not efficacy. Efficacy is shown by committing in advance to publish outcome ranges whatever they show, non-responders and plateau cases included, and by designing the offer to stay worth buying if those ranges turn out small.
- (Amends P4) Show the deliverable before the arithmetic. A sample plan and a stated turnaround answer 'what do I get?'; minutes-per-client math is a hypothesis to test, and any account of where the fee goes includes the operator's own share.
- (New, answers GAPS P-S4) Firmness is calibrated to the buyer's history. The more he has been burned, the more his 'I need to think about it' is due diligence to be supported with verification tools, not an objection to be surfaced.
- (Amends B6) In an appearance business, concern is the market and impairment is the red flag. The screen is worded around impairment and distress, automated where volume is high, handled by a human where money changes hands, and never delivered as a label.
- (Amends B1 for the Early stage) Before an audience exists, trust and demand bind before minutes do. The founding door therefore maximizes trust and learning per conversation, and the paid assessment becomes the default only once sales minutes measurably bind.
- (Amends B7 and B9) Affordability is measured in monthly take-home pay, not annual gross, and a payment plan is safe only if the buyer can stop future installments when he stops receiving the service.
- (Amends B11) Continuity is sold on the client's own question, 'did it hold?'. The dataset is a consented by-product that is shared back with the people who produced it.
- (New) Every internal framework name must pass the Hostile-Screenshot Test, because the Informed-Client Test covers what you call the buyer, not only what you say to him.
