# 4 · Offer Architecture
*Part II — The Offer System*

**The shift:** from *"More offers mean more revenue, or one good offer should do everything"* to *"One flagship, two buffers, one net. Every rung has one job, switches on by stage, and the net is as honest as the flagship."*

At month [10], two operators with similar channels sit down with their numbers. Reid *(composite operator)* sells seven things: a [$19] jaw guide, a [$29]-a-month chat group, a [$79] face audit, a self-paced course, monthly coaching, one-to-one calls, and an affiliate link to a [device]. His revenue is [~$6–7k] a month and his profit is thin. His weeks go to supporting seven products and moderating rating talk in the chat, and buyers keep asking him which package is right for them.

Cole *(composite operator)* sells four rungs, and each has one job. At his door, a free fit conversation or a paid, faster assessment turns interest into a decision. The Program, his 12-week flagship, carries the profit and the proof. Round Two and the Hold, a maintenance block and a measurement subscription, carry graduates into the months when outcomes show. A free, sequenced Starter Path catches everyone who shouldn't buy yet. Every call ends in one recommendation from that short list, and his week fits in about 20 hours.

Reid's instinct was that more offers make more money. In this category each extra offer competes with free information, costs minutes you don't have, and blurs the one recommendation a burned buyer came for. This module builds Cole's kind of ladder: one flagship, two buffers, and one net, with each rung switched on when its stage arrives.

---

## 1. Design From the Target Down

Your ladder comes out of arithmetic: the profit you want, the price your core buyer can carry, and the care hours in your week. Start at $25k of profit a month, the low end of the target, which takes roughly $29–38k of cash collected at a 65–85% margin. Price can't carry that alone, because the core container stays under the cash ceiling, about 1–1.25 months of the core buyer's take-home, or roughly $2.8–3.5k for a buyer aged 20–24 (Module 7). So the target fixes a number of buyers: about 8–9 Program enrollments a month at about $3.1k, plus what graduates buy after week 12.

Then that number meets your week. With about 9–10.5 care hours, a cohort with weekly review holds roughly 25–30 concurrent clients in Growing, about 8–10 new enrollments a month, so the flagship at the ceiling just fits. One-to-one doesn't fit at all. At 45–75 minutes per client-week it earns roughly $100–330 per delivery hour, and 12–14 delivery hours a week cap revenue around $5–20k a month. A cohort earns roughly $800 per care hour at proof prices, and at equal weekly contact the evidence gives one-to-one no edge in results. So the flagship is a group program built on review, and one-to-one survives only as a premium seat.

One offer still can't do everything, because buyers reach you in three conditions the flagship can't serve alone. Some haven't decided, and after being burned, most want a human step before they pay anyone. Some have just finished 12 weeks, with habits not yet automatic and their outcomes still ahead. Some shouldn't buy now: the man who can't pay without borrowing, the one who isn't a fit, the one whose busy season runs until [June]. Serve all three with the flagship alone and you spend free hours on the undecided, lose graduates just before their proof appears, and have nothing honest to hand the rest.

**Work down from the target: profit sets the cash, the cash ceiling sets how many buyers you need, and your care hours set the format that can serve them.**

The opposite instinct, more offers for more revenue, fails for reasons this category sharpens. Information here is priced near zero by e-books, free videos, and rating apps, so a cheap product competes with free and mostly loses. Every extra offer needs a page, a checkout that runs the age and affordability checks, support minutes, and room in your content. It also hands one more choice to a buyer who came to you because he's tired of choosing, and a menu of packages sounds like the upsell pattern he has learned to distrust. Here is what each shape breaks at the target:

| Shape | What breaks first | Why it breaks here |
|---|---|---|
| One-to-one only | Revenue, near ~$5–20k a month | Each client takes 45–75 minutes a week, with no better results to show for it |
| One offer for everyone | Graduates drift at week 12, and "can't afford" gets a pushed plan or nothing | A plan pushed after "I can't afford it" crosses the line on vulnerability |
| A menu of offers | Your selling minutes and the one recommendation | Cheap products compete with free, and a menu reads as an upsell |
| One flagship, with one rung per condition | Nothing, while each rung keeps its job | The default |

The $25k configuration shows the shape in money. The Program brings in about $26k of roughly $31.5k in revenue. The rest arrives in small lines: about $1.5k from Round Two, $1.2k from the Hold, $1.7k from one Private seat, the premium one-to-one tier, sold about once a quarter, and $1k in assessment fees from buyers who didn't enroll. Each small line exists because it does a job the flagship can't do.

No free tier sits underneath. A free community is off this ladder for good, so every rung earns its place with money or with a job that the House Standard, the playbook's rules for persuasion and proof (Intro), requires of you. The net is free and still earns its place, because every no needs somewhere honest to go. The rule fits in four words: one rung, one job.

## 2. Judgment Is the Product, and Reasons to Pay

What a buyer pays you for is judgment applied to his own record, so every tier on your ladder should differ by how much of it he gets and how fast. Dan *(composite, Struggler)* already has information: about eight months of videos, a routine, and [a jaw device] in a drawer. Rating apps will sell him an analysis of his face for pennies. What nobody has given him is someone who reads his log every week and tells him what to change, what to stop buying, and when a lever isn't moving.

That's Judgment Is the Product, the rule that what he pays for is judgment applied to him, and a cheap app can't copy it, because it's tied to a person who reviews him every week. The evidence, drawn mostly from other behavior-change work, points the same way. At equal weekly contact, group, one-to-one, and guided formats do about as well, while contact that reaches out on a schedule roughly halves dropout and support he has to ask for adds little. So "message me anytime" isn't a deliverable, and the Review Rhythm is: weekly proactive review with a stated turnaround, plus one live group call, inside a 12-week container by default (Module 6).

### Every rung is a dose of four reasons

Judgment reaches him through four Reasons to Pay, the benefits he's actually buying: feedback, accountability, speed, and belonging. In the flagship they arrive as a written review every week, a written read at week 6, a re-assessment with matched captures at week 12, a reviewer expecting his log, a stated turnaround, and a cohort of men doing the same work. Each rung carries a different dose of the four, and the dose is what its price buys:

| Rung | Feedback | Accountability | Speed | Belonging |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Starter Path: free, eight weeks | His own self-checks | Templated check-ins | His own pace | Membership by practice, with no room |
| Decision Assessment: a paid written plan | One written plan | A re-check date | Priority tier: faster, with a recorded walkthrough | None |
| Program: the 12-week flagship | Weekly review, the week-6 read, the week-12 re-assessment | A reviewer expecting his log | A stated turnaround | The cohort and its live call |
| Program Async: no live call | The same weekly review | The same | The same | The cohort, without the call |
| Private: the premium seat | Review against fixed deliverables | The same | A faster turnaround | Optional; privacy instead |
| Round Two · the Hold: after graduation | Biweekly review · a quarterly written review | Re-captures on a calendar | A set calendar | Alumni status |

Read down the columns and a rule appears: tiers differ by review depth, speed, and live access, never by content volume or by a promised result. A tier with more content implies the cheaper one holds back part of the method, the gated-method pattern this category's buyers already distrust. A tier sold on better results fails the Informed-Client Test, the House Standard's check that a client at the low end of the range would still endorse what you did, because at equal contact the format doesn't change the result. What rises with price is attention and speed, and you can deliver both to every client and state them as points.

### Give away the why, and sell the judgment

The same logic draws the line between what you publish and what you sell. The why is free: the Honest Answer, your straight public answer to the bone question; how measurement works; what matched captures control for. The decision framework is free too, including self-checks against the Outcome Map, your public sort of what adults can change, what's debated, and what's never claimed (Module 3). What's paid is that framework applied to him: what his log says, which lever to pull first, whether to keep going. That's the Free/Paid Line:

**Give away the why and the decision framework; sell judgment applied to him, week after week.**

One question places any piece of content or feature: does answering it need his record? If it doesn't, publish it; if it does, it belongs behind a review. Publishing the framework costs you little, because the framework was never the scarce thing. It buys trust in a category built on gated methods ("the full routine is inside"), and it makes buyers better judges of everyone selling to them, which favors the operator who has judgment to sell.

## 3. One Flagship, Two Buffers, One Net

Every buyer who reaches you is in one of four places, and your ladder needs exactly one rung built for each. He's deciding, and after being burned he wants a human step before he pays. He's buying, and needs the weekly review that produces results and proof. He's finished, at week 12, with habits not yet automatic and the months when outcomes, proof, and referrals tend to appear still ahead. Or he shouldn't buy now, because he can't pay without borrowing, isn't a fit, or has a real reason to wait. Roughly 5–20% of paid-step applicants show at least one fit signal; most continue after a conversation, and the rest need somewhere to go.

The flagship is where profit and proof are made, and it leaks at both ends. At the front, undecided buyers spend its selling hours. At the back, graduates drift away just before the months their proof would appear. A buffer at each end catches the leak and earns a little on its own. Beneath everything, a net gives every no somewhere honest to go. That makes a stop rule, a fixed point where selling ends, easy to keep, and it lets a burned buyer say no out loud without losing you.

**One flagship earns, two buffers protect it, and one net catches everyone it shouldn't take.**

That's **One Flagship, Two Buffers, One Net**, the ladder's shape. Each slot holds one job:

| Slot | Rung | Its one job | Price | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flagship | The Program, starting as the founding group | Profit and proof | ~$1.2–1.5k founding → $1.5–2.2k → $2.4–3.2k | Module 6 |
| Front buffer | The door's human step: the free fit conversation, the Decision Assessment, its priority tier | A screened decision that spends no flagship minutes | Free · $150–250 · ~$350–600 | Module 5 |
| Back buffer | Round Two · the Hold | Graduates carried through the months their outcomes appear | ~$0.8–1.2k · $39–79/month | Module 10 |
| Net | The Starter Path; later the Self-Serve System, tools for DIY buyers | Somewhere honest for "not now", "not a fit", and "can't afford" | Free (tool $27–97) · $97–297 | This module |

This module places each slot's rungs, and each owner designs them. The flagship starts as the founding group, every founding client in one group with monthly entry, a start every month. The front buffer is a free fit conversation held open up to the Call Cap, a weekly limit of about 6–8 held conversations, with the overflow going to the Decision Assessment, a paid, credited written plan worth its fee even if he never buys (Module 5). Round Two is a maintenance block offered to graduates with measured momentum, and the Hold is a measurement subscription that opens into the alumni room once there are about 30 alumni (Module 10).

Tiers inside a slot aren't extra rungs. Private, a premium seat with fixed deliverables, and Program Async, the Program without its live call, are tiers of the flagship, and the priority tier is the assessment at speed; when each opens, and at what price, follows the Premium Lane, the path from the priority tier to Private seats that earn at least what Program seats earn per care hour (Module 13).

### One buyer, four slots, four hand-offs

Here is the shape carrying one buyer. Sam *(composite, Ambivalent)*, 22, an apprentice electrician, books a free fit conversation in [March]. He names his stake, another season of guessing about photos. You recommend the next start and ask, and he says "after the busy season". You ask what changes then, and his answer is concrete: overtime runs until [June], and until then he lacks [the minutes a day]. That's a constraint, so the no ends the ask, and his written recap hands over the Starter Path with his re-entry trigger in his own words: the first start after the busy season.

He logs through [April and May] on templated check-ins, with no pitch in between, and one check-in arrives on his date. He replies, and his Starter logs become week zero of his Program. At week 12 his record shows measured momentum, so he hears about Round Two and the Hold, and he takes the Hold at [$39–79] a month. Each slot did its one job and handed him on: the conversation reached a decision, the net kept him measuring, the flagship built his record, and the back buffer keeps it going.

Use the shape as a test whenever you add, reprice, or retire an offer, and once a quarter when you review your numbers. Write every offer into exactly one slot, with its job in one sentence. The default is one flagship, tiers inside a slot only when the cash ceiling or your minutes force them, and nothing on sale that has no slot. Keep each buffer a buffer. Assessment fees and Round Two stay small lines at $25k because their job is protecting the flagship, and pushing either one for revenue pulls buyers into the wrong rung.

**When the signals disagree.** Say a [template pack] you added in month [5] at [$37] sells [20–30] copies a month and brings in [$700–1,100]. Revenue says keep it, and the shape says it has no slot. It isn't the net, which is free and names its one tool once. It screens nobody at the front, holds no graduate at the back, and competes with the flagship for the Struggler who needs review more than templates. Go by the job, since the money is the smaller signal: count its support emails, the checkout checks it needs, the content slot it takes, and the buyers it leaves guessing alone.

The default is to fold it into the net as the Starter tool, the path's one low-cost item, named once in each handover and never promoted, and to honor what buyers already paid for. It earns a rung of its own only later, as the Self-Serve System, once about 20 graduates have taught you what belongs in it. One rung, one job.

## 4. The Rung Activation Schedule

Rungs switch on by stage, because each one needs something upstream to feed it and minutes in your week to run it, and a rung that opens early pays both costs with nothing coming in. The back buffer needs graduates, so it can't open before your first start finishes. The paid overflow needs more demand than free conversations can hold, and charging while reach binds costs conversations when every one counts. The Self-Serve System needs what about 20 graduates teach you. A room needs members, since an empty one reads as a failed product to the few who join.

**A rung switches on when the rung before it can feed it and your week can price its minutes.**

That's the **Rung Activation Schedule**. The months are where its triggers usually land in Bands A–B, the reach you should plan on, but the trigger decides and the month only estimates it:

| Months | Switch on | The trigger | Leave off |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | The free fit conversation · the founding group · one founding Private seat, premium with fixed deliverables · the priority tier · the Starter Path | Your first lead, first client, or first "not now" | The paid overflow, Round Two, the Hold, anything self-serve |
| 3–6 | Round Two and the Hold, for founding graduates · price steps toward the opening band | The first graduation; starts that fill | The alumni room; anything self-serve |
| 6–9 | The paid Decision Assessment for the overflow · the Program at its opening band ($1.5–2.2k) | Most weeks above ~6–8 held conversations, show rates sliding toward ~60%, over half ending no-fit | Program Async; Private at parity |
| 9+ | The Self-Serve System · the alumni room · Program Async · Private at parity · the Training-Partner Seat, pair enrollment, and a paid group decision session | ~20 graduates · ~30 alumni · the Program's price past ~$3.2k · Scaling care minutes · demand | Anything whose trigger hasn't fired |

Three rungs that look early start at month 0 on purpose. Every founding client joins the founding group from the first one, with one-to-one-level review while it has fewer than about four members, so you never build a one-to-one product you'd later have to retire (Module 9). One founding Private seat at about $4–6k, sold on speed, privacy, and fixed deliverables while your minutes are spare, gives the Optimizer, the buyer who pays for speed and privacy, a real top tier and makes showing premium first honest. The priority tier at about $350–600 is his way in: the same assessment, faster, with a recorded walkthrough, credited to the Program or Private.

The Starter Path starts at month 0 as well, because the first "I can't afford it" can arrive in your first week, and the stop rule needs somewhere honest to send him. Every paid rung gets a public page from the day it opens, naming who it's for, who it isn't for, its price, and one next step. Your decision-stage videos can carry a pitch section for the flagship, price included. A founding page reads premium first: [one founding Private seat at ~$4–6k], then the founding group at [~$1.2–1.5k] for 12 weeks, with the next price stated, [$1.5–2.2k from the [April] start].

Hiding a real tier fails a buyer as surely as pushing one on him. The Optimizer who never sees the priority tier waits a week for a free slot he would have paid to skip, and the core buyer who never hears when the founding price ends defers past a price he could have had. The Price Steps, small raises on a published schedule that each name what was added, then move the founding price toward the opening band as starts fill (Module 7).

The months move with your band. In a breakout band calls can bind by month 6, so the paid overflow arrives early; in Band A it may not arrive in year one, which costs nothing, since the overflow only receives what the Call Cap can't hold. The schedule also runs backward without a decision. After a quiet quarter fewer buyers overflow the cap and the paid assessment goes quiet by itself, because a trigger defined by flow keeps a rung from outliving its reason.

### Some rungs cost most when they come early

Five rungs do their damage by arriving before their trigger:

| Built early | What it costs |
|---|---|
| A community before ~30 alumni | An empty room reads as failure, and its moderation comes out of review minutes |
| Any self-serve product before ~20 graduates | Your earliest guesses, frozen into a product that few finish and fewer can show results from |
| Program Async before the Program passes ~$3.2k | A cheaper copy of your flagship at a price the core buyer can already carry |
| A second flagship, such as a six-month program | Two review rhythms and a split recommendation, when Round Two already offers the longer path at a lower first price |
| The paid overflow while reach binds | Fewer conversations while every one counts |

A free community never switches on, at any stage, because it spends live minutes on free attention and draws the rating talk and underage viewers your door exists to keep out. Paid community exists on this ladder only as continuity: the alumni room behind the Hold, opened once there are enough alumni to fill it.

## 5. The Starter Path and the Self-Serve System

For the buyer who can't afford you, the net is the whole of your brand he'll ever experience, so it has to be a real path rather than a polite exit. The Starter Path is a free, sequenced eight-week path for anyone who shouldn't buy now. Four kinds of buyer reach it: the man who can't afford it, the one who isn't a fit, the one whose "not now" has a real reason, and the one whose money isn't his. A Decision Assessment's written plan can recommend it too.

In a category where a no usually ends the seller's interest, what happens after his no is what he remembers and repeats. A path that treats his logs as a real record keeps him in the group this brand is built around, adults who measure instead of guess, whether or not he ever pays. It also makes the stop rules cheap to keep. When "I can't afford it" has a concrete next step waiting, you never feel the pull to reopen money, and he never has to defend his budget to end the conversation.

**The net gets the framework and a way back; the judgment waits until he can pay for it comfortably.**

### Eight weeks, two blocks, and a way back

The path runs two four-week blocks and ends in a trigger for coming back. Every method step in it stays a placeholder you fill from your own material. Its one photo follows the Capture Standard, your fixed conditions for distance, light, and angle, and it stays on his own phone:

| Weeks | He does | He checks | The touch (templated) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Logs [his habit block] daily; takes one baseline capture on his own phone | Days logged each week, and what got in the way | Check-ins at the end of weeks 1 and 4 |
| 5–8 | Keeps logging; changes one thing at a time, starting with [the first adjustment] | The week-8 self-check against the Outcome Map | A week-8 message restating his trigger |
| Re-entry | Names his trigger in his own words at the handover: a date, a money condition, or a record condition | Whether it has fired | None; he replies when it fires |

The week-8 self-check does the first job a reviewer would do. He sorts each of his goals into the Outcome Map's columns. Anything changeable and measurable, such as his habits, body composition, grooming, and how he's photographed, gets a measure he can keep. Anything never claimed, such as habits changing an adult's bone, gets a line through every product that promised it. That sort alone ends a lot of wasted spending, and it's a verdict he can reach without you.

The net's human touch is a real choice, and one option fails here:

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Templated check-ins at set weeks | Always; it's the path's only touch | Writing them once |
| A free monthly group Q&A | Never on this ladder | Live minutes on non-buyers; a free community by another name |
| A paid review of his Starter logs | He asks for judgment on his own record | $150–250, sold as a Decision Assessment |

**Default: templated check-ins.** Contact that reaches out on a schedule helps even when it carries no advice, while support he has to ask for adds little. Switch to the paid review only when he asks for judgment on his record, and run it through the same checks as any paid step. Fit-check conversations, Starter Path hand-offs, and renewal reviews share about a quarter-hour a week in an early design week.

### Handed over once, in the written recap

The path goes out in the written recap within 24 hours, once, and it's never chased. When the reason is "I can't afford it", money talk ends there under a stop rule: the path is the one concrete next step, and the pause route is set, a content-free tag that stops sales sequences and date sends for 60–90 days. The path's own touches carry no offer, price, or date, so they run inside it.

The Starter tool is the path's one paid item, a low-cost tool at $27–97, such as [a printable log and capture sheet]. It's named once, in the handover, with its price and the plain line that the path works without it. It's never named to a buyer who said he can't afford it or whose money isn't his, because naming any price reopens money talk. Its checkout asks the affordability question, "Is this comfortable from your own income or savings, without new credit or buy-now-pay-later?", and runs the Fit Check, the plain-language screen for fit signals before any paid step. Price endings barely matter, so choose a round number inside the range.

A buyer whose Fit Check paused the sale gets a reading-only version: the Honest Answer, the Outcome Map, and what measuring would look like later, with no capture step, no appearance log, and no Starter tool. Where the signal was checking or fixation, the referral comes first, because measurement would feed the loop that's hurting him.

When his trigger fires, the path pays you both back. His Starter logs become his Week-Zero Baseline, the pre-purchase record that counts as week zero of his program, so his first review starts from eight weeks of his own record instead of none (Module 17). If a Decision Assessment recommended the path, its credit waits until he enrolls, for up to about 12 months, and it's never used as a deadline.

### The Self-Serve System sells tools, not your judgment

The Self-Serve System is the net's paid upgrade: tools for do-it-yourself buyers at $97–297, one time, built only after about 20 graduates. It holds the logs, the Capture Standard, self-review prompts, and walkthroughs. It never holds the decision rules from your stall taxonomy, the sorted record of why clients stall and what moved each kind of stall. Those rules are the judgment itself, so selling them as a download moves the Free/Paid Line and prices your product against free information.

Two more reasons keep them out. Rules without a reviewer get misapplied, because telling which rule fits his record is exactly the part only review can see. And the taxonomy was built from your clients' records, which makes it the part of your method nobody can copy quickly. About 20 graduates is the trigger because before then you'd be packaging guesses. By then you know which logs clients keep, what a good review says, and whether your Capture Standard holds up in real bathrooms on real phones.

Early products also leave little proof. Unguided courses and apps are rarely finished, with completion in the low single digits, so a self-serve product built early sells a little and leaves almost no measured results behind. Its optional review is a Decision Assessment at $150–250, which keeps judgment on the paid side of the line and gives one product one job, and the fee is credited if he moves up to the Program. The checkout embeds the age attestation, the affordability question, and the Fit Check, and a checking or fixation signal means no capture tools are sold to him at all.

At the $50k configuration the Self-Serve System brings in roughly $3k a month, about 15 sales at about $197. That makes it a leverage layer, built last among your leverage projects, and never the engine. Graduates who skip the Hold can buy it as a kit for keeping their own record.

## 6. Packaging the Method, and the New-Creations Index

Package the path he'll walk and the deliverables he'll hold, because the method a buyer can't see is the first thing this category taught him to suspect. Names come first and stay small. Every product name describes what happens, sits under your brand, promises nothing about bone, and is settled before your first group starts (Module 3). The rest of the packaging is the client journey itself, laid out as dated deliverables he can picture before he pays:

**baseline day → weekly written review → the week-6 read → the week-12 re-assessment → graduation → Round Two, the Hold, or a clean finish.**

Name each stage by what he'll hold: a baseline, a written read, matched captures set side by side, a record he keeps. The method itself lives inside the review as your [weekly habit block] and [first adjustment], never as a hidden curriculum he pays to unlock. Packaging built this way answers "what am I buying?" with things he can check, and it keeps your claims on process and education. It passes the Hostile-Screenshot Test, the check that a line survives the category's harshest critic. The Path and Timeline Card, the one-page view of the whole path and its likely total cost, puts it in front of him before any payment.

### Seven new creations, each with one owner

Seven offers and formats on this ladder were built for jobs this business has and the category's usual menus don't cover. Each is taught in full by its owner:

| New creation | What it is | Slot | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Decision Assessment | A paid, credited written plan, worth its fee without buying | Front buffer | Module 5 |
| The priority tier | The same assessment, faster, with a recorded walkthrough | Front buffer; the Optimizer's entry | Module 5 |
| Round Two | A maintenance block for graduates with measured momentum | Back buffer | Module 10 |
| The Hold | A measurement subscription that opens into the alumni room | Back buffer | Module 10 |
| Program Async | The Program without the live call, a real tier under the 20–24 ceiling | Flagship tier | Module 13 |

### What never goes on the ladder

Some offers fail at every stage, and each has a reason you could give a buyer directly:

| Never | Why |
|---|---|
| A free community, or a free live group for non-buyers | It spends live minutes on free attention and draws rating talk |
| Low-ticket products as a profit line | They compete with free information and leave little proof |
| Decoy tiers | Every tier is real and bought by someone |
| A face rating or "analysis" | It sells what apps give away, and you never score faces |
| Window-only bonuses or prices | Your dates come from the calendar, and a price that exists only this week is a launch |
| Names that promise structural change | A name is a claim, made every time it's read |
| Tiers split by content or promised results | Tiers differ by review, speed, and live access |

What's left is a short ladder a skeptic can read on one screen: one thing to buy for the work, one step before it, two ways to keep measuring after it, and one free path for everyone else. It works in this category because every rung can be explained in a sentence, and a buyer who understands the whole ladder trusts the one recommendation you make from it.

## Worked Example: Cole's Ladder at Month 0 and Month 9

Cole starts in January with about 20 hours a week and a channel that grows steadily, which puts him in Band B, the steady band. Here is his ladder at both ends of his first nine months, with the decisions between them.

**Month 0, January. The read: nothing upstream exists yet.** No buyers, no graduates, and no data, so only rungs that need nothing upstream switch on. The first working version of his door runs a self-assessment with an age fork, the legal-adult question, and books a free, disclosed fit conversation of about 45 minutes. His founding page shows [one founding Private seat at ~$4–6k] first, then the founding group at [~$1.2–1.5k], with its next price stated. The priority tier sits on his result page at [~$350–600]. The Starter Path is written as a page and [three] templated emails, with the Starter tool at [$40].

Each rung's job fits in one sentence on his planning page. Left alone: the paid overflow, because reach binds and every conversation counts; Round Two and the Hold, because nobody has graduated; and the "routine as a PDF" that [a dozen] commenters ask for, which would be self-serve before a single graduate.

**Month [4]. The read: the back buffer's trigger has fired.** [Four] founding clients reach week 12, and [two] show measured momentum on their records. ## Checklists: Ladder Design and the Rung Job Card

Run the first whenever you add or retire an offer, and once a quarter, answering from your pages, your sales records, and your design week. Fill in the second before any rung goes live.

### Ladder design

1. Does one offer carry most of your revenue and all of your weekly review? → If two do, keep one flagship and make the other a tier of it or retire it.
2. Can you write each rung's job in one sentence that names its slot? → If not, move its buyers to the right rung and retire it with notice.
3. Has every paid tier sold at least once in the last [two quarters]? → A tier nobody buys works as a decoy; take it down.
4. Does every paid rung have a public page with who it's for, who it isn't for, its price, and one next step? → Publish it before you pitch it again.
5. Does the Starter Path go out in every recap sent within 24 hours of a no? → If not, write the handover before your next call.
6. Did any rung open before its trigger: self-serve before about 20 graduates, a room before about 30 alumni, the paid overflow before most weeks held more than 6–8 conversations? → Pause its sales until the trigger fires.
7. Do your tiers differ only by review depth, speed, and live access? → If one differs by content or a promised result, rewrite its card.

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** Minutes and buyers are both scarce, so the ladder stays small: the free fit conversation, the founding group, one founding Private seat, the priority tier, and the free Starter Path. The default move is writing the net before your first call, since the first "I can't afford it" can come in week one. The trap is building a product because followers ask for one; a template pack before 20 graduates sells a little and proves little.

**Growing.** Selling minutes bind, so the paid overflow switches on at the front while monthly Program starts and the first graduations switch on Round Two and the Hold at the back. The default move is holding each buffer to its job, with Round Two offered only on measured momentum. The trap is chasing the buffers' revenue: an assessment pushed on a buyer who needed a free conversation, or Round Two sold to a graduate whose record doesn't support it.

**Scaling.** Care minutes bind, and the ladder grows by tiers and leverage: Program Async under the ceiling, Private at parity, the Self-Serve System, the alumni room. The default move is adding tiers inside the flagship slot before any new slot. The trap is the menu, each option sensible on its own until the one recommendation becomes a list and buyers start asking which package again.

## Standard Check

- **Real tiers, premium first.** Every tier is real, shown premium first, priced from its range, and bought by someone; a tier nobody buys comes down, because it works as a decoy (*Present the price*). Tiers rise by review and speed, never by a promised result, so a client at the low end still endorses the tier he chose.
- **The net, handed over once.** The Starter Path goes out once, in the written recap, and the Starter tool is never named to a buyer who said he can't afford it (the line on vulnerability). Its touches carry no offer, price, or date, so they run inside the pause route.
- **Pages and names.** Every paid rung has a public page and price (*Sell directly*), and no rung or stage name promises a change to bone (the line on structural claims).
