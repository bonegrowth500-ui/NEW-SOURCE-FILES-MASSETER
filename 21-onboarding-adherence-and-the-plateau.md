# 21 · Onboarding, Adherence and the Plateau
*Part V — Selling and Transformation*

**The shift:** from *"Results depend on the client's discipline."* to *"Adherence is designed. Every plateau renews the decision, and some end in an honest exit."*

Sunday night, week 5. The check-in from Dan *(composite, Struggler)*, 24, arrives at [9:40 pm]: [2] of [7] days logged, after four weeks at six or seven. His plan-fit rating reads [1], and where his question usually goes there's one line: "I don't think this is working." He has been here before. His eight months of solo effort ended in a week like this one, with a flat stretch nobody could read and a quiet decision to stop. The category has taught him to hear any stall as a verdict, on the method, on his genes, or on him.

What happens in the next 48 hours decides three things you care about. It decides whether he asks for a refund at week 6, whether his week-12 record argues for more coaching, and whether he ever sends a friend your way. Very little of it turns on his discipline. It turns on what you built before his first bad week: terms he signed on day one, a first win he could check, and a review that reaches him before he goes quiet. It also turns on a plan, written in advance, for exactly this Sunday. This module builds that design, and the honest exit for the plateaus that should end.

## 1. Why Adherence Is Designed

Whether a client follows through depends more on what you build than on the discipline he brings. The tools with the best evidence behind them are things you design, so that's where your hours pay. Effort still matters. It's the part you can't install.

### A record helps most when someone reads it

Tracking progress toward a goal has a moderate effect on reaching it, one of the better-established findings in behavior change. The effect grows when progress is written down and reported to someone who reads it, and that last condition is what his eight months lacked. He checked constantly, in the mirror and in comparison photos. Nothing was recorded the same way twice, and nobody turned any of it into a decision. That's how a man in this category spends a year both busy and uninformed.

Unguided products show the same thing from the other side. Self-paced courses and apps keep a few percent of their users past the first weeks, and paying lifts completion partly because paying selects the committed. Most men at your door have run that experiment already, with a device, a course, or a feed of tips. So the Program, your 12-week flagship, asks about 10 minutes a week for his check-in, and your review turns that log into his next decision, the part no course can ship.

### Contact you start keeps him from going quiet

In trials against self-help, encouragement that reached out on a schedule gave better results and roughly half the dropout, moderate evidence from other kinds of behavior change. Support people had to ask for added little, and more contacts a week mattered more than the total number of sessions. The reason should bite harder here, because shame sits close to this work. A man who has missed four days on a program about his face doesn't write to his coach that night. He waits for a better week, and silence feels safer than a report.

"Message me anytime" hands the burden of contact to the man least able to carry it. Contact you start can't be dodged, because the check-in is due whether his week went well or not, and your review arrives before he feels ready to be reached. Format matters less than rhythm: at equal intensity, one-to-one, group, and guided formats do about equally well. That evidence is strong in other fields and untested in this one.

### Small tools stack, and none of them is the engine

If-then plans, a pre-set "when this happens, I do that", help less than their headlines suggest once bias is corrected. The effect is real and small. Give them five minutes on his first day and keep them off your sales page, since no single tactic doubles adherence and a page that claims one is selling a hero the evidence can't support.

Habits get the same honesty. Automaticity takes a median of about two months and ranges from days to most of a year. So many of his habits won't run on their own by week 12, which sets his expectations and explains why some records need the months after the Program. It never sets a date for an outcome, least of all one on his face.

**Adherence is built from a record he keeps, a reader who answers on a clock, and contact you start before he goes quiet.**

## 2. The Commit Ritual and the First-14

The first fortnight decides whether he judges the Program on evidence or on doubt, so it runs on two designed moments. One makes the terms clear on his first day. The other hands him a result he can check before his fit window, a full refund in his first 14–21 days of delivery, closes.

Doubt peaks after a costly purchase: unease, second-guessing, a worry that he overpaid. Here it cuts deeper, because every earlier purchase felt like this for a week before it turned out to be a course, a device, or a feed of tips. Reassurance can't answer that. Preparation can, since knowing what a process involves tends to reduce dropout, and so can real work that registers on a record he can read.

### Baseline day makes the terms clear and asks for nothing else

Baseline day, the Program's first review-and-decision day, produces his first capture, his first log week, and his markers, the two or three pre-agreed measures that decide his verdict (Module 6). Inside it, in about [25] minutes, three things happen. First, you read his week-zero record, the days he logged before day one, and decide the first change from it: [his habit block] moves to [before his shift], and nothing else changes for [two] weeks.

Second, he writes two or three lines, in his own words, on what he wants these twelve weeks to settle. Third, he signs off the Expectation Document, the written terms he read before paying, and you read its adherence condition aloud once: [80%] of days logged. Then you name every exit with its date. Up to day [16] the fit window refunds in full. From his week-6 read, the written mid-program review, the exit right refunds unused weeks to a client who did the work. At week 12 the non-response clause returns part of his fee in cash if he did the work and no marker moved. That's the **Commit Ritual**, and its one job is clarity:

**Decide the first change from his record, let him write his reasons for you alone, and sign the terms with every exit in view.**

The ritual stays small on purpose. Pledges do less for follow-through than their reputation suggests, and an identity announced to an audience can feel like progress already made. A pledge would also sit beside a refund right: a man who declares himself all in on day 0 has to break his word to use a fit window that runs to day [16]. So nothing in the ritual is public or promised. His reasons are his to reread whenever he likes. You read them only to check that the work still fits his goal, and they never come back to him as a reason to stay.

### The first fortnight answers doubt with his record

Two moves carry the fortnight. Within 72 hours of baseline day, a short note reads his first [two or three] days back to him and names one fact from them, such as the new slot holding twice. It proves the rhythm is proactive before he needs it, while doubt runs highest and no habit exists yet. A templated welcome doesn't count, because it shows nothing about whether anyone reads.

The second move is a win chosen on baseline day: a result about his practice that his record will show by day 14. The default is his first change read against week zero, such as shift-day misses falling from [3] to [1]. Size it so most clients reach it, keep his appearance out of it, and state it as a count from his record, with no label yet.

Together the two moves are the **First-14**: a reviewer's touch inside 72 hours and a win he can verify inside the first fortnight. Succeeding at something he can check is how a man comes to believe he can do the steps, and it's what his eight months never gave him.

### Doubt gets evidence, and a decision to leave gets a refund

Remorse inside the fit window takes two forms. "I'm second-guessing this" is doubt, so answer it with the plan and the day his record will settle it. By day 14 he'll see whether [the new slot] survives his shifts. Then say once, plainly, that the window runs to [date] and one line claims a full refund. "I want out" is a decision: the refund goes out within 7 days, any conversation stays optional and feedback-only, and nothing in it re-pitches.

The First-14 exists so his fit decision rests on delivered review, which is why the window closes just after his [second] weekly review. It's never built to carry him past that date. A man who leaves on day [12] takes a full refund and his fortnight's record, and that clean exit costs far less than the dispute a harder one invites.

## 3. Running Proactive Review

A weekly review keeps him logging only while he trusts the person writing it, so every review reads the relationship as closely as the record. What he bought is the Review Rhythm, proactive review every week on a stated clock plus one live call, and the Measurement Calendar, behavior logged weekly and captures only on fixed dates (Module 6). Both work through the reviewer.

### He follows through for a reviewer he trusts

People keep going for a reviewer they judge competent, well-meaning, and trustworthy, and they judge it inside the loop, whatever titles you hold. The working alliance, agreement on goals and tasks plus a bond, tracks outcomes at a steady correlation of about .3 across many studies in coaching and nearby fields, though nobody has measured it in this niche. Your client reads each review for signs he's being handled, because the category taught him the signs. Cheer from a template, a new tip every week, praise that doesn't match his log: each one tells him he bought another course.

So check the relationship as often as the log, with two questions: does he feel heard, and does the work fit his goal? After every live call he answers both with one tap each on a [1–5] scale. In every review you read them again in his check-in: his plan-fit rating, the length of his answers, a blank blocker, a question he stopped asking.

That's the Alliance Check, the two-question read on heard and fit. A drop of [2] points, or any answer at [2] or below, opens the next review: name it, ask one question, change one thing. Polite ratings run high, so watch the change more than the level, and read goal fit against the reasons he wrote on baseline day.

### Feedback counts what the record shows

Reviews speak in action and data words, such as record, block, plan, and decision, with the warmth kept in. "That's frustrating, and it makes sense" is validation he needs before he can hear a change. That's the Plain-Language Rule, action and data vocabulary with the clinic's words and framing left out (Module 15). A clinic's framing tells him he's receiving care for a condition, when the work he bought is work he directs.

Praise follows the same discipline. Earned Labels, praise that rests on his record, lead with a count and name the practice it shows: "[22] of [24] days logged is what doing this properly looks like." Praise his record doesn't show is out, even when it would be kind, because flattery about a [3] of [7] week is what he'll later remember as a lie. Labels wait out his fit window and pause for the 7 days after his week-6 read, so none sits beside a refund decision.

### The comeback earns the credit

One missed day doesn't undo a forming habit. The story the miss gets can. Blaming a lapse on a lasting flaw, "I always quit", predicts the slide, and one broken rule tends to become "the week's gone anyway". In one large, month-long study of gym members, a reward for coming back after a missed workout did best among dozens of tactics tested. So the rule is Never Miss Twice: reward the comeback, not the streak.

A Struggler's history is a string of broken streaks, and a streak counter turns one miss into a loss he knows too well. So nothing you run counts streaks. Reviews count returns: "[three] misses this month, each followed by a logged day." The day after a miss runs his miss plan, [a minimum version] agreed in review. Silence gets a ladder: a one-line note the same day, a personal touch at [48] hours, then the offer of a short call. No logging reminder mentions his refund terms, because his log decides his refund eligibility, and a reminder would aim his own record at his rights.

Two structural rules protect all of this. Routing help, the task-billed helper who moderates your public comments (Module 11), never sees a check-in, and no one but you reads or answers his messages, since check-ins are where distress, checking, and jaw pain first surface. And under Quiet Commitments, his commitments go to you and stay private by default, while boards stay opt-in, open after the fit window, rank only timestamps, and freeze while an exit window is open (Module 17). No status depends on leaving a refund right unused, and no room or board mentions who left.

**Read the relationship with the record, count only what's true, and give the comeback the credit.**

## 4. The Plateau Plan and the Renewed Decision

Most clients hit a plateau, and whether it becomes a stall, a refund, or a comeback depends on what you set up before it and do in the 48 hours after it shows. Measures like his markers rarely climb in a straight line, and motivation sags in the middle of a goal, when the start is behind him and the finish far off. Here the middle weeks also hold the week-6 read and the exit right that opens with it.

### A flat stretch reads as a verdict unless you read it first

A plateau lands on a man the category trained to hear stalls as verdicts. On the method it says "another grift", on his genes "it was always genetics", and on himself "I always quit". Every reading ends in a stop, a refund request, or silence. A flat stretch turns into a verdict when it arrives unexpected, unexplained, and unanswered, so the design removes all three. Announced in advance, it lands as the plan behaving as described. Re-read from his record, it gets the cause the record shows, often one he controls. Answered within 48 hours, it can't become a second lost week. That's the **Plateau Plan**, in one line:

**Announce the plateau before it comes, re-plan within 48 hours, reward the comeback, and at week 6 ask the verdict question first.**

| Step | When | What it does | What stays out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Announce | The Expectation Document, read at the Commit Ritual | Names the flat stretch and what you'll do | A promise it will be short |
| Re-read | The first review that shows it | Checks when the misses fell, what plan fit did, and how each marker was read, then names the cause the record shows | A cause the record doesn't show; "you weren't consistent"; "it's genetics" |
| Re-plan | Within 48 hours of the sign | One change in writing, or a hold with its reason when the record is clean; the marker sheet stays as signed | A new plan, a second change, a fresh tip |
| Reward the comeback | Every review after it | Counts the return | Streaks; any mention of refunds |
| Read week 6 in order | The week-6 read | Verdict question, then markers, then the honest stall reasons | A search through his photos |

The 48-hour clock starts on any of five signs: [two] or more fewer logged days than his average, a missed check-in, a marker flat across [two] readings, an Alliance Check dip, or any line saying it isn't working. Write the re-plan by default, since he bought written review on a clock and a re-plan he can reread is one he can follow. Offer a short call when the Alliance Check dips or a second plateau follows within [three] weeks. Reid *(composite operator)* answers each sign with a pep talk and a new tip. By week 6 he has changed three things in three weeks, and no marker can say which one did anything.

### Week 6 asks the verdict question first

At week 6 the order of the read decides its honesty. The verdict question comes first: given your adherence, is the lever moving? Below his adherence threshold, the record can't judge the lever yet, so the decision turns on fitting the plan to his week. At or above it, each marker is read against its baseline wobble, the normal spread in his day-one readings. If they're flat, Stall Verdicts sort the three honest reasons a stall happens: unmeasured, misdirected, or "the lever doesn't move for this person" (Module 14). The letter then lands on one of four outcomes: adjust one lever, hold the plan, refer him out, or the exit right.

"Misdirected" has to earn its label: a lever named before you look at the result, a correction in writing, and early movement at the next reading. Without all three, "the lever doesn't move for this person" stands, and nobody relabels it to sell more weeks. The next reading falls inside his exit window, so a provisional label is confirmed or dropped before the window shuts and can't hold him past his exit right. When the miss was your own review's, a signal you didn't catch or a step you set wrong, the weeks it cost come back free as corrective weeks, capped at 6.

Run it on Adrian *(composite, Optimizer)*, 31, at his week-6 read. He has logged [39] of [42] days, his readings are clean, and his [posture tally] sits inside its wobble. Before the next reading, you name one lever in writing as wrong: his [habit block] runs in [hotel mornings], while the tally is taken in [meetings], where the habit never shows up. The letter carries the correction, [a cue inside meetings], and two dates, the confirming reading on day [46] and the window's close on day [49]. If the tally moves past its wobble, the label holds, and because the step was yours, the weeks it cost come back free. If it doesn't, the label is dropped, and he decides on the exit right with that answer in hand. The tally reads [5], and he continues.

### Every plateau renews the decision

A plateau re-read with his own data reopens the question he answered when he paid, so put it to him plainly: continue as planned, change one thing, or stop. That's **Every Plateau Renews the Decision**. It comes with your one recommendation from the record, because a client left to drift through week 6 is as poorly served as one who's pushed. Stop is always on the list, stated with the terms that apply that week.

When heavy weeks drive the plateau, one rule picks between a smaller plan and a stop. If a minimum version keeps his logging where the verdict can still read the lever, keep the record running. If the stretch covers most of the weeks he has left, recommend the stop: the exit right inside its window, and deferral after it.

Then say the hard true thing, with force aimed at the situation, and end on the recommendation. To a man at [26] of [42] days: "[Twenty-six] days can't judge this lever, and I won't pretend they can. The plan hasn't had a fair test, and that's the problem to fix, not you. I'd move [the habit block] to [the slot your week protects] and read it again in [two] weeks. Yes?" Softening it into "plateaus are normal, hang in there" leaves him alone with the mirror. On the Dignity Route, the way of running a sale or review that keeps force off his worth, the force aims at the guessing, and no missed moment he named comes back to him.

## 5. The Honest Exit and Vulnerability During Delivery

Some plateaus should end, and an exit handled cleanly is part of the product. The programs your burned clients left before ended in arguments about money. A clean exit is also cheaper: refunds and disputes plan at about 2–8% of revenue, and a refund paid on time rarely becomes a dispute.

### A clean exit is part of what he bought

Sometimes he asks to stop under a right he holds, sometimes a trigger ends delivery, and sometimes his markers say the lever doesn't move. The move is the same each time: stop, pay per the written terms, and refer where his question belongs. That's the **Honest Exit**, run the same way every time:

**Confirm it in writing, pay within 7 days, refer where a trigger applies, and ask for nothing.**

The terms come from the Layered Guarantee, one refund layer for each thing that can go wrong, plus the stops outside its windows (Module 7):

| Stop | Who, and when | What he gets |
|---|---|---|
| Fit window | Anyone, from day one of delivery to day [14–21] | A full refund within 7 days |
| Stop before the week-6 read | Anyone, once the fit window has closed | Delivery ends at his word; the exit right at the read is his refund route |
| Exit right | A client at or above his adherence threshold who asks to stop, flat record or not, from the week-6 read until 7 days after | What he paid beyond delivered weeks, about half the fee if he paid in full, within 7 days; unpaid installments cancel |
| Referral stop | Anyone, in any week, when a refer-out trigger ends delivery | What he paid beyond delivered weeks, within 7 days; unpaid installments cancel |
| Stop by choice after the exit window | Anyone | Installments cancel forward, and unused weeks can move to a start within 6 months; delivered weeks aren't refunded |
| Non-response clause | At week 12, adherence at or above his threshold and no marker at its threshold | A fixed cash share inside 25–50%, raised by you, within 7 days of the verdict, with no renewal offer |

"Haven't moved" means no marker reached its threshold, so a single crossing means the lever moves and nothing is owed. A client below the threshold at week 12 isn't covered and gets an honest verdict. If he was at or above it at week 6, the exit right was his route out; if he wasn't, the week-6 read already put the drift to him as a decision.

A trigger ends delivery when he chooses to stop and see someone, or when continuing would keep the trigger alive, as when the program's own measurement feeds his checking. Otherwise his behavior review can run alongside a clinician's care. The Scope Boundary names who can help: "I can't help with this, and here's who can" (Module 15), for sleep or snoring signs, jaw pain, bite concerns, distress, or fixation, raised at first mention.

Three things stay out of every exit: his written reasons, a re-pitch, and a testimonial ask. No testimonial is asked for where a refund right is decided, and a client who got a refund is never asked, then or later. He keeps his record, a closing count of what he did, and his standing among the adults who measure, a membership the practice earned. Nobody in the room hears about it from you.

### Rising checking or distress pauses every ask

Weekly review puts you closest to him in the weeks a checking habit can grow, so read for it in every check-in. The signs are a climb in how often he checks his face, interim photos sent to you, a request to rate a capture, distress in his answers, or "everything is ruined". Any one of them pauses persuasion: no renewal offer, no referral or testimonial ask, no hard true thing, and no sharing or board. The scheduled captures stop, and his markers carry the verdict without them.

The pause route, a content-free tag that blocks sales sequences, date sends, and checkout for 60–90 days, goes on too. Asks come back only after it runs out, he agrees to hear from you again, and his record reaches a measured peak. The referral conversation names a doctor or mental-health professional, with crisis resources if it's acute, and names no condition. Record only what the referral needs, and a stopped offer leaves only "stopped: stop rule".

**When the signals disagree.** Say a client logs [42] of [42] days and sends every check-in on time. In week [6] he mentions he has started photographing his side profile every morning "to see if it's working". The log says model client, maybe one due a label. The photos say a checking habit is growing around the work. The checking outranks the log, because the log counts his habit blocks and says nothing about his relationship with the mirror.

So his week-6 read runs under the rising-checking rules. The week-6 capture is skipped, and the letter says why: a phone at arm's length distorts a face, and checking tends to feed the dissatisfaction it's meant to settle. It reads his markers, which never needed a photo, counts his days with no label, states the exit right as plainly as ever, and offers the referral conversation in plain words. Every ask waits. If his answer shows checking many times a day, or checking that eats his sleep or work, the referral comes first, and if he'd rather stop to see someone, the referral stop pays for his unused weeks.

## 6. Adherence Data as the Renewal Case

His renewal case at week 12 is written in weeks 1 to 11 or not at all, from data the review collects anyway. Measured momentum, the qualifier for Round Two, the maintenance block after graduation, has two halves (Module 10). His adherence sits at or above his threshold, and at least one marker is still improving across its last two readings. Both halves must be on the record before anyone asks, so capture five things now:

1. Days logged each week, with comebacks counted, since the threshold reads all twelve weeks.
2. Every marker reading on schedule through weeks 10–12, since "its last two readings" needs two readings near the end.
3. His effort and plan-fit ratings by [habit block], which show which habits still take effort at week 12. That's the honest case for maintenance, and never a timeline for his face.
4. Every re-plan, with its named lever, its correction, and the next reading, so a corrected misdirection has its evidence and a review error shows its free weeks.
5. The Alliance Check's trend, which says whether the relationship can carry a recommendation.

The same data tells some men they don't need more, and "you don't need Round Two" belongs to the case as much as a renewal does. Every ask waits for a measured peak: adherence at or above his threshold and at least one marker at its threshold, with no refund decision open. The ask belongs to the Renewal Case, argued from his record at the week-12 re-assessment (Module 22), and everything captured stays in the record he takes with him.

## Scripts: The Commit Ritual, the Plateau Conversation, and the Honest Exit

### The Commit Ritual, at the sign-off

Adrian travels most weeks for work, joined under a handle with his camera off, and has just agreed his first change. The sign-off comes next.

> **You:** "Before you sign, the terms, read once. They turn on one line: at least [80%] of days logged. You have three exits. Until [date], one line gets a full refund. From your week-6 read, if you're at or above [80%], you can stop and get back what you paid for the weeks you won't use. At week 12, if you did the work and no marker reached its threshold, part of your fee comes back in cash. Last, two lines in your own words on what you want these twelve weeks to settle. They're for me, and every review aims at them."
> *[Stop. Let him answer.]*
>
> **Adrian:** "Why tell me how to leave on day one?"
>
> **You:** "Because a commitment made with the exits in view is one you can keep honestly."

Notice the move. The adherence condition and every exit were read once, plainly, before he signed, so what he committed to is exactly what he can check later. That's the Commit Ritual doing its one job, clarity, and for an Optimizer, precision about the terms is the reassurance.

Three days later, at the First-14 touch, the doubt arrives:

> **Adrian:** "Honestly, I'm half wondering whether I should've just done this myself."
>
> **You:** "Fair, and common in the first days. You've logged [three] of [three], and the pre-meeting slot held twice. By day 14 your record shows whether it survives a travel week, which beats a feeling either way. Your fit window runs to [date], and one line gets a full refund, no call needed."

What you never say: "Most men who leave in the first fortnight regret it." It invents a number and aims his first days at a fear of regret. Then return to the decision: "[The pre-meeting slot] through next week's trip, and we read it on day 14. Does that work?"

### The plateau conversation, at week 8

Sam *(composite, Ambivalent)*, 22, an apprentice electrician, hits his busy season [three] weeks early, with [four] weeks of the Program left after it. His check-in shows [3] of [7] days and a note: he might "pause till after the busy season". He takes a [15]-minute call inside the 48 hours.

> **You:** "That's a rough week, and it makes sense: twelve-hour site days leave nothing over. Here's your record. Seven weeks at six or seven days, then three, every miss on a site day. Your [posture tally] went from [2] to [5] and has read [5] twice, the flat stretch page [two] of your terms described. I'd keep the record running: on site days, [the minimum version] after your shift, and the full plan back on [date]."
> *[Stop. Let him answer.]*
>
> **Sam:** "Can't I just pause and pick it up after?"
>
> **You:** "You can. Stop now, and your remaining payments cancel and your [four] unused weeks can move to a start within six months. Notice the move. His frustration came first, then the record, which named site-day timing and placed the flat reading inside the stretch his terms predicted. His question about pausing got the true terms before the recommendation, so the recommendation had to win on its reasons. That's Every Plateau Renews the Decision, with the Plateau Plan's one change on the table.

If he pushes:

> **Sam:** "So why not just defer?"
>
> **You:** "Because three weeks on the minimum version keep your markers readable, and a gap of a few months starts the habit cold. If the minimum version fails, deferring is still yours."

What you never say: "Stop now and you lose your week-12 refund." His refund terms never argue for staying. Then return to the decision: "[The minimum version] after site days, check-in Sunday as usual, full plan back on [date]. Yes?"

### The honest exit, at week 12

Theo *(composite, Burned Struggler)*, 26, logged [77] of [84] days, every reading was clean, and neither marker reached its threshold. His question about [his bite] went to an orthodontist in week [3], the day he raised it. You raise the clause before he asks.

> **You:** "Here's your verdict. [77] of [84] days logged, every marker read the same way each time, and neither reached its threshold. Your effort isn't the question, and I won't make it one. This lever doesn't move for you on this plan. So [a fixed share inside 25–50%] of what you paid comes back in cash by [date], inside 7 days. Your record is yours: every log, every capture with its conditions, the marker sheet."
> *[Stop. Let him answer.]*
>
> **Theo:** "Could we call it misdirected and try another lever? I'd pay for another round."
>
> **You:** "No. I can't name a lever in your plan that was wrong for your goal, and misdirected starts there. Another round of this would be the [device] again with better paperwork."

Notice the move. The verdict arrived with its evidence and its payment in one breath, and his request for more time met the standard's first condition instead of a sale. That's the Honest Exit: stop, pay per the clause, and refer at first mention, which for Theo was week [3]. For a burned buyer, a coach who turns down his money sends the signal his last two purchases never did.

If he offers something back:

> **Theo:** "You're the first one who's been straight with me. Want me to write something up?"
>
> **You:** "Thank you, and no. Once a fee comes back, I don't ask for a testimonial, today or later, so nothing about your refund looks bought."

What you never say: "On day one you wrote that you wanted to know for sure, and now you do." His reasons stay out of every exit, even as a compliment. Then close on the facts and stop: the refund lands by [date], no Round Two offer follows, and the conversation ends there.

## Template: The Plateau Pre-Announcement

The plateau paragraph for the Expectation Document. It goes out before payment, is read aloud at the Commit Ritual, and is quoted back at his first plateau: the terms he signed are fair to quote, and his reasons aren't.

> **Plateaus.** Expect a flat stretch, often somewhere in weeks [4–8]. Markers rarely climb in a straight line, and a flat reading or two is part of the plan. When a check-in shows one, whether it's fewer logged days, a missed check-in, a flat marker, or you telling me it doesn't feel like it's working, I'll re-plan within 48 hours. I'll read your record for the cause and either change one thing or hold the plan, and tell you why. Everything else stays as signed, including your markers and their thresholds. The day after a missed day, do [the minimum version]; a single miss changes little, and I count comebacks. At week 6 I ask one question first: given your adherence, is the lever moving? Then I read your markers and write the answer down, and you decide from that page whether to continue, adjust, or stop, with your exit right on the same page. No photos between capture dates, however flat a week feels.

Leave out of this paragraph any promise that the flat stretch will be short and any timeline for visible change. The adherence condition and the refund terms each live in their own section of the document, stated once.

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** What binds is knowledge: you don't yet know where plateaus land or what a good re-plan says, and your founding clients will show you. The default move is to log every plateau with its week, pattern, change, and next reading, writing each re-plan from scratch at about 30–35 care minutes, your review and call time, per client-week. The trap is answering the founding group's closeness with "message me anytime", which feels generous and teaches your first clients to wait.

**Growing.** Conversion binds the business, and delivery feels the starts stacking: a whole start's week-6 reads land in the same week. The default move is to template the plateau response's structure from what the founding plateaus taught you, then batch the reads. The trap is a template that answers without reading, a stock "plateaus are normal" a burned client spots at once.

**Scaling.** At about 13–15 care minutes per client-week, a thin review can miss a plateau until week 6. The default move is numeric flags, used with his consent: fewer logged days, a missed check-in, a flat marker, or an Alliance Check dip moves his check-in to the top of your reading. You still handle every plateau conversation and every exit yourself. The trap is letting flags decide which check-ins you read at all.

## Standard Check

- **Force at the plateau.** The hard true thing aims at the unreadable stretch and the plan, never at his face or worth, and on the Dignity Route at the guessing (*Say the true thing*; the line on shame).
- **Reasons and refunds kept apart.** His baseline-day reasons never come back in any exit, labels and boards pause in the fit and exit windows, and no logging reminder mentions his refund terms (*Build identity on evidence*).
- **The renewed decision.** Every plateau offers continue, adjust, or stop with that week's true terms, and "misdirected" is settled inside the exit window, never used to sell more weeks (*Close*).
- **Exits and vulnerability.** Every stop pays on its written terms within 7 days with no re-pitch, no refunded client is ever asked for a testimonial, and rising checking or distress pauses every ask, automation included (the line on vulnerability).
