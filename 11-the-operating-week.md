# 11 · The Operating Week
*Part III — Start, Extend, Run, Grow*

**The shift:** from *"Work harder when it's busy"* to *"My week is designed line by line, overload triggers a fixed order of cuts, and the leverage gets built on a protected line."*

Reid *(composite operator)* logs [31] hours in week [23] of his second year. He answers every DM, including [a dozen] from men asking him to look at their jaw. He spends [an evening] arguing with a rival's fans in his comments and holds a free call with a man who wanted a promise about bone. By Friday night his inbox is empty, and his channel has nothing new on it. It feels like the most productive week of his year.

Six weeks later his eligible leads, the adults who could pay, fall by [about a third]. He blames the season and a rival's viral clip. He never connects the two weeks, because nothing connects them on the day: a skipped video leaves nobody waiting, while every message is a person asking for him now.

A solo business has to run in about 20 hours with slack, or it doesn't run. This module designs that week line by line for your stage, protects the content that fills your calendar a month from now, and fixes the order of cuts before a heavy week arrives. It prices every per-person minute, builds leverage on a line nothing else can take, and keeps the systems, the AI, and the legal list as small as the business allows.

## 1. The Design Week

Your week needs a line for every kind of work before the work arrives, because in this niche per-person demand outbids anything that pays off later.

### Per-person demand outbids the work that pays later

One-to-one demand runs unusually high in this category. A young audience raised on rating culture asks for private verdicts on its faces by DM. Skeptics want to argue in public, and burned buyers want a free call to test you before they'll trust a page. Each request is small, and most feel like service. Together they grow with your audience rather than your revenue, and because each one arrives with a person attached, they win every contest against work whose payoff comes weeks later.

A solo week has a hard edge at 20–25 hours, and work without a budget expands into it in the order it shouts. Content shouts least, since a skipped video sends no message. Review shouts late: a slipping turnaround surfaces a week after the damage, as a credit you owe. So a week nobody designed drifts toward replies and calls, starves content and building, and runs hottest on the lines that compound least.

The answer is to decide where every hour goes before the week starts. Each kind of work gets its own line, sized to your stage, so a request without a line has to take its hours from one you can see. The total sits near 20, and the gap to 25 absorbs the weeks that lump. That's the **Design Week**, and it fits in a sentence:

**Every hour has a line, every line has a size, and the gap to 25 is slack.**

### Each stage's week is priced line by line

Every line in the four stage weeks carries hours, including the four that operators leave off: milestones, the Hold, Private, and building. The last column says what each line buys.

Read each column as a budget for its stage's job. Early spends about 8.5 hours on content because short-form is your reach engine and nobody knows you yet, and 4.5 on conversations because month 3 is decided by how many you hold. Growing moves hours from making content to converting interest, and care, the review, call, and milestone minutes your clients take, becomes the biggest block. Admin gains half an hour there, because freelancers and routing help each need a brief. At the configurations, templates and enrollments without a call hand hours back, which is why the $25k week is lighter than the Growing one.

### The forgotten lines are promises with dates

Milestones cost about 0.75–1.25 hours per enrollment beyond weekly review: the Commit Ritual, the review and decision on baseline day; the written week-6 read; and the week-12 re-assessment, the closing capture and verdict. So that line grows with monthly enrollments rather than with clients, and it lumps, because a start's week-6 reads all land in the same week. The Hold's line climbs as graduates join, from about a quarter-hour in Growing to about 0.6 hours at $50k, since every member is owed a re-capture and a written review each quarter.

Private gets a line only while a seat earns its place: a founding seat while minutes are spare, and about one seat at $25k. At $50k it has none until a seat passes the Parity Rule by earning at least what a seat in your 12-week Program earns per hour of care (Module 13). The build line is where leverage gets made: door v0, the first working version of your door, and the check-in form in the first months, then the templates that make Scaling lighter. It shrinks from 1.5 hours to about 1, and it's protected, because every later week is only as light as what this line built.

Slack is the fifth line, and it has no row. Your range is 20–25 hours, and the design sits near the bottom on purpose: about 5 hours of slack early and at $25k, about 4 at $50k, and about 3 in Growing. It absorbs what the Measurement Calendar, your fixed dates for captures, makes lumpy: baseline days at the start of a cohort and re-assessments at its end, plus a dispute, a sick week, or a piece that lands. Growing has the thinnest gap because selling and untemplated review peak together there, while the leverage that lightens Scaling is still on the build line.

### Cole's founding week holds its lines

Cole *(composite operator)* is in month [2], with [three] founding clients and [four] conversations a week. His week follows the Early column: a long-form piece every other week, [five] native short pieces batch-filmed on [Monday], his replies, and four Dual-Purpose Conversations, his disclosed fit-and-research calls. The founding group, every early client in one group, gets review at one-to-one depth, and this month the build line holds the check-in form.

In week [7], two baseline days land together, and Adrian *(composite, Optimizer)*, 31, asks about a founding Private seat. The baseline days come out of slack, and the week reaches [about 22] hours with no line moved. The Private seat fits its half-hour line while minutes are spare, so after the same checks every paid step runs, Cole sells it with its fixed deliverables. He doesn't take that hour from short-form, the line producing his month-3 conversations.

Design your week once per stage from the column that fits, and give each line a place as well as a size: content in batches, review in fixed blocks, live work in windows, all on the calendar before any client slot exists. Whenever the stage changes or a week runs past 25, log hours by line for two weeks, because the overrun hides in whichever line nobody was logging. At the monthly Operator Review, your standing review of the numbers, compare each line with its design. A planning line gives way to your own measured one only after about 30 events stand behind it: 30 reviews timed, 30 conversations held.

## 2. The Protected Content Minimum and the De-Scoping Order

Two rules keep a heavy week from eating the business: content has a minimum that client work can never take, and past 25 hours the cuts come in an order fixed in advance.

### This week's skipped video is next month's missing leads

Content is the line an overloaded week cuts first, because its cost arrives weeks later, when nobody is looking. Leads come from pieces published weeks before, so a skipped video costs nothing this week and shows up a month on as a thinner calendar, with its cause forgotten. In this category the lag runs long. A burned buyer watches for weeks before he trusts you enough to book, and long-form carries that trust, so every gap is paid for by a skeptic you were halfway to convincing.

So the content lines at each stage form a minimum that client work can't take. They cover long-form, short-form, email, and public replies, and they come to about 8.5 hours early, 6.75 in Growing, 5.25 at $25k, and 5 at $50k. An editor and batch filming can shrink your measured lines, yet no week at any stage goes below about 5 hours. That's the **Protected Content Minimum**. It holds line by line, since an extra hour of DMs won't fill your calendar a month from now the way a long-form piece will.

**Content goes on the calendar first, Seat Math keeps clients off it, and no week drops below about 5 hours.**

Three things hold it. Filming happens in batches every two weeks, blocked before any client slot is offered. Seat Math, the monthly seat caps set from your measured care minutes, keeps client work from ever needing those hours (Module 6). And your weekly review flags any week under the minimum, so a slide gets caught in days, well before it reaches your leads.

### Past 25 hours, the cuts come in a fixed order

Decide the cuts before the week arrives, because inside an overloaded week everything looks urgent and the loudest line wins. When a week runs past 25 hours, cut in this order, cheapest now first:

1. **Building beyond the protected line, and experiments.** Their payoff is the longest and least certain, so a pause costs nothing this month. The build line itself stays.
2. **Extra platforms: X first, then native Instagram, keeping the routing.** X goes first because its job, testing arguments in public, sits furthest from your door. Instagram's own content goes next, while the Keyword Route, the DM keyword that returns your door link, keeps running, because adult leads arrive there. It's the logic of the Platform Count Rule: run only the platforms your editing budget can serve (Module 25).
3. **Short-form above its weekly minimum.** Past your stage's line, short-form is mostly hook testing, and a short view yields far fewer leads than a long-form view.
4. **Long-form above its minimum cadence.** That's every other week early, three a month in Growing, and two to three at scale. Above it, a cut costs trust slowly.
5. **New conversations.** Pause booking and tighten the Call Cap, your weekly limit on free conversations. This step comes last because it costs revenue this month.

That's the **De-Scoping Order**. Five things sit outside it and are never cut: review turnaround, milestones, Hold and Private deliverables, the content minimum, and the Operator Review. The first three are the product, and they're guaranteed, so a cut there returns as a service credit and a client who stops trusting the review. The minimum protects the leads a month out, and the review is the hour that tells you whether the order worked.

The order protects selling on purpose. Building, platforms, and extra content all go before a single conversation does, and even at step 5 the offer stays on sale: warm pages keep their public prices and checkouts, and the booking page shows its next open week. A screened, good-fit buyer still meets the offer, the price, and a next step while your hours come back under 25.

### Two weeks at step 5 means capacity binds

One heavy week is noise. Two weeks running at step 5 is the signal that capacity binds, and the fix is capacity work. Take the next Price Step, a small raise on your published schedule (Module 7), let Seat Math cap the next start, and move templated review to the top of the build line. Extra hours never appear on that list, because a week past 25 is the problem the order exists to end.

**When the signals disagree.** A piece breaks out, and inquiries run at [three times] normal for [two] weeks. The hours say step 5; the revenue says take every call. Go by the order, since the Call Cap and its overflow exist for spikes: free conversations stay at about 6–8 a week, the rest get the Decision Assessment, your paid, credited written plan, and every inquiry hears your next real start. Keep the content minimum, since the next piece turns new viewers into leads. Two weeks still at step 5 is the capacity signal, spike or not. Never turn the spike into a launch: the Launch Line, the test that keeps a date honest, rules out a window price, a countdown, and extra sends as the start nears (Module 8).

## 3. The Unpriced-Minute Rule

Every minute you spend on one person needs a price, a window, or a template, because per-person work grows with your audience while revenue grows only with the eligible adults inside it.

### Per-person minutes grow with every viewer you reach

A per-person minute is any minute spent on one named person: a DM, a follow-up, a rebooking, a fit-check conversation, a question between check-ins. One video serves a thousand viewers, and a DM serves one. In this niche one-to-one requests arrive from every direction: minors ask for verdicts on their faces, skeptics want a free call to test you, and hopeful buyers book at night and vanish by morning. Left alone, these minutes rise with every viewer you reach, and they take their hours from the lines that send no reminders.

So each one gets one of three answers. Priced means it has a line in the week, plus a fee where he's buying judgment, the way the paid assessment turns an overflow hour into paid work. Batched means it happens only inside a fixed window. Templated means its words are written once, approved by you, and reused. A minute with none of the three is a leak, and naming the three gives you the **Unpriced-Minute Rule**:

**Every per-person minute is priced, batched, or templated.**

Hunt the free face read first. It's the rating you refuse to sell, given away by DM, often to someone whose age you don't know, so one reply can leak your hours and put a face rating in front of a minor.

| Per-person minute | Priced, batched, or templated | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Can you look at my jaw?" in a comment or DM | Templated: one plain line and your door link | A free read is a face rating, and he may be a minor |
| DMs | Batched into a fixed daily slot; templated by case (Module 25) | DMs are where minors and distress show up most, so you work them yourself |
| Speed-to-lead replies | Priced on their line; sent from your template once replies pass about an hour a day | Speed lifts booking, and a template keeps it fast |
| Fit-check conversations | Priced on their line; batched into live windows; never skipped | Skipping one sells into a signal |
| Follow-up after a held conversation | Templated: a recap within 24 hours, one check-in on his date, one close-the-loop | A fixed count can't turn into chasing |
| No-shows and reschedules | The no-show rule | One message, then the door |
| Starter Path handovers | Templated and handed over once: the free path for anyone who shouldn't buy now | Free live help for non-buyers is a free group by another name |
| Client questions between check-ins | Batched into his next review; distress the same day | The weekly review is the product, and on-call help isn't |

### Live work happens in fixed windows

Put every live minute in a few fixed windows a week: fit conversations, live assessments, group calls, Private calls, and fit-check conversations. Your booking page shows only those slots. The reason is review. The reviewer's job includes catching quiet signals, such as a rising urge to check his face, a distress line in a log, or a capture shot under different light, and a day chopped up by calls misses them. A missed signal is exactly what your service guarantee pays for with free corrective weeks. Put at least one window outside working hours, since the core buyer works full-time and many keep this pursuit private at work.

### One rule covers every missed call

The no-show rule is one rule for every missed call and every reschedule, and its time sits on the half-hour line for no-shows, recaps, and follow-up. Bookings land within 24–48 hours with one reminder, because show rates fall the further out a slot sits. A no-show gets one templated message, sent once, with the next open slots and no reproach, and a reschedule request gets the same message. If he misses the rebooked slot, it goes back on the calendar and the next booking is his to make. The door stays open, and the written path his self-assessment gave him carries him meanwhile.

Nobody chases him with a second message, a "still interested?" sequence, or a fee after the fact, and the reason is who misses. A man who books on a hopeful night and loses his nerve by morning is common in a category this close to shame, and a reproachful chase confirms what he was afraid of. If no-shows still eat your selling hours once bookings land fast with a reminder, switch the fit conversation to a small credited booking fee, the door's own option for exactly this (Module 5).

## 4. The Keep-List, Routing Help, and the Build Queue

Four kinds of work never leave your hands, production goes to freelancers billed by the task, public routing goes to a helper with hard limits, and the hours they free go to a queue ranked by hours saved.

### Four kinds of work stay with you

The Keep-List is the work that never leaves your hands: claims approval, selling, review, and client communication. Each one carries a line of the House Standard or the product itself, so handing one off hands away what buyers came to trust you with.

- **Claims approval.** Every title, thumbnail, caption, and clip is a claim. In a category built on structural promises, one careless caption can undo a year of your Honest Answer, your straight public answer to the bone question. Public replies beyond a templated link are claims too, so they stay yours.
- **Selling.** The Fit Check before any paid step, the affordability question about paying without new credit, and the stop rules that end a sale all take judgment in the moment. So does the other half of selling: asking a good-fit buyer, and naming what waiting costs him.
- **Review.** It's the product, and your written terms promise that every line of judgment in it is yours.
- **Client communication.** His check-ins, captures, and messages hold what he tells nobody else, and distress shows up in them first.

### Freelancers take production, billed by the task

Production goes out first: long-form editing, then clips cut from it, thumbnails, design, and scheduling. Hand off editing before anything else, because it's the largest block of hours in which your judgment isn't the product. Each task is billed per piece, so the business stays solo and any handoff can end the week it stops earning its fee. Every derivative comes back to you before it ships, since a clip cut away from its caveat is a new claim you never made. Freelancers work from raw footage of you and your brand assets, and they never see a capture, a check-in, or a client's message.

### Routing help moderates, links, and stops there

From Growing, public comments outgrow your reply line, and the Keep-List still holds. Routing help, a helper who works only your public comments, is defined once, here:

- It's task-billed per batch, never paid by the hour, and never staff.
- It moderates public comments against your published comment policy and sends one templated door link.
- Anything involving a minor, distress, a purchase question, or client content goes to you, untouched.
- It never sees check-ins or fit-check answers.
- It never works DMs or email replies.

Each limit follows a line. A comment that reveals a minor needs the education lane, public content and a referral with nothing collected, and you're the one who gives it. Distress needs a referral conversation, and a purchase question needs the checks every paid step runs. DMs and email are where minors disclose, distress arrives, and buyers ask about price, so those channels stay with you. The templated link counts as routing, never contact, because the door asks his age before it keeps anything. Honest criticism is never removed, and the helper flags the best of it for you to answer.

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| You do all of it | Early, while replies are a source of conversations | Content hours as your comments grow |
| Routing help, billed per batch | From Growing, once moderation starts eating the reply line | A per-batch fee, a written brief, and your weekly spot-check |
| An auto-reply tool on comments | Never, for routing | It can't see a minor, distress, or a purchase question, so it links all three to the door |

**Default: you through Early, then routing help from Growing.** Early replies start conversations and need your judgment, while later ones are mostly moderation a brief can hold. Switch when routing help's trigger fires in your Scaling Triggers, the signals you set in advance for each scaling move (Module 12).

### The Build Queue runs in order of hours saved

Hours freed by templates and help go to the build line, which spends them in one fixed order. That order is the Build Queue, your leverage builds ranked by hours saved each week per hour spent building:

1. **Templated review.** Review is the biggest recurring care line: 12–20 minutes per client-week for your first 20 or so clients, 6–10 once templated. Build it once about 20 clients have taught you what a good review says, and disclose how reviews are made before any tool helps.
2. **The Decision Assessment template.** A written plan takes 1–1.5 hours all-in early and 0.5–0.75 once templated, and the saving starts the week the Call Cap overflows.
3. **The Async Arc.** Carrying the call's arc through pages, emails, and a recorded walkthrough moves most Scaling enrollments off your calendar (Module 20).
4. **Round Two and Hold terms.** Written once, they end the per-person negotiation at every graduation.
5. **Private deliverables.** Fixed deliverables make a seat priceable, and checkable against the Parity Rule.
6. **The Self-Serve System.** These tools for DIY buyers wait for about 20 graduates, and they're the biggest build with the smallest weekly saving.

Take them in order, one at a time, on the build line only. A build started too early freezes your weakest version into the product: templated review before 20 clients locks in reviews you haven't yet learned to write.

## 5. Systems, AI, and Privacy Operations

Run one tool per job, let AI work only behind the scenes, and keep privacy as records you could show a client, because every tool you add is another place his face or his words can leak.

### One tool per job, added only for minutes or safety

The stack stays short: an owned site with the self-assessment and checkout, one email tool, one booking tool that shows only your windows, and one payment processor with buy-now-pay-later and third-party financing switched off. Client records live in one secure place, and freelancers get a shared folder of raw footage and brand assets. Add a tool only when it takes minutes off a line or removes a risk. Before it touches anything a client gave you, answer four questions in writing: what it touches, who can see it, where it stores, and how it deletes. A tool that can't answer the fourth stays away from check-ins.

### AI stays behind the scenes, and he's told where

The AI rule is one sentence: "AI assists behind the scenes, never speaks as you, and clients are told where it helps." Behind the scenes means drafting, transcribing your own footage, suggesting clips, and tallying logged numbers, all under your claims approval. Never speaking as you means no AI avatar, no cloned voice, and no AI-written reply sent in your name. The line on fabricated proof covers the rest: no AI-made or AI-edited face or result, ever. No tool reads a capture either, because software that measures a face is face analysis, whatever the tool calls it.

**Tools touch numbers, never faces, and only with his consent.**

Client consent comes before any tool processes a check-in. His written terms name each tool and what it touches, numbers only, and he opts in or out; a client who declines gets the same review, with his numbers tallied by hand. Record each consent with its date and scope, because your review disclosure is only as good as the record behind it.

### Privacy runs on a retention map and a consent log

What the client experiences belongs to delivery: captures seen only by you, pseudonymous and camera-optional cohorts, deletion on request (Module 6). Operations make those promises true, and they come down to one map and one log.

| Record | Who can see it | How long it's kept |
|---|---|---|
| Self-assessment answers | You; your email tool gets tags, never answers | [Your stated period], or until he asks |
| Fit-check answers | You only; never a marketing tool or a helper | [A short stated period], then deleted |
| Check-ins | You, plus a consented tool that reads numbers only | While he's a client or in the Hold, then [period] |
| Captures | You only, in one store that no tool reads | As check-ins; published only with separate consent |
| Consent records | You | As long as what he agreed to is in use |
| Payment records | You and your processor | As the law requires |
| Anything from a minor found after the fork | Nobody | Deleted at once; any payment refunded |

The consent log holds every yes with its scope and date: a tool on his check-ins, a capture or testimonial in marketing, his record in a consented dataset. Each is separate and revocable, and you check the log before anything publishes. A deletion request gets closed in every tool on the map, then confirmed to him in writing. Health-adjacent answers stay out of pixels, event names, and URLs, where an ad platform would read them.

### A minor found late is deleted and refunded

The age fork, your door's first question about whether he's a legal adult where he lives, keeps minors out before any data exists. When one surfaces later, on a call, in a DM, or in his answers, the conversation ends. What was collected is deleted, anything he paid is refunded, and he's pointed to the education lane: public content and an orthodontist referral. Keep no list of minors, hashed or otherwise. A list of who was underage is a minor's data kept after the minor is gone, and the fork asks again if he ever returns.

## 6. The Risk Register

Six legal and platform risks are big enough to write down, and each needs one line: what can go wrong, your default, and the point where you pay for advice.

### Six flags carry the risks worth writing down

Legal risk in this business sits where the category's history does: young audiences, health-adjacent claims, money taken over months, and private data about faces. A long compliance manual goes unread, while six lines get read at every quarterly review. So the Risk Register, your short list of the biggest legal and platform risks, holds six flags. It tells you when to buy advice and never stands in for it.

| Flag | The risk, in one line | Your default | Get advice when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Payment processor and disputes | At solo volume one dispute can breach a processor's tolerance, near one in a hundred transactions, and a closed account can follow you | A descriptor in your brand's name; at most one dispute per rolling 90 days early; refund before he disputes (Module 7) | A processor warns you, holds funds, or questions your business model |
| Installment plans as consumer credit | Where local law counts installment plans as credit, even your own plan can carry licensing or disclosure duties | Your own installments only, at most three, inside delivery; no lender or buy-now-pay-later | Before you offer a plan, and again for each new country you sell to |
| Health-adjacent advertising and claims | Structural or medical claims, testimonials without typical context, and condition names can bring ad rejections, a health label on your whole site, and regulators | Process language; outcome claims only from your records, with range and denominator; no condition names in URLs, events, or ads | Before your first paid ad, your first published outcome range, or a product name |
| Minors and platform policy | In self-selected samples most of the category's keenest audience is under 18, adulthood starts at 19 in two US states and 21 in one state and one territory, and platforms restrict appearance-comparison content for teens | The age fork; the education lane; delete and refund when a minor surfaces | A platform strikes or age-restricts your account, or a parent writes to you |
| Data privacy | Health-adjacent answers, captures, and messages are sensitive data, and some places protect face data specially | The retention map and consent log; captures coach-only; no answers in pixels; no tool that measures faces | Before any tool touches check-ins or captures, and before your first client in a new region |
| Statutory cancellation | UK and EU consumers can cancel online services within about 14 days, with rules on early starts, and subscriptions carry renewal rules in many places | Terms that state those rights ahead of your own fit window; the Hold reminds before each charge, with a one-click exit | Before your terms go live, and before selling a subscription in a new country |

### The register says when to pay for advice

Read the register at your quarterly Operator Review, and whenever you add an offer, a tool, a country, or a paid channel, since each of those can move a flag. Advice costs less at the trigger than after the problem, and in this category a problem often arrives as a screenshot before it arrives as a letter. Keep the register to six. A seventh flag earns a row only when your own business creates a risk this size, and it goes on with its advice trigger the same day.

## Worked Example: Cole's [29]-Hour Growing Week, Redesigned

Cole is Growing in month [15], with about [22] concurrent clients across three monthly starts, the Program at [$2.4k], and eligible leads near [90] a month. Nothing broke. His weeks crept from [22] to [29] hours over [a quarter], and his last long-form piece went up [three] weeks ago.

**Week 1. The log.** He logs a fortnight by line against the Growing column. Five lines carry the overrun: replies and DMs at [4.5] hours against 1.0, review at [6] against 3.75, short-form with X and Instagram posts at [3] against 1.25, conversations at [4.5] against 3.0, and no-shows and follow-up at [1.25] against 0.5. Long-form sits at [1] against 3.5, and his building hour goes to [a website redesign] while the Build Queue hasn't moved in [five] weeks. His content lines still total [9.5] hours, so the total looks healthy while the trust engine starves.

**The read.** Little of it is new work. It's unpriced minutes: [a dozen] face-read requests a week answered one at a time, [four] fit conversations running past [an hour] each because nothing time-boxed them, [two] no-shows each rebooked twice, and client questions answered the moment they arrived. That's a design problem, so the fix is design, and his price stays out of it.

**Week 2. Cut in order, then price the leaks.** Step 1 pauses the redesign. Step 2 drops X, then his Instagram posts, keeping the Keyword Route. Step 3 brings short-form back to its 1.25 hours of clips and a few native pieces. The same week, fit conversations go back to 20–30-minute slots, which returns their line to its 3 hours with the Call Cap untouched. DMs move into a fixed daily slot with templates by case, so the face-read request gets one plain line and the door link. The no-show rule replaces his double rebookings, and client questions wait for the next check-in.

**Week 3. The minimum first.** Long-form comes back with [two] pieces batch-filmed on [Monday], because the minimum is the line the overrun ate, and it's next month's leads. Live work moves into [two] windows, [Tuesday afternoon] and [Thursday evening]. With long-form back in it, the week lands near [24] hours, so steps 4 and 5 are never needed.

**Week 4. Handed off.** Routing help starts on a task-billed batch [twice a week], working from the brief below: comment moderation and the templated door link, nothing more. Minors, distress, purchase questions, DMs, and every client stay with Cole. His editor takes the clipping.

**Weeks 5–10. Queued.** The build line comes back at 1.25 hours and goes to the Build Queue's first item. With [60-plus] clients behind him, he knows what a good review says, so he writes the template and adds the tool line and consent step to his written terms. Review falls from [about 15] minutes per client-week toward [about 8].

**Left alone.** His price stays on its step schedule. Group calls, milestones, turnaround, and Hold deliverables never moved, and he added neither a third live window nor a helper for DMs.

**Week 10. The outcome.** His logged week sits at [about 21] hours, with content at its 6.75, review near [3], and conversations near [3]. Eligible leads dipped to [about 70] in the month after the gap, then climbed back to [85–100] as the cadence held.

**What it shows.** An overloaded week is usually a design problem, and the order of repairs matters. Cutting down the De-Scoping Order and pricing the leaks made room, the content minimum took that room before anything else, and help arrived only after the design was fixed. The one saving that compounds came from the build line, which is why no step of the order touches it.

## Checklists: The De-Scoping Card and the Risk Register Review

### The de-scoping card

Run it the Monday after any week past 25 hours, from last week's log.

1. Did last week run past 25 hours? → Yes: work down this card this week.
2. Is anything beyond your build line, or any experiment, on this week's list? → Pause it.
3. Are you publishing natively anywhere beyond long-form, short-form, email, and the Keyword Route? → Drop X, then native Instagram.

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** Reach binds, so content takes the biggest share of the week and conversations the next. The default move is to block the short-form batches and your live windows first, then spend the build line on the door and the founding assets. The trap is answering every comment and DM at length because volume is low, since those habits become the unpriced minutes that sink a Growing week.

**Growing.** Selling minutes and untemplated review bind together, which makes this the heaviest design, about 22 hours with the thinnest slack. The default is to hold the build line through bad months and bring in routing help once its trigger fires. The trap is skipping the build line to survive a heavy month, which keeps every later month heavy.

**Scaling.** Care minutes bind, yet the week falls to about 20–21 hours because templates do the work. The default is to hand derivatives out, approve every claim yourself, and read two weeks at step 5 as a price and seat decision. The trap is letting routing help drift into DMs because it's quicker, which puts minors, distress, and purchase questions in front of someone who can't hold those lines.

## Standard Check

- **Claims never leave your hands.** Every title, caption, thumbnail, and clip a freelancer makes passes your approval before it ships, and no tool makes or edits a face or a result (the line on structural claims; the line on fabricated proof).
- **Routing help routes, and that's all.** It moderates against the published policy and posts one templated link. It never sells, makes a claim, works a DM, or sees a check-in, and anything touching a minor or distress comes to you (the line on vulnerability).
- **Consent before any tool.** The AI rule sits in his written terms, no tool processes a check-in without his recorded consent, and captures stay coach-only (the house rules on AI and privacy).
- **Overload cuts selling last.** Building, platforms, and extra content go before any conversation, and at step 5 warm pages keep their prices and checkouts, so a good-fit buyer still meets the offer (*Sell directly*).
