# 7 · Price, Plans and Promises
*Part II — The Offer System*

**The shift:** from *"Price by gut, discount to close, and treat guarantees as a risk"* to *"Price sits under three ceilings and steps up on a schedule, plans are priced at cost, and I guarantee what I control, loudly and collectably."*

Cole *(composite operator)* has a full [October] start: [8] seats taken and [3] names on the waiting list. His [December] start is priced [about 8%] higher, and the whole case for that step fits in three lines:

1. Before the step: [8] seats × [$1.9k] = [$15.2k] a start.
2. After it: [8] seats × [$2.05k] = [$16.4k], because the [one or two] buyers the step loses are replaced from the waiting list.
3. What the extra [$1.2k] costs him: processing, about [$40]. The rest is profit.

At capacity, a step that loses a smaller share of buyers than its percentage flows almost entirely to profit, because the seats still fill. The same step in a half-empty month is a different bet. A discount is a bad bet in every month, because in this category a slashed price and a countdown are how the grifters sell.

Three limits decide how far that arithmetic can run: what the core buyer can pay from his own pocket, what your minutes must earn, and what a skeptic will believe before you have proof. Two more decide whether a good-fit buyer can say yes at all and still endorse it a year later: how he pays, and what you promise if it goes wrong. This module sets all five.

---

## 1. Price as Lever and Signal

Price does two jobs in this business, and which one it's doing depends on whether your seats or your buyers run out first. At a 75–85% margin nearly every extra dollar of price is profit, so a 1% raise lifts profit by about 1.2–1.3% if volume holds. Everything turns on that last condition. Volume holds when the buyers a step loses are replaced, and it breaks when they leave an empty seat behind.

### A full start turns a step into profit

When seats bind, the start fills before its date and a waiting list forms behind it. The buyer a step loses is replaced by the next name on the list, so volume holds by construction and the step's whole percentage lands as profit. When buyers bind, the start opens with empty seats, and the man a step loses leaves one of them empty. He takes more than his fee with him: Round Two, the lighter maintenance block some graduates buy, the Hold, the referral he'd have made, and his place in the record you publish.

| | Seats bind | Buyers bind |
|---|---|---|
| Signs | A waiting list; starts full before their date; review turnaround near its stated limit | Open seats on start day; eligible leads under your band's waypoint; a close rate in range on too few conversations |
| What a step does | Lost buyers are replaced from the list, so nearly all of it is profit | Each lost buyer leaves a seat empty and takes his lifetime value with him |
| The move | Step on schedule | Announce no new step until starts fill; fix conversations or reach first |

The niche makes the second column bite harder. The core buyer feels price in months of take-home pay rather than as a share of a salary, so near the top of your range each step asks more of him than its percentage suggests. A step a wealthier audience would absorb can push a 23-year-old from paying out of savings to reaching for credit. The affordability question, the one-sentence check before every paid step that he can pay from his own income or savings without new credit, turns that reach into a lost sale. That's the right outcome for him, and it's why you read which column you're in before you step.

### Price is a weak signal of quality

Buyers do read price as a hint of quality, but the effect is weak to moderate, and it shrinks as a buyer gets to know a category. Your buyer knows this one too well. He has seen weekly rating apps, one-off "analysis" reports, coaches billing monthly for promises about bone, and devices sold on before-and-afters, and he has probably paid for at least one of them. A high price with nothing visible behind it reads as the pattern he's trying to escape, and a low one reads as another e-book.

What moves him is what the price visibly buys: delivery units he can count, terms he could collect on, and a record he can check. Price can't carry credibility here, so credibility has to carry price. That's why the higher band waits for proof, and why every raise names what it added.

**At capacity, price is the profit lever; before proof, it's a weak signal that only your record can back.**

One move fails both jobs: discounting to close. When seats bind, it gives away profit you'd have kept, and when buyers bind, it teaches the next buyer to wait. Buyers who later learn that someone paid less for the same seat also tend to buy less from that seller afterward, and the best customers pull back most. A founding price with its next price printed beside it is a real price. A price that drops for the man who hesitates is a discount.

Get the two jobs straight and price becomes the calmest lever you have. Small, scheduled, and announced in advance, it adds profit in full months and costs nothing in trust, because every buyer saw it coming.

## 2. The Three Ceilings

Every price you set has to fit under three separate limits, and in this niche each one binds at a different stage of the business. Most operators set price from a single number: a rival's rate, a figure that feels premium, or a salary that makes any program look small. Each of those ignores two of the three limits, and the one it ignores is usually the one that binds.

### Cash is measured in months of take-home

The first limit is his pay. Full-time men aged 20–24 earn about $42k a year, roughly $2.8k a month after tax, and full-time workers aged 25–34 earn about $59k, roughly $3.9k a month. A price that looks modest against a year is most of a month against his account:

| Program price | Months of take-home at 20–24 | Months at 25–34 | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|---|
| $2.4k | ~0.86 | ~0.61 | Bottom of the proof band; inside both ceilings |
| $2.8k | ~1.0 | ~0.71 | Inside both |
| $3.1k | ~1.1 | ~0.79 | Top of the proof band; near the 20–24 ceiling |
| $3.5k | ~1.25 | ~0.89 | The 20–24 ceiling's edge |
| $3.9k | ~1.4 | ~1.0 | Past the 20–24 ceiling; inside 25–34 |
| $4.5k | ~1.6 | ~1.14 | Past the 20–24 ceiling; inside 25–34 |

The cash ceiling is the most a core container may cost the buyer it's built for: about 1–1.25 months of the core buyer's take-home, payable from income or savings without new credit. That's roughly $2.8–3.5k at 20–24 and $3.9–4.9k at 25–34. Past it, most core buyers can pay only by borrowing, which the affordability question rules out, so the ceiling is where your price and your own eligibility rule agree. Installments follow the same logic and stay at or below about a third of monthly take-home. Three installments on a $2.8k price are each about a third of a 20–24-year-old's month, so the plan rule and the ceiling meet at the same place.

### Capacity sets how far price has to climb

The second limit is your week. Care minutes, the review, group-call, and milestone minutes each client takes per week, cap how many clients you can serve. At Growing's 18–20 minutes per client-week, your Capacity Ceiling, the number of concurrent clients your care hours allow, sits around 25–30, or 8–10 new enrollments a month. $25k of profit needs about $29–38k collected, and at that ceiling the Program gets there only near $3.1k, the top of the proof band, with the back end doing the rest.

So capacity works as a ceiling on volume, and it pushes price up from below. A price far under that level can fill every seat and still cap the business short of its target, which is why founding and opening prices are stages to step through, never a place to stay. Measured per care hour, a Program seat earns about $800 at proof prices in Growing and about $1,250–1,400 at the ceiling in Scaling, and every other offer you sell is judged against that yield.

### Credibility is what a skeptic believes before proof

The third limit is belief. Before you have graduates, a buyer can check only your standards: the sample plan, the terms, and the Honest Answer, your straight public answer to the bone question. A price above what those can carry reads as grift to a man who has been sold to before. That's why the Program, your 12-week flagship, opens at $1.5–2.2k, and why the proof band, $2.4–3.2k, waits for the proof milestone. Credibility rises as the rest of the system does its work: a guarantee honored in public, written plans, ranges with denominators, and months of a record anyone can check.

**Capacity decides how high price must climb; cash and credibility decide how high it may.**

Those are the **Three Ceilings**. Early on, credibility binds and capacity doesn't, which is why founding and opening prices sit low on purpose. Later, capacity pushes price up through credibility's rising ceiling until it meets cash, the one ceiling your success never lifts, because his pay sets it.

Reid *(composite operator)* sets his first program at [$3.5k]. A rival bills [about $1k a month], and a higher price, he reasons, will read as serious. In his first month he holds [8] conversations and enrolls nobody, and the ceilings say why. On cash, $3.5k is about 1.25 months of take-home at 20–24, the ceiling's very edge, and [half] his buyers answer no to the affordability question. The two who ask about plans want [six] payments, a loan in all but name. On credibility, he has no graduates, no sample plan, and no ranges, so the price asks a burned buyer to believe what he can't check. Capacity, the one ceiling that could justify a high price, isn't binding at all: every seat is open.

He moves the price into the opening band at [$1.8k] before anyone has paid, prints the next steps beside it, and puts his sample plan and terms on the page. Had a buyer already paid $3.5k, the difference would have gone back to him first, because no buyer should learn he paid more than the next man for the same seat. [Two] starts later his close rate sits at [~24%], inside the 15–35% an offer without proof should hold.

### Set the first price one step above the founding price

Read all three ceilings when you set a price and again before every scheduled step. Cash comes from the table and moves only if your buyers' age mix does. Capacity comes from your monthly seat count. Credibility comes from your close rate against its range, and from whether the proof milestone is met. The ceiling that binds now sets the price, and the next one tells you how far the scheduled steps can run.

The founding group's price, a real price near the real one with the next price printed beside it, belongs to your founding phase (Module 9). The first Program price is one scheduled step above it, low in the opening band, and published with the step after it. Starting low leaves the steps room to run before the proof milestone, so price keeps rising on schedule instead of stalling at the band's top while you wait for graduates.

## 3. The Price Steps

Price rises in small steps on a published schedule, and every step you announce happens. Big, occasional raises and quiet discounts teach a buyer the same lesson: the number moves if you wait. In this category that lesson costs more than elsewhere, because moving deadlines and slashed prices are the grift's signature, and your buyer has watched both.

A small step that always happens does three jobs at once. It adds profit whenever seats bind. It gives every buyer a real date to decide by, since a price that changes on a known day is a fact he can plan around. And it stays below the share of buyers it would have to lose before it stopped paying, which keeps the opening's arithmetic working for you.

**Small steps on a published schedule, each naming what was added: the proof milestone opens the proof band, and the cash ceiling closes the top.**

Those are the **Price Steps**, and three rules run them.

### The schedule runs while starts fill and close rates hold

Plan steps of about 5–10% every second start. Before you announce one, check two conditions: the last start filled, and your close rate over the last 30 held conversations sits inside its range, 15–35% before proof and 25–45% with it. Then announce the step at least 30 days out. From that moment it happens, whatever the next start looks like, because a step that reverses teaches every future buyer to wait. What you gate is the announcement, never the step itself. If a start runs thin or the close rate leaves its range, the next announcement waits, and nothing already announced moves.

An announced step is also one of his decision points, the real dates every assessed buyer should meet within a few weeks (Module 8). So his written recap names today's price, the next one, and the date it changes, once, as a plain fact. A buyer who drifts past a step he never heard about was failed by your silence: you let him treat waiting as free when you knew it wasn't.

### Every step names what was added and cuts nothing

Each step names something real the next buyer gets that the last one didn't: [a second group-call time], [the capture kit's lighting guide], [a plateau guide in the Expectation Document], and after the proof milestone, the published ranges his week-6 read is set against. A step never travels with a cut to anything published, whether turnaround, reviews, calls, or re-captures. A raise that quietly moves turnaround from [48] to [72] hours is a cut dressed as a raise, and the buyer who saved your old page will find it.

Protect the people who already paid. Alumni keep the rates they joined at on anything they already hold, such as the Hold, the measurement subscription for graduates. A step never touches anyone already enrolled, and no new buyer pays less than recent buyers paid for the same seat.

### The proof milestone opens the proof band

Steps run inside the opening band until the proof milestone is met: at least 10 graduates with consented process testimonials, plus the first outcome ranges with their denominators, published as a labeled small sample. The standing public log starts later, at 30 graduates, as part of the Dated Record, the pre-committed log of process and outcomes you keep from month 1 (Module 3). The milestone gates the move into the proof band because that band charges for observed results, and a skeptic can weigh them only once ranges exist, low end included. However full your starts are, the price waits at the top of the opening band until the milestone is met.

The value you set beside the price has to be honest too. Honest anchors are ones he can check against his own situation; banned ones borrow force from something false or from his worst moment:

| Anchor | Use it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The real premium tier, shown first | Yes | Every tier is real and bought by someone, so the comparison is true |
| The delivery math: [12] written reviews, [12] group calls, the week-6 read, the week-12 re-assessment, three captures to standard | Yes | He can count it, and nothing is inflated |
| The value of months not wasted: another year of guessing beside a year where he knows | Yes | It prices his time from here on |
| Surgery or filler | Never | Implies coaching can stand in for clinical care, and draws the buyer most at risk |
| What he has already spent | Never | The recovery-pitch pattern; his past spending stays in the conversation as information, far from the price |
| Hourly rates | Never | An internal check only; it invites per-minute pricing |
| An invented "total value" or a crossed-out price | Never | Decoy arithmetic a skeptic spots at once |

On a call the same policy becomes a sequence: the real tiers premium first, one recommendation, the price stated once, then silence. That's Premium First, Price Once (Module 19).

**When the signals disagree.** Say your last two starts filled with a waiting list and your close rate sits near [~40%], high in its range with proof. Every sales signal says step. But the schedule's next step would take the Program from [$3.2k] to [$3.45k], a whisker under 1.25 months of take-home at 20–24, and the step after that would cross the cash ceiling. The ceiling wins, because it protects him, and a full calendar is evidence about your demand, never about his pay. Take the last step that fits, then stop stepping the core container for the buyer it was built for. From there, profit comes from the back end and templated review, or from crossing with a real tier kept under the ceiling. That second path, and the change in who buys that comes with it, is the Buyer-Mix Shift (Module 13), with the share of buyers answering no to affordability as its guardrail.

## 4. Plans at Cost

A payment plan decides who can say yes, so it's where the line on vulnerability, the rule against selling past a buyer's means or into his distress, runs through your checkout. Plans lift sales by roughly a fifth, and the lift comes mostly from the buyers least able to absorb the risk. For a 20–24-year-old paying about a month of take-home, a plan is the difference between a stretch he can carry and a debt he can't. The design question is how to keep the first without selling the second.

Two mechanisms shape the answer. Payments that fall due after the work has ended feel like paying for nothing, and plans that outlive delivery are the ones that go unpaid and end as disputes. A seller who earns on the plan itself also has a reason to sell more plans, which is the incentive the category's worst offers run on. So your plan earns nothing and ends before delivery does. Plans at Cost, the payment-plan rules that keep a plan from becoming a loan, come to six:

- At most three installments, all due inside delivery, so he never pays for weeks that are over.
- A premium of 0–5%, covering processing and the 3–8% of plan revenue that short plans tend to leave uncollected, stated as one total: "[$1.95k] in full, or three payments of [$670], [$2.01k] in total."
- Each installment at or below about a third of his monthly take-home, which is the cash ceiling split three ways.
- The affordability question, asked again word for word at the plan step, because the plan step is a paid step of its own and a no there ends money talk.
- No plan after a Fit Check signal. The Fit Check, the short plain-language check before any paid step, already pauses payment on a signal: nothing that day, adjusted expectations in writing, at least 72 hours to cool off (Module 5). A plan is the tool that lets a stretched buyer say yes, which is why a signal removes it first.
- Cancel-forward: a client who leaves after the fit window, under the exit right or the non-response clause, owes nothing further. Future installments stop, and whatever he paid for undelivered weeks comes back.

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Pay in full only | The fee is small or the product is short: the Decision Assessment, the Hold's monthly fee | Nothing, because there's no lump sum to split |
| A plan at cost that you run | The Program, Round Two, and the founding group | About 3–8% of plan revenue uncollected, plus retries to run |
| A regulated provider's plan | Only where local rules would treat your own plan as consumer credit | It is credit, so installments must still come from his income, and the provider's terms become part of your promise |
| Longer plans, or buy-now-pay-later at checkout | Never | Payments that outlive delivery, sold hardest to the buyer least able to carry them, against the affordability question's own words |

**Default: a plan at cost that you run, offered beside pay-in-full on every Program checkout.** It lets a buyer with a steady income but no lump sum pay from that income, which is exactly what the affordability question asks. Switch to a regulated provider only where the rules require it.

The rules are moving in one direction, toward treating split payments as credit, with affordability checks and clear totals. A short plan at cost, inside delivery, with its total stated and the question asked, sits on the right side of that direction. Where your buyers live decides the details, and those belong on your Risk Register, the short list of the business's biggest legal and platform risks (Module 11).

A request for more installments is information. A buyer who wants [six] payments is telling you the price is past his cash ceiling, so answer with the affordability question, word for word. If he says no, money talk ends, and he gets the Starter Path, the free sequenced path for anyone who shouldn't buy now, handed over once. A serial appearance purchaser, a man who has bought one product after another on installments, gets the same care as a signal: no plan and a cooling-off gap, because the next plan is the one that hurts him most.

## 5. The Layered Guarantee

A guarantee shows him, before he pays, that you'll keep the promises you control, and in this category it persuades only if he could actually collect it. Your buyer has read guarantees before. Most promised results on his face, left "results" undefined, and routed every claim through a call built to talk him out of it. He learned that a guarantee is the trap's last step, and a generous one from an unknown seller at a high price reads the same way.

Two properties make a guarantee believable here. It covers only what you control, so you can keep it for every client, including the one at the low end of the range. And honoring it costs you something visible, so a skeptic can watch it work. Build it in layers, because what can go wrong changes across twelve weeks: fit shows early, your delivery can slip in any week, his life can change midway, and at the end the lever may not have moved. Each layer pays in the currency of its own failure.

**Guarantee what you control, in layers that match what can go wrong and when, and write each one so a client who did the work can collect it.**

That's the **Layered Guarantee**, six layers in order:

| Layer | What it covers | What he gets | How he claims |
|---|---|---|---|
| Statutory rights | Whatever the law where he lives gives him; UK and EU consumers get ~14 days to cancel online services, with rules on early start | At least what the law says | However the law allows |
| The fit window | The first 14–21 days of delivery, counted from baseline day | A full refund | A written request; any conversation is optional and feedback-only, with no re-pitch |
| The service guarantee | Your inputs: each review inside the stated turnaround (e.g., 48–72 h), the written week-6 read, the week-12 re-assessment, Hold and Private deliverables | A credit or refund for each miss | Nothing: you log misses and apply them |
| The week-6 exit right | He did the work and asks to stop | A pro-rata refund of undelivered weeks (~half) | A written request at the week-6 read |
| The week-12 non-response clause | His markers haven't moved despite adherence at the agreed threshold | A cash partial refund (~25–50%), an honest verdict, and no Round Two offer | None: it's read from his record, and you raise it first |
| The plan-usefulness refund | The Decision Assessment's written plan wasn't useful to him | The fee back | A written request |

Never on the list: how he looks. A promise about his face is the one promise nobody can keep, which is why the grift made it and why you don't (the line on structural claims).

### Each layer answers one failure

**Statutory rights come first.** Never print "no refunds": for buyers in some countries it misstates the law, and every other layer sits on top of it.

**The fit window.** It counts from baseline day, the first day of delivery, because a buyer who paid [three weeks] before his start can't judge fit on a calendar that hasn't begun. Close it the day after his [third] weekly review, so he judges on delivered review rather than on your sales page. A written request is enough, and any conversation is feedback-only, with no re-pitch and no counter-offer. A refund he has to talk his way through is a sales call, and a burned buyer knows that pattern by its first line.

**The service guarantee.** Your inputs are written as points: turnaround in hours, the week-6 read and the re-assessment by dates, the Hold's quarterly review and Private's deliverables on their cards. Log each miss and apply the credit before he asks, since this is the one layer only you can see failing.

**The exit right.** Week 6 is when a new job or a move can change his life, and when some clients learn the rhythm doesn't fit them. A client who did the work and asks to stop gets back what he paid for the undelivered weeks, about half, because a clean exit costs less than a dispute and treats him better than a trapped seat. The conversation itself is the Honest Exit (Module 21).

**The non-response clause.** His markers are the changeable-column measures and thresholds agreed at baseline, designed with the Program's milestones (Module 6). They're never adherence alone, which would pay him for logging, and never appearance change, which would guarantee his face by the back door. What the clause pays is a real choice:

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| More weeks free | Never | Pays him in more of what didn't move |
| A cash partial refund at one fixed share inside 25–50% | By default, for every Program client | Real cash on each claim, and a claim rate you publish |
| A full refund | Never on this layer | Draws buyers who are buying the guarantee, and at solo volume a cluster of claims can empty a month |

**Default: one fixed cash share inside 25–50%, printed in the terms.** A stated number is collectable, while a share settled case by case leaves the payout to your discretion. Revisit it once 30 graduates give you a measured non-responder share.

**The plan-usefulness refund.** The Decision Assessment, the paid, credited written plan that ends in one recommendation, is sold as worth its fee to a man who never buys. If the plan wasn't useful to him, his written word is enough and the fee comes back, which is what makes that promise checkable.

### A client at the low end can still collect

Dan *(composite, Struggler)* joins the [March] start. In week [4], one review lands at [80] hours against a stated [72], and a [one-week] credit appears on his account before he notices. At baseline you agreed two markers, [a body-composition measure] and [a posture-habit measure], each with a threshold, plus an adherence threshold of [80%] of days logged. By week 12 he has logged [74 of 84] days, well above it, and the re-assessment shows both markers short. You raise the clause before he asks: the stated cash share, paid within [5] working days, the verdict that this lever isn't moving for him on this plan, and no Round Two offer. He leaves with his money and with the knowing the program promised: which lever doesn't move for him, and what to stop buying. That's a client at the low end of the range who can still say the business served him.

State every layer before payment, in plain words, on the offer page and at checkout, and say it loudly in offer content, because a guarantee persuades only when he meets it before the price. Publish your claim rate, the share of clients who claimed each layer, in your public log every month. A policy page is a cheap signal, required but easy to fake, while a guarantee honored in public with its claim rate is a costly signal, one a grifter can't afford to send (Module 15).

No consistency device, testimonial ask, or written reason is ever timed to his refund rights. The first testimonial ask waits until the fit window has closed, the reasons he writes at baseline are never quoted back when he asks to leave, and no pledge ever trades away a refund.

### The Collectability Test

Read every clause as Theo *(composite, Burned Struggler)* would. He bought a device and a course that both promised structural change, and he reads terms before prices. Here's a clause he'd meet on plenty of coaching pages:

> "Complete every check-in, and if you don't see results by week 12, we'll extend your coaching free until you do."

He'd take it apart a line at a time. "Every check-in" means one missed week voids it. "Results" is undefined, so you decide whether it pays. "Extend your coaching" pays him in more of what didn't work, and "until you do" never ends, so it never has to pay at all. Could a client who did the work actually collect? That question is the **Collectability Test**, and it checks five things:

1. The trigger is a number in his own record: days logged, and markers against the thresholds set at baseline.
2. The record decides, never your judgment of his effort or his face.
3. The claim is a written request, and any conversation is optional.
4. The payout is cash or a stated credit, on a stated timeline.
5. Nothing is traded for it: no testimonial, no silence, no extra hoop.

> "If you log at least [80%] of days and your two markers, set with you at baseline, haven't reached their thresholds at the week-12 re-assessment, you get [a fixed share inside 25–50%] of your fee back in cash within [5] working days. You don't need to ask; we'll raise it."

Theo can collect that one, so he can believe it. Run the test on every clause before it's published, again whenever a step changes the page, and on every claim you pay, because a clause that's collectable on paper and slow in practice fails it too.

## 6. Refunds, Disputes, and Cash

At solo volume, refunds and disputes are counted one at a time, and cash is managed so that one bad month can't end the business. Processors watch dispute ratios at around one in every hundred transactions, and with a few dozen charges a month a single dispute can breach that. So track the count: early on, at most one dispute in any rolling 90 days, each one investigated. Refunds and disputes together plan at about 2–8% of revenue, lower when the guarantee is collectable, onboarding sets expectations, and the affordability question is asked every time.

### A refund offered beats a dispute filed

Disputes here mostly say one of two things: "I don't recognize this charge" or "I didn't get what was promised". The category made the second one every buyer's first suspicion, which is why a guarantee he can claim by a written request is also your dispute policy. A man who can get his money back from you rarely asks his bank. Keep a record of his acceptance of the terms at checkout and on baseline day, so a dispute that does come can be answered from the record.

The first complaint is a naming problem. Your billing descriptor, the name on his card statement, should be your brand's name: recognizable to him, and never a holding company he's never heard of or words about faces and jaws. Discretion is part of the product, since many buyers keep this pursuit private, but a charge he can't recognize is a dispute waiting to be filed.

### Open early, hold reserves, keep other income

Open your payment processor in the first weeks with small charges, such as Decision Assessment fees and the founding group's first payments, so it sees a clean history before larger ones, and ask how it handles reserves on programs delivered over months. Hold about 2–3 months of costs in reserve, because refunds cluster after a weak start and a processor can hold funds without warning. Keep your other income until your trailing 3-month profit covers your personal costs.

That last rule protects the standard as much as the budget. An operator who needs this month's enrollments to pay his rent will feel his hand drift toward the discount, the longer plan, and the second ask after a clear no. Refund and dispute counts sit among your guardrails, the numbers you watch at every stage whatever else is growing (Module 12), and the reserve is what lets you honor every claim on the day it arrives.

## Worked Example: Cole's Twelve Months of Price

Cole starts in January with a door, a founding page, and his guarantee and plan terms written before his first price. His channel grows steadily, the kind of year to plan on. Here is his pricing year at eight decision points.

**Month 0. The first price.** No graduates, no ranges, open seats: credibility binds, and neither capacity nor cash does. He prices the founding group at [$1.5k], with [$1.6k] printed beside it for the [April] start. He opens his processor with the first Decision Assessment fees and sets his brand's name as the descriptor. All six guarantee layers and the plan terms go on the page: three payments of [$515], [$1.55k] in total. Left alone: a "premium" price to look serious, and any bonus.

**Month 2. A plan declined.** Sam *(composite, Ambivalent)*, 22, an apprentice electrician, fits on most counts, but one Fit Check answer shows a signal: he's saving for [a clinical procedure] and wants coaching first. The pause runs as written: nothing paid that day, expectations adjusted in writing, including that coaching never stands in for clinical care, and 72 hours to cool off. When he comes back asking for three installments, Cole declines the plan by the rule, in one plain line, with no label attached. Sam says "after the busy season", takes the Starter Path, and leaves with the next start and its price in writing.

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** Credibility binds your price, and cash flow binds you. The default move is a founding price with the next one printed beside it, steps that begin once starts fill, and every guarantee layer published from the first client. The trap is pricing high to look serious: with no graduates, a high price reads as the grift your buyer is escaping, and open seats mean capacity can't justify it.

**Growing.** Conversion binds, so every step has to keep your close rate inside its range. The default is a step every second start, each naming what was added, with the proof milestone opening the proof band. The trap is letting one slow start cancel a step you already announced: a single start is too small to read, and a reversed step teaches every buyer after it to wait.

**Scaling.** Care minutes and the cash ceiling bind together. The default is to hold the core container at the ceiling for the buyer it was built for, and to take profit from two real tiers, the back end, and templated review. The trap is a longer plan that makes a price past the ceiling look affordable, which turns the ceiling into a loan.

## Standard Check

- **A price page with nothing invented.** Real tiers, premium first, delivery units he can count, and no decoy, crossed-out price, or "total value" (*Present the price*). Every step names what was added and cuts nothing published, and an announced step always happens (*Use real dates*).
- **Plans that stop at his means.** At most three installments inside delivery, at cost, each under about a third of his take-home, with the affordability question at the plan step and no plan after a Fit Check signal (the line on vulnerability).
- **A guarantee he can collect.** Every layer passes the Collectability Test, the claim rate is published, and no testimonial ask or written reason is timed to his refund rights. Nothing is ever guaranteed about his face (the line on structural claims).

## Quick Reference

**In one line.** Price under three ceilings, step it on a schedule you keep, finance it at cost, and guarantee what you control in layers a client who did the work can collect.

**Takeaways**
- At capacity, a step that loses a smaller share of buyers than its percentage is almost pure profit; with seats open, each lost buyer takes his lifetime value with him.
- Capacity decides how high price must climb; cash and credibility decide how high it may.
- Steps of about 5–10% every second start, announced 30+ days out and always kept, each name what was added; the proof milestone opens the proof band.
- Plans: at most three installments inside delivery, 0–5% stated as a total, cancel-forward, none after a signal.

*The Three Ceilings*

| Ceiling | The number | Binds when | The move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | ~1–1.25 months of take-home: ~$2.8–3.5k at 20–24, ~$3.9–4.9k at 25–34; installments ≤ ~⅓ of a month | Near the proof band's top | Stop stepping the core container; a real tier under the ceiling |
| Capacity | ~25–30 concurrent in Growing; ~40–45 in Scaling | A waiting list | Step on schedule |
| Credibility | Opening band $1.5–2.2k until the proof milestone; proof band $2.4–3.2k after | Early, or when a step drops close rates below range | Proof, terms, the record |

*The guarantee layers*

| Layer | Pays | When |
|---|---|---|
| Statutory rights | What the law gives | Always, first |
| Fit window | A full refund | 14–21 days from baseline day |
| Service guarantee | Credit or refund per miss | Any week |
| Exit right | Pro-rata for undelivered weeks | Week 6, if he did the work |
| Non-response clause | Cash ~25–50%, an honest verdict | Week 12, markers unmoved despite adherence |
| Plan-usefulness refund | The assessment fee | If the plan wasn't useful |
