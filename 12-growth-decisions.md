In [April] one more yes came fast, so he announced another, and in [May] a buyer called the price fair, so he announced a third. That's [three] steps in [two] months, each announced [30] days out, all resting on [four] calls. He wanted to walk the price back, and his own terms ruled it out: no new buyer pays less than recent buyers for the same seat.

Every step felt like reading the data, and none was. Four calls can't tell a price that fits from a good week. In this category a man's yes turns on whether payday has landed, and on whether last weekend's group photo is still on his mind. Reid didn't lack tracking; he had spreadsheets. He lacked three things: a short list of numbers worth reading, a rule for when a ratio may change his mind, and a fixed day for decisions. ## 1. Stage Metrics and Guardrails

At each stage, a handful of numbers tells you whether the business is working. Nearly everything else you could track follows from them or counts people who can't buy. This category offers more numbers than most, and the loudest point the wrong way. Views, followers, and likes climb fastest on rating and comparison content, which pulls the youngest viewers. Raw leads include every door start that ends at the age fork, your door's legal-adult question. None of these is a term in the equation that makes profit.

Each stage needs its north star, the few numbers that move first when the right lever works, and one that shows whether delivery holds. The binding constraint picks them. Early, reach and then conversations bind, so the list counts conversations and the cash they become. Growing, conversion binds, so it adds the rates that show where eligible leads, adults past your age fork who could pay without new credit, get lost. Scaling, care minutes bind, the review, call, and milestone minutes each client takes a week, so the list turns to profit per hour and the waiting list. Beside every list sit the guardrails, numbers you watch at every stage and never push.

**Five numbers early, at most six at any stage, and guardrails always.**

That's **Stage Metrics**. The early five are counted weekly: eligible leads, held conversations, enrollments with the cash they bring, pieces published, and check-in completion. The first two show reach working where it pays, and enrollments with cash show whether conversations become a business. Pieces published is the one input fully in your hands while reach binds. Check-in completion, the share of client-weeks with a check-in received, is your first read on the product. A client who stops checking in has stopped building the record your review reads.

Each stage, placed by trailing three-month revenue plus a volume signal, swaps in the numbers its constraint needs:

| Stage | Volume signal | The numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Early | < ~50 eligible leads/month; < ~15 concurrent clients | Eligible leads · held conversations · enrollments + cash · pieces published · check-in completion |
| Growing | ~50–150 eligible leads; ~15–25 concurrent | Eligible leads · eligible lead → enrollment · enrollments/month · revenue per eligible lead · renewal take · check-in completion |
| Scaling | ~150+ eligible leads; ~25+ concurrent, or a waiting list | Profit per operator hour · care minutes per client-week · enrollments/month · waiting-list depth · lifetime value per client · eligible-adult yield by source |

Swap sets at a quarterly review, never mid-month, so each set runs long enough to be read against its own ranges. When revenue and volume disagree, run the set for the constraint that binds, because the constraint decides which moves apply.

### Each guardrail sits where a shortcut would show

A guardrail earns its place by showing when a number was moved by a shortcut. Guardrails about people are also read by case, whatever the totals say: every refund, dispute, exit, and signal-flagged enrollee gets his file opened. Several watch the Fit Check, the plain-language check for warning signs before any paid step. A check that goes soft lifts conversion now and hands it back later as refunds and disputes. The eight run at every stage:

| Guardrail | Counted as, with its line |
|---|---|
| Refunds and disputes | Rolling count; ~2–8% of revenue; disputes ≤1 per rolling 90 days early |
| Complaints | Per delivered email over a rolling month: under 0.1%, and never 0.3% or more |
| Fit-check signals and declines | Signals and declines, in aggregate; ~5–20% of paid-step applicants signal |
| Refunds and exits among signal-flagged enrollees | Each case read, and the count set beside everyone else's |
| Promotional sends to paused leads | Any marketing send, counted weekly; target zero |
| Affordability "no" share | Share of paid-step applicants |
| Non-responder share | Graduates at or above adherence with no marker at its threshold; published at 30+ |
| Review turnaround kept | Share of reviews inside your stated point |

The affordability "no" share should hold steady as price steps up, so a climb after a step says the price has reached past the man it was built for. It counts paid-step applicants who answer no to the affordability question: "Is this comfortable from your own income or savings, without new credit or buy-now-pay-later?"

The zero protects the pause route, a content-free tag set by "I can't afford it", a distress answer, or a fit-check pause, which holds every marketing send and blocks checkout for 60–90 days, then asks permission once (Module 5). One send that slips through reopens a conversation he closed, and at solo volume a single one is a breach, never a rate.

### A number you chase stops measuring

Every number on these lists stands in for something you can't count directly: held conversations for buyers who need a decision, check-in completion for a record worth reviewing. Once the number becomes the goal, its cheapest route wins, and in this category the cheap routes are well worn. So each number is read beside whatever moves when that route is taken:

| Number | The cheap route | What catches it |
|---|---|---|
| Pieces published | Trend-chasing hooks | Eligible leads flat while pieces rise |
| Held conversations | Booking results the door marked "not yet" | Over half ending no-fit |
| Check-in completion | A one-tap check-in | Check-ins far under their ~10 minutes |
| Close rate, enrollments | A softer answer about bone; a softened check | Refunds and disputes; the fit-check signal count |
| Renewal take | Asking every graduate, momentum or not | Take above its range |
| Care minutes, profit per hour | Thinner review | Turnaround kept; the non-responder share |
| Revenue per eligible lead | Price past the cash ceiling | The affordability "no" share |

Reid takes his five early numbers and makes each one a target. For pieces published, he doubles his shorts on trend-chasing hooks. His count goes from [5] to [10] a week while eligible leads stay flat, because most new viewers are under 18 and leave at the age fork. For held conversations, he books every adult who finishes the door, including results marked "not yet". His count rises from [3] to [6] a week, and more than half end no-fit: men who wanted a promise about bone, or could pay only on credit.

For check-in completion, he trims the check-in to one tap, and completion climbs to [~95%] while his reviews have nothing left to read. By month [5], three of his five numbers show progress and his cash hasn't moved. Each rose by its cheapest route, and each route spent the thing the number stood for.

So chase none of them, except where a threshold carries a written move. The Founding Sprint, your labeled early sources, aims at 3–6 held conversations a week, and its week-3 source check acts on a miss (Module 9). The Call Cap, your weekly limit of about 6–8 free fit conversations, triggers the overflow, and sends to paused leads stay at zero. Read counts and guardrails weekly, and rates monthly, once a full window stands behind them.

## 2. Small Numbers Lie and Denominator Discipline

At solo volume, a rate built on a handful of events is mostly noise. So you decide by thresholds set in advance, and your own ratios replace the planning ranges only as events add up. The arithmetic is blunt. At four calls, each call is a quarter of the evidence, and one buyer who waits for payday moves your close rate further than its planning range is wide.

### A small window measures the calendar

This niche adds noise of its own, because its buyers decide in clusters. They come after payday, after a wedding or a work photo, around New Year, and in the days after a video answering the bone question reaches them. So a week's close rate measures your calendar as much as your call. The changes you want to detect, such as a Price Step of about 5–10%, are small beside that swing.

Two definitions make the rule usable. An event is one unit of whatever the rate is counted per. That's an eligible lead for eligible lead → held conversation, and a held conversation for the close rate. For the take of Round Two, the maintenance block some graduates buy, it's a graduate. A window is the stretch it takes to collect about 30 of those events. Windows run back to back without overlapping, and a window that judges a change starts the day the change reaches buyers.

**Plan on the range until about 30 events stand behind your own ratio; change course only on two consecutive windows outside it, or on a signal that names its cause.**

That's **Small Numbers Lie**. Thirty events can tell a rate inside its range from one well outside it, which is what a solo business needs. They can't rank two settings that sit close together. The second window matters because a healthy rate will read outside its range once, by chance, often enough to fool you within a year. Twice in a row, it rarely does unless something moved.

Single-digit rates need one more rule, because 30 events hold too few outcomes to read. At 1.5–7%, 30 eligible leads hold between none and two enrollments, so a healthy eligible lead → enrollment reads zero by chance all the time. Read it through its stages, which run high enough to count: eligible lead → held conversation at 10–20%, held conversation → enrollment at 15–45%, and the no-call route as a count against plan. Or wait for about 30 enrollments before reading the overall rate directly.

In [September], Cole *(composite operator)* rewrites the opening of his result page, the page every self-assessment ends on. His next 30 eligible leads bring no enrollments, and read directly, his conversion has collapsed. Read by stage, [five] of those 30 reached a held conversation, about [17%], and his no-call enrollments that month held at their usual count. The next window reads [4] of 30, about [13%], still inside 10–20%, so the page stands. The close rate on those conversations waits for its own 30, and the overall rate for 30 enrollments, about [six] months at his volume.

A signal is strong when it names a mechanism in buyers' own words, or repeats across people who don't know each other. It's also strong when it touches a line of the House Standard, the playbook's rules for persuasion and proof (Intro). [Three] refund requests quoting the same offer-page line as a promise about the jaw is one. So is a call where you hear yourself soften the Honest Answer, your straight public answer to the bone question, to save a sale.

Lines are counted in ones. A page that reads as a structural promise gets fixed the day you learn of it, with no window to wait for. The 30-event rule protects your reading of rates, never a promise you shouldn't have made.

The rule reaches price through the gate on each Price Step, the small raise on a published schedule (Module 7). The gate reads the close rate over the 30 held conversations since the last step was announced, when buyers first saw its price. So a thin fortnight can't cancel a step the gate calls for, and a hot week can't add one it doesn't. Until those 30 exist, filling starts carry the step alone, at the bottom of the range. Once the window is readable, one window below range holds the next announcement, and two send the work to the call.

The same rule sets what you publish. The non-responder share and the share told they don't need Round Two wait for 30 graduates, and the recommendation mix for 30 Decision Assessments, the paid written plans that end in one recommendation. Your first outcome ranges are the deliberate exception, going up at the proof milestone, 10 or more graduates with consented process testimonials, labeled as a small sample so a buyer can weigh them for what they are.

### Every rate keeps its denominator

A rate can move because the business moved, or because what it's counted per moved, and only a fixed denominator tells you which. Platforms redefine what they count, your door gains a question, and a video spreads among viewers too young to buy. Each shifts a ratio while the business stands still. So wherever you can, count each rate you steer by per something you own and count yourself: eligible leads at your door, held conversations on your calendar, graduates, emails delivered, hours worked.

| Rate | Count it per | Never per | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eligible lead → enrollment | Eligible-lead cohort | Views, followers, or raw leads | Raw counts swing with how young a month's viewers were |
| Close rate | Held conversation | Booked call | No-shows move bookings with your calendar, not your call |
| Lead yield | 1,000 engaged views, by source | Followers | The one borrowed count: date a break when a platform redefines it |
| Email results | Delivered | Opened | Mail apps inflate and hide opens |
| Round Two take | Graduate | Client offered it | Offered only on measured momentum, adherence met and a marker still improving, so per offer flatters |
| Profit per hour | Every operator hour, content and selling included | Delivery hours | Selling and content hours are where profit per hour leaks |
| Outcome ranges | Every graduate | Clients who agreed to share | The willing few flatter any range |

That habit is **Denominator Discipline**, written into each metric's name so a change in what's counted can't slip past you. The conversion you steer by has one name, eligible lead → enrollment, counted per eligible-lead cohort, because only your door decides who counts as eligible.

In [November], Cole's form tool updates and starts counting every door start as a lead, including the under-18 exits it never stores. Its lead → booked rate drops from [~15%] to [~7%] overnight. His own sheet counts only the adults his door stores past the age fork: [~26] that week and [4] held conversations, about [15%], unchanged. The tool's denominator moved and the business didn't, and read off the tool, the drop would have sent him to rewrite a working result page. He dates the break, starts a fresh window, and never splices the two series, because a spliced line manufactures a trend out of a definition.

## 3. The Operator Review

Decisions get made in one place, on a fixed clock, one at a time. A solo operator's worst decisions get made mid-week, in whatever mood the last conversation left. This business supplies strong ones: a buyer who calls your price a scam, a week with three yeses, a comment thread about your face. A fixed review separates noticing from deciding. Holding it to one decision keeps each change readable, because every change needs its own clean window before it says anything.

**Weekly counts, monthly rates, quarterly structure: one decision at each, and the rest left alone on purpose.**

That's the **Operator Review**. Its time sits in the admin line of the Design Week, your week designed line by line to about 20 hours (Module 11). The monthly level opens with the Monthly Constraint Read, the short checklist that names which of reach, conversations, or care minutes binds (Module 1). Each level reads only what its evidence can bear:

| Review | Reads | Decides | Never decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly | The stage's counts, every guardrail, turnaround, content hours against the minimum | One repair, or none | Anything that changes a joint |
| Monthly | Rates with a full window, the Monthly Constraint Read, the month's profit, the next start's seat cap, the month's week-6 and week-12 verdicts | One change at one unlocked joint, a due Price Step included, with two moves written down as left alone | A new rung, platform, or paid test |
| Quarterly | Stage placement, every route from reach to renewal, the triggers set for scaling moves, publications that fall due | One structural move, from the trace or a fired trigger | Anything no trigger or trace has called for |

### A decision is any change that touches a joint

A joint is any rate between reach and renewal. A decision is any change that could move one: a new script, a rewritten result page, a rung switched on, a scheduled step announced. A step is a decision even when a rule chose its timing, because it opens a window that has to be read. Two limits keep decisions readable. A joint with an open window is locked, so nothing else changes there until that window closes. And the quarterly review, which falls on the same day as that month's review, can add its one decision only at a different joint.

Repairs don't count. A repair puts back a rule you already hold, and it happens the day you find the break, whatever the clock says. Two ordinary weekly fixes show the difference. Content hours fell to [6] against the early minimum, so the lost filming block goes back on [Thursday] before anything new is added. One door completion waited [30] hours for a reply, so that day's reply batch moves up. A send to a paused lead is handled the same way: fixed that day, and never counted as a decision.

### A month in range still ends in a decision

Cole's [October] review, month [10] of a Band B year, finds every number in range. Content held 6.75 hours all four weeks. Eligible leads ran near [80], [~15%] reached a held conversation, the close rate since his last step was announced sits at [~28%], and check-in completion at [~87%]. The guardrails are clean, and no step falls due until [November]. The Monthly Constraint Read names conversations at about [3] held a week, far below the Call Cap's trigger, and September's result-page change has read in range for two windows, so it stands.

The pull is to spend the hour on a queued idea: [a shorter booking form] or [a new first welcome email]. Each would open a window over a joint that works, and a change made in a clean month spends the clean reads you have. So the month's decision is to keep, written in the closing line: no change · every joint's next window runs clean · left alone, the booking form and the welcome email · next check, the [November] review.

## 4. The Leak Trace

When a number disappoints, the fix lives at one joint. You find it by tracing each route from reach to renewal and pricing every gap in dollars. The reason is the Demand Equation, which makes profit the product of eligible adults, conversion, lifetime value, and margin, capped by care minutes (Module 1). A joint on the way in multiplies: a free-call joint running a third below plan costs a third of the enrollments that route produces. A back-end layer only adds. At the $25k configuration Round Two brings about $1.5k of roughly $31.5k, so the same shortfall there costs about $500 a month, yet proportion ranks the two level.

Buyers reach enrollment by three routes, and each is traced on its own. One is the free fit conversation. Another is the Decision Assessment, the paid written plan that takes the Call Cap's overflow, and the third is the no-call route through your offer page. They share the top, and the back end sits below all three:

| Route · joint | Planning range | Each one through is worth | Owner, and where its fix lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shared: engaged views → raw leads | 1–10 per 1,000 (plan 2–5 early) | Eligible share × revenue per eligible lead | Content's route to the door · Module 23 |
| Shared: raw leads → eligible leads | ~30–70% | Revenue per eligible lead | Packaging for adults · Module 23 |
| Free call: eligible lead → held conversation | 10–20% | Close rate × lifetime value | Speed to lead · Module 5 |
| Free call: held conversation → enrollment | 15–35% before proof; 25–45% with it | Lifetime value | The call · Module 19 |
| Assessment: eligible lead → Decision Assessment | 2–7% | The fee, plus 25–45% of lifetime value | The overflow offer · Module 5 |
| Assessment: Decision Assessment → program | 25–45% | Lifetime value | The written plan · Module 5 |
| No call: warm offer-page visitor → enrollment | ~1–3% | Lifetime value | Selling without the call · Module 20 |
| Back end: graduate → Round Two | ~10–26% | Round Two's price, ~$0.8–1.2k | The renewal ask at a measured peak, adherence met and a marker at threshold · Module 22 |
| Back end: graduate → the Hold, the measurement subscription | 20–40% | Its monthly price × the months members stay | The Hold's deliverables · Module 10 |

Price every gap the same way: the middle of the joint's planning range minus your rate, times the volume that reached the joint this month, times what each one through is worth. A back-end gap comes out as lost lifetime value per graduate, which you multiply by this month's graduates. Hold churn, typically 4–8% a month, is priced the same way, in months lost.

A joint at or above its midpoint isn't leaking. Read single-digit joints as counts against plan until 30 of their outcomes stand behind them. When the Call Cap binds, read the free-call and assessment routes as one at the lead end, since the cap moves buyers between them by design.

**Trace each route from reach to renewal, price every gap in dollars a month, and fix only the biggest.**

That's the **Leak Trace**. Three things stay out of its ranking. Refunds are a guardrail, because the cheap way to lower them is a guarantee that's harder to collect. Check-in completion and referrals have no planning range, so they're read and never ranked. Revenue per eligible lead, lifetime value times eligible lead → enrollment, spans the whole trace. Lifetime value itself is the LTV Stack's sum per client (Module 10). That's why revenue per eligible lead sits on the Growing list: it falls when any joint leaks, and the trace says which.

The call's joint leaks both ways. A close rate below its range can mean calls full of men your door should have routed elsewhere. It can also mean good-fit buyers who left without a recommendation, a stated price, and an ask. Guardrails catch the pushed sale, and the trace catches the un-asked one.

### A flooded joint isn't a leaking one

A joint can also fail the other way, by flooding. The Call Cap fires on volume: most weeks above about 6–8 held conversations, with selling eating your content hours. Then the fix is to cap free conversations and send the overflow to the Decision Assessment (Module 5), since tightening the door would plug a joint that's working. At lower volume, shows sliding toward about 60% or more than half ending no-fit call for the door's screening instead. A flood can also hide a leak upstream: a full calendar makes reach look fine while half of it is men who needed a different answer than a call.

Most leaks here sit early in the chain and look like manners. Dan *(composite, Struggler)* finishes your self-assessment at [11 pm] after a shift, the day after a team photo he didn't like. A reply within hours meets him while he still wants a decision; a reply [three] days later meets a man back in his routine. So check speed to lead first whenever eligible leads grow faster than your habits: a personal reply within hours, and a booking inside 24–48 hours.

Fix one joint at a time, through its owner's move, and trace again once its windows close. A repaired joint can move the biggest drop elsewhere. Faster replies send more men to your calendar, and the next trace may find the call is now the costliest gap.

## 5. Scaling Triggers

Every scaling move gets a trigger written before the evidence arrives, pointing to the module that owns the move. The moments that tempt a scaling move are the ones where judgment is worst. A short that spreads makes a new platform feel overdue, a waiting list makes a price jump feel safe, and a thin start makes paid reach feel urgent. A vivid week outweighs a quiet quarter in memory, so a trigger written in a calm month carries the quarter's judgment into that week.

Several of these moves sit near a line of the House Standard. Price past the cash ceiling, about 1–1.25 months of the core buyer's take-home, can reach past his pocket. Paid reach puts your claims in front of strangers, so its pages have to pass the claims rules first. A premium seat can take review minutes your Program clients paid for. So Private, the premium seat with fixed deliverables, follows the Parity Rule. At Scaling it sells only when it earns at least what a Program seat earns per care hour, and otherwise only while minutes are spare (Module 13). Every trigger therefore carries a check that can hold it.

**Write each trigger before the evidence: a signal, a threshold, a window, the move, its owner, and the check that can hold it.**

Those six parts make a trigger checkable on a bad week. Scaling Triggers, the signals set in advance for each scaling move, are read at the quarterly review. The Price Steps and the Call Cap ride the monthly review instead, because they turn on each start and each month's calendar. The owner teaches each move:

| Move | Fires when | Window | The check that can hold it | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Call Cap and the paid overflow | Most weeks above ~6–8 held, with selling eating the content minimum | Four weeks, at the monthly review | Profit per operator hour per 100 eligible leads, over ≥30 events at each setting | Module 5 |
| Switching on a rung | Its upstream count: the first graduation (Round Two, the Hold); ~20 graduates (paid tools); ~30 alumni (the alumni room); the Program past ~$3.2k (Program Async) | The count, read quarterly | Your week can price the rung's minutes | Module 4 |
| The next Price Step | Starts filling, and the close rate inside its range | 30 held conversations since the last step was announced | One window below range holds the next announcement; two send the work to the call | Module 7 |
| Templating review | ~20 clients reviewed, and turnaround creeping toward its stated point | Four weeks of turnaround | How reviews are made, published first; turnaround and the non-responder share hold | Module 13 |
| Private | Founding seats while minutes are spare; at Scaling, price per operator hour at or above the cohort's ~$1,250–1,400 per care hour | Monthly, with the seat caps | Review turnaround kept | Module 13 |
| Routing help, task-billed moderation and door links | Growing, with moderation and door-link replies overrunning the replies line of your Design Week | Most weeks of a quarter | One escalation slip pulls it back until the brief is fixed | Module 11 |
| Paid adult reach | The Month-3 Gate's volume leg, fewer than ~15 held conversations by week 12, with pages that pass the claims rules: a test at ~$300–1,000 a month from months 3–4. Otherwise only after the Ad Gates (compliant pages, organic proof, an offer that converts without ads) | About 30 eligible leads bought, against a holdout with washout weeks | Cost per eligible lead at or under your affordable maximum (~$10–70) | Module 27 |
| Adding or dropping a platform | Add: reach binds and the editing budget can serve it. Drop: before long-form degrades, or at the second cut of a week past 25 h | 30 eligible leads from its source label | Eligible-adult yield by source | Module 25 |
| Crossing the cash ceiling | All four: the Program at about $3.2k after the proof milestone; starts full with names waiting; the close rate inside 25–45%; the last ~30 enrollments mostly 25 or older | A 30-event window for each | The affordability "no" share per tier, two consecutive windows outside its prior band | Module 13 |

### Triggers are read at their review

Three rules keep the table honest. Read each trigger at the review it belongs to, never in the week it feels close, since a threshold read mid-week is the vivid week the trigger was written to outvote. When two fire together, pull the one that answers your binding constraint and let the other wait a window. And expect triggers to run backward: after a quiet quarter the Call Cap stops binding, and the overflow goes quiet by itself.

### Paid reach depends on your band

Paid reach is the one trigger that turns on your reach band, because the band decides whether organic reach can carry the plan. Bands are named at month 9, and at month 3 Band A, the channel that never breaks out, still overlaps Band B. So the signal is the Month-3 Gate's volume leg (Module 9), and the test it opens runs as a measurement buy.

A broad benchmark puts paid reach at about $36–100 per eligible lead, above what you can afford at opening prices, so you're buying your own real cost per lead. Pass the gate, and paid waits for the Ad Gates.

## 6. When the Signals Disagree

Two credible numbers can point opposite ways, and a short order of precedence decides between them before either becomes a move. Check both denominators first, since a changed denominator explains more disagreements than a changed business. Then guardrails outrank growth numbers. A leading number, one that moves before revenue does, outranks a trailing one only when its trend reaches your binding constraint before your next review of it.

**Denominators first, guardrails over growth, and a leading trend over a trailing total only once it arrives before your next look.**

### A full waiting list and a thinning pool

At Scaling, both of these can be true at once. Your waiting list holds [8] names, most of a start, so the Monthly Constraint Read names care minutes and the month's levers are price and templated review. Meanwhile eligible leads have fallen two months running, from about [190] a month to [130]. A run of comparison pieces pulled younger viewers and shrank the eligible share behind your yield-by-source line. The list says demand is fine, the count says it's leaving, and both numbers are honest.

The rule turns the argument into arithmetic: carry the lead count forward at your own conversion, and ask when the list runs dry. At [7%], [130] leads bring about [9] enrollments a month against [12] seats. The list drains by about [3] a month and empties in under [three] months, before your next quarterly review. Packaging for adults becomes that quarter's structural move, while the month's lever stays on care minutes. Had the list outlasted the quarter, the count would stay a watched number and nothing would move yet.

### A close rate can rise on refunds

A close rate that climbs while refunds and exits climb behind it is selling past fit. The refunds are the rate's true value arriving late, most of them inside the fit window, the first 14–21 days of full refunds. The usual causes are a softer Honest Answer on calls, or Fit Check wording that drifted until fewer buyers show a signal. So a climb doesn't count until the refunds from its own starts are explained. Read refunds by start, check the signal count, listen to calls from the climb, recorded with consent, and put back whatever changed.

**When the signals disagree.** Say revenue has grown [three] months running and every number in your stage's set sits in range. But [2] of the [4] clients who enrolled after a fit-check signal have left early. One took a refund, and one used the week-6 exit right, the pro-rata refund for a client who did the work and asks to stop. Among everyone else it's [1] of [30]. Four is far too few for a rate, and Small Numbers Lie says the ratio proves nothing. The by-case rule says open the files anyway, and it wins, because each one is a person the check was built to protect.

If both files say the work asked more of their weeks than the written expectations warned, the note that follows a signal wasn't plain enough. Rewrite it to state the time the work takes and the plateau in plain words, beside the Starter Path, the free path for anyone who shouldn't buy now. Priced honestly, it may send [one] signal-flagged buyer a quarter to the Starter Path, about [$2.4k] of revenue, less than the two exits cost before any dispute.

If a file points at the check itself, a question worded so a signal slipped past, that's a line, fixed the day you read it. One case read closely can change a process; no single case can change a rate.

## Worked Example: Cole's Quarterly Review at [~$14k] a Month

Cole is [12] months into a Band B year, and Growing on both readings. He has trailing revenue of about [$14k] a month, [~110] eligible leads a month, and [20] concurrent clients. In month 11 he began templating his review, met the proof milestone, and announced his [January] step. On the last Friday of [December], the month's review and the quarter's share an afternoon.

**The numbers.** His Growing six, from the sheet he fills in by hand each week:

| Number | This quarter | Planning range |

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** Too few events bind every rate you'd like to read, so your five numbers are counts. The yardstick is your band's waypoints, the lead counts your reach band plans on at months 3, 6, and 9. The default is a weekly look and one monthly lever. The trap is publishing an early result. [Two] founding graduates whose markers moved can't make a range, and in this category a lone early win reads as the proof the grifters sell.

**Growing.** Conversion binds, and revenue per eligible lead is the number that catches it, because it falls whenever any joint between your door and the back end leaks. The default is one change per window and a full trace every quarter. The trap is celebrating more enrollments while revenue per eligible lead slides: growth bought with leads converted worse.

**Scaling.** Care minutes bind, so profit per operator hour becomes the number, with care minutes and the waiting list beside it. The default is to let price and templated review lift it while turnaround and the non-responder share hold. The trap is a profit per hour that rises because review got thinner, which the guardrails show before renewals do.

## Standard Check

- **Guardrails beside every sales number.** A close rate or enrollment count is always read beside refunds, disputes, and fit-check counts, so chasing it can't become a softened check or a second ask after a clear no (*Close*; the line on vulnerability).
- **People read by case.** Every refund, exit, and signal-flagged enrollee gets his file opened, and a send to a paused lead is fixed the day it's found, because Stop Rules bind automation too (the line on vulnerability).
- **Shares published on schedule.** The non-responder share, the recommendation mix, and the "you don't need Round Two" share go up at 30, with denominators, whatever they show, and first outcome ranges carry a small-sample label (*Name the destination boldly*; the line on fabricated proof).
- **Steps the gate calls for.** A thin fortnight never cancels an announced step, and a price never walks back, so the schedule stays plannable (*Present the price*).
