# 12 · Growth Decisions
*Part III — Start, Extend, Run, Grow*

**The shift:** from *"Track everything and grow by effort"* to *"A few numbers, fixed thresholds, and pre-set triggers."*

In [March], Reid *(composite operator)* raised the price of his 12-week Program after [two] buyers said yes in one week. In [April], [two] calls ended without a sale, so he cut the price back and added a bonus call to the next start. In [May], one buyer called the new price fair, so he raised it again. That's [three] changes in [two] months, each made with a number in hand, all resting on [four] calls. By June the clients in his founding group, the one group every early client joins, had paid [three] prices for the same seat. The one who noticed asked, reasonably, which of them was real.

Every change felt like reading the data, and none was. Four calls can't tell a wrong price from a slow week. In this category a man's yes turns on whether payday has landed, and on whether last weekend's group photo is still on his mind. Reid didn't lack tracking; he had spreadsheets. He lacked three things: a short list of numbers worth reading, a rule for when a ratio may change his mind, and a fixed day for decisions. This module gives you all three, plus a trigger written in advance for every scaling move.

## 1. Stage Metrics and Guardrails

At each stage, a handful of numbers tells you whether the business is working. Nearly everything else you could track either follows from them or counts people who can't buy. This category offers more numbers than most, and the loudest point the wrong way. Views, followers, and likes climb fastest on rating and comparison content, which pulls the youngest viewers. Raw leads include every door start that ends at the age fork, your door's legal-adult question. None of these is a term in the equation that makes profit, so an operator who steers by them feels busier every month while the money stays put.

Each stage needs its north star, the numbers that move first when the right lever works, and one that shows whether delivery holds. The binding constraint picks them. Early, reach and then conversations bind, so the list counts conversations and the cash they become. Growing, conversion binds, so it adds the rates that show where eligible leads, adults who passed your age fork, get lost. Scaling, care minutes bind: the review, call, and milestone minutes each client takes a week. So that list turns to profit per hour and to the sources that still yield adults. Beside every list sit the guardrails, numbers you watch at every stage and never push.

**Five numbers early, at most six at any stage, and guardrails always.**

That's **Stage Metrics**. The early five are counted weekly: eligible leads, held conversations, enrollments with the cash they bring, pieces published, and check-in completion. The first two show whether reach is working where it pays, as adults at your door and conversations that took place. Enrollments and cash show whether those conversations become a business. Pieces published is the one input fully in your hands while reach binds. Check-in completion, the share of client-weeks with a check-in received, is your first read on the product itself. A client who stops checking in has stopped building the record your review exists to read.

Later sets swap in rates as the events behind them add up:

| Stage | The numbers | Why this set |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Eligible leads · held conversations · enrollments + cash · pieces published · check-in completion | Reach, then conversations, bind; counts only, since no rate has enough events behind it |
| Growing | Eligible leads · eligible lead → enrollment · enrollments/month · revenue per eligible lead · renewal take · check-in completion | Conversion binds; revenue per eligible lead joins the door's rate to the back end |
| Scaling | Profit per operator hour · care minutes per client-week · enrollments/month · renewal and continuity take · referral rate · eligible-adult yield by source | Care minutes bind; what's left to move is price, minutes, and which sources to keep |

Swap sets at a quarterly review, so each set runs long enough to be read. When revenue and volume place you in different stages, run the set for the constraint that binds, because the constraint decides which moves apply.

### Each guardrail sits where a shortcut would show

A guardrail earns its place by showing when a number on your list was moved by a shortcut. Most are counts, since one dispute or one sales email to a man who asked to be left alone matters on its own. Several watch the Fit Check, the plain-language check for warning signs before any paid step, because a check that goes soft lifts conversion now and hands it back later as refunds and disputes. The eight run at every stage:

| Guardrail | Counted as | What it catches |
|---|---|---|
| Refunds and disputes | Rolling count; ~2–8% of revenue; disputes ≤1 per rolling 90 days early | Enrollments bought by selling past fit |
| Complaints | Per delivered email: under 0.1%, never 0.3% | Sends pushed harder than the list wants |
| Fit-check signals and declines | Signals, and buyers declined or referred, in aggregate; ~5–20% of paid-step applicants signal | A check softened to lift conversion |
| Refunds and exits among signal-flagged enrollees | Counted beside everyone else's | Expectations after a signal that weren't adjusted enough |
| Promotional sends to paused leads | Count after every start; target zero | Automation reopening money talk, or selling into distress |
| Affordability "no" share | Share of paid-step applicants | Price reaching past the core buyer's own pocket |
| Non-responder share | Graduates whose pre-agreed markers didn't move; published at 30+ | Growth that brings buyers the lever doesn't move, or review thinned by volume |
| Review turnaround kept | Share of reviews inside your stated point | Seats sold past what your minutes can review |

Two need a word. The affordability "no" share counts paid-step applicants who answer no to the affordability question: can he pay from his own income or savings, without new credit? It should hold steady as price steps up, so a climb after a step says the price has reached past the man it was built for.

The zero for paused leads protects the pause route, the tag that stops sales and date sends for 60–90 days after a no, a distress answer, or a fit-check pause (Module 5). One send that slips through reopens a conversation he closed. Fit declines, turnaround kept, and the claim rate also go into the Dated Record, your pre-committed public log (Module 3), where a skeptic can check them.

### A number you chase stops measuring

Every number on these lists stands in for something you can't count directly. Held conversations stand in for buyers who need a decision, and check-in completion for a record worth reviewing. Once the number becomes the goal, its cheapest route wins. In this category the cheap routes are well worn: rating hooks for reach, a softer answer about bone for the close rate, a one-tap log for completion. The number keeps rising after it has stopped describing anything.

Reid takes his five early numbers and makes each one a target. For pieces published, he doubles his shorts on rating-style hooks. His count goes from [5] to [10] a week while eligible leads stay flat, because most new viewers are under 18 and leave at the age fork. For held conversations, he books every adult who finishes the door, including results marked "not yet". His count rises from [3] to [6] a week, and more than half end no-fit: men who wanted a promise about bone, or could pay only on credit.

For check-in completion, he trims the weekly check-in to one tap. Completion climbs to [~95%], and his reviews have nothing left to read. By month [5], three of his five numbers show progress and his cash hasn't moved. Each rose by its cheapest route, and each route spent the thing the number stood for.

So read the five and target none of them. A target belongs only inside a threshold that triggers a decision, such as the Call Cap, the weekly limit of about 6–8 free fit conversations. Read every growth number beside the guardrail that would catch its shortcut. Look at counts and guardrails every week, and leave rates to the monthly review, once a full window of events stands behind them.

## 2. Small Numbers Lie and Denominator Discipline

At solo volume, a rate built on a handful of events is mostly noise. So you decide by thresholds written in advance, and your own ratios replace the planning ranges only as events add up. The arithmetic is blunt. At four calls, each call is a quarter of the evidence, so one buyer who waits for payday moves your close rate further than its planning range is wide.

### A small window measures the calendar

This niche adds noise of its own, because its buyers decide in clusters. They come after payday, after a wedding or a work photo, and around New Year. They come in the days after a video answering the bone question lands in their feed. So a week's close rate measures your calendar as much as your call. The changes you most want to detect are small by comparison. A scheduled price step moves price by about 5–10%, and a better opening line moves the close rate a few points. Both hide easily inside one month's swing.

**Thirty events before your ratio replaces the range; two windows outside it, or a named cause, before you change course.**

That's **Small Numbers Lie**. An event is one unit of whatever the rate is counted per. For the close rate that's a held conversation, for lead → held conversation an eligible lead, and for renewal take a graduate. Thirty events can tell a rate inside its range from one well outside it, which is what a solo business needs. They aren't enough to rank two settings that sit close together. The second window matters because a healthy rate will read outside its range once, by chance, often enough to fool you within a year. Twice in a row, it rarely does unless something moved.

At the 3–6 held conversations a week the early months aim for, a 30-conversation window takes 5–10 weeks. That's why Early runs on counts and your band's waypoints while the planning ranges carry the plan. Once a window fills, your own ratio replaces the range in your plans. The range stays on as the tripwire: two consecutive windows outside it change what you do.

A signal is strong when it names a mechanism in buyers' own words, or repeats across people who don't know each other. It's also strong when it touches a line of the House Standard, the playbook's rules for persuasion and proof (Intro). [Three] refund requests quoting the same offer-page line as a promise about the jaw is one. So is a call where you hear yourself soften the Honest Answer, your straight public answer to the bone question, to save a sale.

Lines are counted in ones. A page that reads as a structural promise gets fixed the day you learn of it, with no window to wait for. The 30-event rule exists to protect your reading of rates, and it was never meant to protect a promise you shouldn't have made.

The rule cuts both ways at a Price Step, the small raise on a published schedule (Module 7). Its gate reads whether starts are filling and where the close rate sits across the last 30 held conversations. It never reads the last fortnight. So a thin fortnight can't cancel a step the gate calls for, and a hot week can't add one it doesn't. Skipping a due step because two calls went quiet turns a published schedule into a mood. Buyers who plan around your dates then learn that your dates move.

The same rule governs what you publish. The non-responder share waits for 30 graduates, and so does the share told they don't need Round Two, the maintenance block some graduates buy. The recommendation mix waits for 30 Decision Assessments, the paid written plans that end in one recommendation. A share from your first handful of clients would claim more than it knows. The one deliberate exception is your first outcome ranges. They go up at the proof milestone, 10 or more graduates with consented process testimonials, labeled as a small sample, so a buyer can weigh them for exactly what they are.

### Every rate keeps its denominator

A rate can move because the business moved, or because what it's counted per moved. Only a fixed denominator tells you which. Platforms redefine what they count, your door gains a question, and a video spreads among viewers too young to buy. Each shifts a ratio while your business stands still. So wherever you can, count each rate you steer by per something you own and count yourself: eligible leads at your door, held conversations on your calendar, graduates, emails delivered, hours worked.

| Rate | Count it per | Never per | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eligible lead → enrollment | Eligible-lead cohort | Views, followers, or raw leads | Raw counts swing with how young a month's viewers were |
| Close rate | Held conversation | Booked call | No-shows move bookings with your calendar, not your call |
| Lead yield | 1,000 engaged views, by source | Followers | Views are the one borrowed count, so date a break whenever a platform redefines them |
| Email results | Delivered | Opened | Mail apps inflate and hide opens |
| Round Two take | Graduate | Client offered it | It's offered only on measured momentum, so per offer flatters |
| Profit per hour | Every operator hour, content and selling included | Delivery hours | Selling and content hours are where profit per hour leaks |
| Outcome ranges | Every graduate | Clients who agreed to share | The willing few make any range look better than the program |

That habit is **Denominator Discipline**, and it lives in each metric's name, so a change in what's counted can't slip past you unnoticed. The conversion you steer by has one name, eligible lead → enrollment, counted per eligible-lead cohort. Sometimes a denominator has to change, because a platform redefines a view or your door adds a question. Date the break in your records and start a fresh window. Splicing the old series onto the new one manufactures a trend out of a definition.

## 3. The Operator Review

Decisions get made in one place, on a fixed clock, one at a time. A solo operator's worst decisions get made mid-week, in whatever mood the last conversation left. This business supplies strong moods: a buyer who calls your price a scam, a week with three yeses, a comment thread about your face. A fixed review separates noticing from deciding. Holding it to one decision keeps each change readable, because every change needs its own clean window before it says anything. Two changes made together leave you unable to tell which one worked.

**Weekly counts, monthly rates, quarterly structure: one decision at each, and the rest left alone on purpose.**

That's the **Operator Review**. Its time sits in the admin line of the Design Week, your week designed line by line to about 20 hours (Module 11). That week also holds the Protected Content Minimum, the content hours client work may never eat. The monthly level opens with the Monthly Constraint Read, the short checklist that names which of reach, conversations, or care minutes binds (Module 1). Each level reads only what its evidence can bear:

| Review | Reads | Decides | Never decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly | The stage's counts, every guardrail, turnaround, the content-minimum flag | One fix for the week, often none | Price, offers, platforms, or anything a rate would have to justify |
| Monthly | Rates with a full window, the Monthly Constraint Read, the next start's seat cap, the gate for any scheduled step | The month's one lever, with two moves written down as left alone | A new rung, platform, or paid test |
| Quarterly | Stage placement, the path from reach to renewal joint by joint, the triggers set for scaling moves, published shares that are due | One structural move, from a trigger that has fired | Anything no trigger has called for |

### The content minimum is checked first

The weekly review opens with one question: did content hours fall below your minimum? That's about 8.5 hours a week early, and never under 5 at any stage. Leads here trail content by weeks, because a long-form piece keeps routing adults to your door long after it's published. The piece you skipped never starts. So a week when client work ate the camera time shows up as a thin month later, long after you could have protected it.

Flag every week under the line, and put the hours back the next week before anything new is added. If client work keeps eating them, the fault sits upstream, in a seat cap set above what your minutes carry. Lower it at the monthly review. Working longer to cover both spends the slack your week keeps for bad weeks, so the next bad week eats the content again.

### A rule you follow isn't a decision

The month's one decision is the lever the Monthly Constraint Read points to. Everything else feeds that choice or runs by a rule already written. The next start's cap comes from last month's measured minutes, and a scheduled step goes out if its gate holds. So a month can carry a step and a lever and still make one choice. A decision is any change you'd have to judge on a fresh window: a new script, a price off the schedule, a rung switched on. Write it down with the rate and window that will judge it, the two moves left alone, and the date you'll look again.

Guardrail breaches don't wait for the clock. A send to a paused lead or a missed turnaround gets fixed the day you find it. The fix never counts as a review's decision, because repairing a line was never optional. Only the quarterly review may change the business's shape, since only a quarter holds enough events for stage placement and a full trace. It falls on the same day as that month's review, so the afternoon can end in two decisions, each made at its own level.

## 4. The Leak Trace

When a number disappoints, the fix usually lives at one joint between reach and renewal. You find it by walking every joint in order against its range. The reason is the Demand Equation, which makes profit the product of eligible adults, conversion, lifetime value, and margin, capped by care minutes (Module 1). Because the terms multiply, a joint running at two-thirds of its range costs a third of everything below it, wherever it sits. An hour spent anywhere else pours into the leak. A thin month is rarely fixed by more content or a sharper call until you know which joint dropped.

Each joint in this business leaks in a shape you can name in advance:

| Joint | Planning range | Owner, and where its fix lives | A leak here looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engaged views → raw leads | 1–10 per 1,000 (plan 2–5 early); short-form ~0.1–1 | Content's route to the door · Module 23 | Pieces that spread with no route to the self-assessment |
| Raw leads → eligible leads | ~30–70% | Packaging for adults · Module 23 | A rating or comparison piece fills the age fork's exit with under-18s |
| Eligible lead → held conversation | 10–20% | Speed to lead · Module 5 | Replies a day late; first slot two weeks out; shows toward ~60% |
| Held conversation → enrollment | 15–35% before proof; 25–45% with it | The call · Module 19 | No-fit calls, or a good fit left without a recommendation and an ask |
| Enrollment → past the fit window (14–21 days) | Refunds + disputes ~2–8% of revenue | Expectations and the first fortnight · Module 21 | Refunds from men who expected visible change by week 2 |
| Client-weeks → check-ins | Your own rate | Weekly review and follow-through · Module 21 | Completion sliding after the first plateau |
| Graduate → Round Two or the Hold subscription | ~10–26% · 20–40% | The asks at the measured peak · Module 22 | Asks made at a flat read, or never made |
| Graduate → referral | No planning range | The private referral ask · Module 22 | No ask, so no referrals |

Name each joint's owner: the part of your system, and the module where its fix lives. At solo scale, a joint with no owner is a job nobody does. Then trace from the top. Set each joint beside the bottom of its range, or beside your own ratio once 30 events stand behind it, and mark every joint below. The biggest drop is the one furthest below in proportion. When two are close, fix the one nearer the money, since its fix pays in the next start rather than the next quarter.

**Trace reach to renewal joint by joint, name each joint's owner, and fix only the single biggest drop.**

That's the **Leak Trace**, and its two halves are read in different units. Above enrollment, a drop costs enrollments per 100 eligible leads. Below it, a drop costs lifetime value. The LTV Stack adds that up from the Program, Round Two per graduate, and premium seats, plus months of the Hold, the measurement subscription graduates can keep (Module 10). Revenue per eligible lead, lifetime value times eligible lead → enrollment, spans both halves. That's why it sits on the Growing list: it falls when any joint leaks, and the trace tells you which one.

The call's joint leaks in both directions, and the trace counts both. A close rate below range can mean calls full of men your door should have routed elsewhere. It can also mean good-fit buyers who left without a recommendation, a stated price, and an ask, which costs you the same enrollment a no-show does. Guardrails catch the pushed sale, and the trace catches the un-asked one.

### A flooded joint isn't a leaking one

One joint can also fail the other way, by flooding. The signs arrive together: most weeks above about 6–8 held conversations, shows sliding toward 60%, more than half ending no-fit, and selling eating your content hours. Together they're the Call Cap's trigger: cap free conversations and send the overflow to the Decision Assessment (Module 5). Tightening the door further would plug a joint that's working. A flood can also hide a leak upstream. A full calendar makes reach look fine while half of it is men who needed a different answer than a call.

Most leaks in this business sit early in the chain, and they look like manners rather than strategy. Dan *(composite, Struggler)* finishes your self-assessment at [11 pm] after a shift, the day after a team photo he didn't like. A reply within hours meets him while he still wants a decision. A reply [three] days later meets a man back in his routine, and the lead → held joint drops without a single bad conversation. So check speed to lead first whenever eligible leads grow faster than your habits: a personal reply within hours, and a booking inside 24–48 hours.

Fix one joint at a time, through its owner's move, and trace again after the next full window. Two fixes at once leave neither readable. A repaired joint can also move the biggest drop somewhere else. Faster replies send more men to your calendar, and the next trace may find the call is now the narrowest point.

## 5. Scaling Triggers

Every scaling move gets a trigger written before the evidence arrives, and each trigger points to the module that owns the move. The moments that tempt a scaling move are the moments your judgment is worst. A short that spreads makes a new platform feel overdue. A waiting list makes a jump in price feel safe, and a thin start makes paid reach feel urgent. Each of those weeks is vivid, and a vivid week outweighs a quiet quarter in memory. A trigger written in a calm month carries the quarter's judgment into that week.

Several of these moves also sit near a line of the House Standard. Price past the cash ceiling, about 1–1.25 months of the core buyer's take-home, can reach past his own pocket. Paid reach puts your claims in front of strangers, so its pages have to pass the claims rules first. A premium seat can take review minutes your Program clients paid for. That's why Private, the premium seat with fixed deliverables, follows the Parity Rule: it sells only when it earns at least what a Program seat earns per care hour (Module 13). So every trigger carries a check that can hold it, often one of your guardrails.

**Write each trigger before the evidence: a signal, a threshold, a window, the move, its owner, and the check that can hold it.**

Those six parts make a trigger checkable by anyone, including you on a bad week. Scaling Triggers, the signals set in advance for each scaling move, are checked at the quarterly review. The Price Steps and the Call Cap ride the monthly review instead, because they turn on each start and each month's calendar. The table names each trigger and its owner, and the owner teaches the move:

| Move | Fires when | The check that can hold it | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Call Cap and the paid overflow | Most weeks above ~6–8 held conversations; shows toward ~60%; over half no-fit; selling eating the content minimum | Profit per operator hour per 100 eligible leads, judged over ≥30 events | Module 5 |
| Switching on a rung | Its upstream trigger: the first graduation (Round Two, the Hold); ~20 graduates (paid tools); ~30 alumni (the alumni room); the Program past ~$3.2k (Program Async) | Your week can price the rung's minutes | Module 4 |
| The next Price Step | Starts filling, and the close rate over the last 30 held conversations inside its range; ~5–10% every second start, announced ≥30 days out | Two windows below range after a step hold the next announcement | Module 7 |
| Private | Founding seats while minutes are spare; at Scaling, only when its price per operator hour clears the cohort's ~$1,250–1,400 per care hour | Review turnaround kept | Module 13 |
| Routing help, task-billed moderation and door links | Growing, once moderation and door-link routing overrun the replies line of your design week | Anything touching a minor, distress, a purchase question, or client content stays with you | Module 11 |
| Paid adult reach | Band A: the default test at months 3–4, ~$300–1,000 a month, once your pages pass the claims rules. Bands B–D: only after the Ad Gates (compliant pages, organic proof, an offer that converts without ads) | Cost per eligible lead against a holdout, at or under your affordable maximum (~$10–70) | Module 27 |
| Adding or dropping a platform | Add: reach binds and the editing budget can serve it. Drop: before long-form degrades, or at the second cut of a week past 25 h | Eligible-adult yield by source | Module 25 |
| Crossing the cash ceiling | Scaling, with starts full at the proof band's top (~$3.1–3.2k) and close rates in range | The affordability "no" share, with Program Async, the call-free tier, live under the 20–24 ceiling | Module 13 |

### Triggers are checked at their review

Three rules keep the table honest. Check each trigger at the review it belongs to, never in the week it feels close. A threshold read mid-week is the mood the trigger was written to replace. When two fire together, pull the one that answers your binding constraint and let the other wait a window. And expect triggers to run backward. After a quiet quarter the Call Cap stops binding, and the overflow goes quiet by itself. A platform your editing budget can't serve comes off before long-form thins.

### Paid reach depends on your band

Paid reach is the one trigger that changes with your band, since the band decides whether organic reach carries the plan. In Band A, the channel that never breaks out, $25k needs a reach lever content alone won't supply. So a small test is the default from months 3–4, run as a measurement buy. In this category it tends to cost about $36–100 per eligible lead, above what you can afford at opening prices, so you're buying your real cost per lead. In Bands B–D organic reach still carries the plan. Paid waits for the Ad Gates and scales only while its measured cost stays at or under your maximum.

## 6. When the Signals Disagree

Two numbers usually disagree because one of them is measuring something else. Check both denominators first, since a changed denominator explains more disagreements than a changed business. Then let guardrails outrank growth numbers, and leading numbers outrank trailing ones. Revenue reports on decisions made a start or two ago, while eligible leads and yield report on the starts to come.

**Guardrails outrank growth, leading numbers outrank trailing ones, and a named cause outranks a mood.**

### Revenue can rise while the pool thins

Revenue can climb for a quarter on a Price Step, a strong back end, and starts filled from the waiting list. Underneath it, eligible-adult yield, eligible leads per 1,000 engaged views, can be falling. In this niche the usual cause is packaging: a run of rating or comparison pieces pulls younger viewers, and the share of your audience who can buy shrinks. Revenue can't tell you this for a start or two, because it's still being paid by the leads you had before.

The rule is to let the eligible-lead count decide, read beside yield by source. If the count holds and one piece's yield collapsed, a single video found teenagers, so note it and don't make more of it. If the count falls across sources, the next two starts will be thinner than revenue says. Adult packaging then becomes the month's lever, while revenue still looks healthy.

### A close rate can rise on refunds

A close rate that climbs while refunds and exits climb behind it is selling past fit. Watch the fit window most closely, the first 14–21 days, when a full refund is his for the asking. The refunds are the rate's true value arriving late. Here the usual causes are a softer version of the Honest Answer on calls, or Fit Check wording that drifted until fewer buyers show a signal. The rule is that a climb doesn't count until the refunds from its own starts are explained.

Read refunds by the start the buyers joined, beside the close rate that produced them. Then check the signal count, because signals falling while enrollments rise means the check went soft. Listen to your recorded calls from the climb, and put back whatever changed, in your answer about bone or in the check's wording.

**When the signals disagree.** Say revenue has grown [three] months running and every number in your stage's set sits in range. But [2] of the [4] clients who enrolled after a fit-check signal took a refund or used the week-6 exit right. That's the pro-rata refund for a client who did the work and asks to stop. Among everyone else it's [1] of [30]. Four is far too few for a rate, and Small Numbers Lie seems to say wait. It doesn't govern this case, because a guardrail about people is read by case, never by rate.

Open the four files. If both refunds say the work asked more of their weeks than the written expectations warned, the adjustment after a signal wasn't enough. Rewrite that note so it states the time the work takes and the plateau in plain words. Offer the Starter Path, the free path for anyone who shouldn't buy now, as plainly as the Program. Leave the rate to its window and every revenue lever alone this month. One case read closely can change a process; no single case can change a rate.

## Worked Example: Cole's Quarterly Review at [~$14k] a Month

Cole *(composite operator)* is [14] months in and Growing on both readings. His trailing revenue is about [$14k] a month, with [~95] eligible leads a month and [21] concurrent clients. His channel grew steadily rather than explosively, the Band B kind of year you should plan on. On the last Friday of [March], the month's review and the quarter's fall on the same afternoon, inside the admin line of his week.

**The numbers.** His Growing six come off a one-page sheet he fills in by hand each week:

| Number | This quarter | Planning range |
|---|---|---|
| Eligible leads | [~95]/month, up from [~70] | ~50–150 in Growing |
| Eligible lead → enrollment | [~5%] | 1.5–7%; 3–7% with a proven door |
| Enrollments | [~5]/month, about half without a call | Up to ~8–10 at the Growing ceiling |
| Revenue per eligible lead | [~$145] | ~$25–210 |
| Renewal take | [5] of [26] graduates took Round Two | ~10–26% per graduate |
| Check-in completion | [~88%] | His own; steady for [two] quarters |

The guardrails are clean. He has [one] fit-window refund and no disputes in the rolling 90 days, and complaints run at [0.05%] of delivered email. [Six] of [38] paid-step applicants showed a fit-check signal. [Four] continued with adjusted expectations, [one] took the Starter Path, and [one] got a referral, and no signal-flagged enrollee has asked for a refund. No promotional send reached a paused lead. The affordability "no" share holds at [~11%], and the non-responder share sits at [4] of [26], measured and unpublished until 30. Every review landed inside [48] hours except [one], credited within [2] days.

**The read.** Revenue is up [~15%] on the quarter, so the tempting read is that nothing needs doing. The Monthly Constraint Read disagrees. In each of his last two windows of 30 eligible leads, only [2] reached a held conversation, about [7%] against 10–20%. So conversations bind, through a leak.

The trace finds one joint below range. The eligible share, the close rate at [~31%] across his last 30 held conversations, and Round Two take all sit inside theirs. The drop is at speed to lead. His leads grew by [a third] while his reply habit didn't. Completions from [Thursday] evening wait for his [Monday] reply window, his first open slot sits [9–12] days out, and shows have slid to [~65%].

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** Too few events bind every rate you'd like to read, so your five numbers are counts and your band's waypoints are the yardstick. The default is a weekly look and a monthly lever. The quarter's one big call belongs to the Month-3 Gate, the week-12 read of your conversation volume and conversion (Module 9). The trap is a dashboard built before the business: a dozen charts in month 1 measure mostly the time spent charting.

**Growing.** Conversion binds, and revenue per eligible lead is the number that catches it, because it falls whenever any joint between your door and the back end leaks. The default is one change per window and a full trace every quarter. The trap is celebrating a rising enrollment count while revenue per eligible lead slides: growth bought with more leads, converted worse.

**Scaling.** Care minutes bind, so profit per operator hour becomes the number, with care minutes per client-week beside it. The default is to let price and templated review lift profit per hour while turnaround and the non-responder share hold. The trap is a profit per hour that rises because review got thinner, which the guardrails show before renewals do.

## Standard Check

- **Guardrails beside every sales number.** A close rate or enrollment count is always read beside refunds, disputes, and fit-check counts, so chasing it can't become a softened check or a second ask after a clear no (*Close*; the line on vulnerability).
- **Zero means zero.** Sends to paused leads sit on the weekly guardrail list, and a slip is fixed the day it's found instead of waiting for a review, because stop rules bind automation too (the line on vulnerability).
- **Shares published on schedule.** The non-responder share, the recommendation mix, and the "you don't need Round Two" share go up at 30, with denominators, whatever they show, and first outcome ranges carry a small-sample label (*Name the destination boldly*; the line on fabricated proof).
- **Steps the gate calls for.** A thin fortnight never cancels a step whose gate holds, and each step names what was added, so the price stays plain and plannable (*Present the price*).
