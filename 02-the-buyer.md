# 2 · The Buyer
*Part I — How the Business Works*

**The shift:** from *"My buyer wants a better jawline and is anyone who watches"* to *"My buyer is an eligible, stalled adult who wants to stop guessing. I know his state, his destination, and when he shouldn't buy."*

Dan *(composite, Struggler)* is 24 and works full-time in logistics. For about eight months he has done [his daily routine] most mornings. He takes a comparison photo most weeks, under whatever light the bathroom has, and the [jaw device] he bought now lives in a drawer. None of it was measured, so none of it can be read. On your self-assessment, the box that asks what he wants gets five words: "A sharper jawline, less soft."

A few questions into your first conversation, a different answer arrives. He wants to stop angling away from the camera when the team photo comes out. He wants the people above him at work to take him seriously. Most of all, he wants to know whether the last eight months did anything, because not knowing keeps sending him back to the comparison folder.

The first answer is his surface goal. The second is his destination, and it's what he'll pay for. This module covers the distance between them: who your buyer is, what he wants beneath the jawline, which state he arrives in, and when he shouldn't buy at all. Get those right and the door, the offer, the price, and the call all get easier to build, because each is built for someone real.

## 1. The Stalled Adult

Your buyer is a stalled adult: usually a man between 19 and 32 who has put months into how he looks and can't tell whether any of it worked. He's the core buyer, the employed Struggler every default in this playbook is built around. The band has reasons at both ends. Below it, many of the category's most engaged viewers are minors or have no income of their own; above it, the buyer who pays usually arrives as an Optimizer, the higher-earning buyer who's short on time. And employment describes the core buyer without admitting him: who may buy is settled by the Eligibility Rule, two checks on age and money.

### Stalled matters more than age or income

Stalled is the trait that makes him a buyer. A man who hasn't started doesn't know yet what he's missing, and a man whose effort is visibly working has no reason to pay you. The stalled man has proven he'll do the work and hit the limit of what work alone can show him, so judgment applied to his own record looks like the missing piece.

You'll recognize him by [the category's best-known habit], a device or two, and a photo folder he can't compare. He keeps asking whether he's doing it wrong. He's tired of the category's grift and just as tired of its shrug that it's all genetics, because neither gives him anything to do on Monday.

### He has effort and lacks three things

Ask what he's been doing and the answer is rarely laziness. Dan has done something most days for eight months. What he hasn't had is any of the three things effort needs before it becomes progress, and he can't supply any of them for himself.

- *Direction.* The category offers dozens of levers in no order, some of them promising bone. With no map from his goal to the few that matter for it, his effort spreads across everything, or runs hard at the wrong thing.
- *Feedback.* Nothing he does returns a readable signal. Distance, lens, and light change how a face looks in a photo, so a folder shot under shifting conditions mostly records the conditions.
- *Interpretation.* Even accurate information has to be read against his situation: what can move for an adult, what's noise, what's his to change. Without that reading he swings between the category's promises and its shrug.

Those three gaps travel together, and together they're the **Struggler Gap Triad**, the three things a stalled buyer lacks once effort stops working on its own:

**Effort without direction, tactics without feedback, information without interpretation.**

The Triad explains why more information didn't end his stall, and why free content, however good, can't end it for him. Each gap is a missing structure. Another video adds levers, which widens the direction gap. It adds claims to weigh, which widens the interpretation gap, and it returns no feedback at all. A Struggler who has watched everything you've published can know a great deal and be exactly as stuck, because what he lacks has to be applied to one person.

Read the Triad on every door answer and every call, and note which gap is widest, because that's what he'll value first. A buyer whose widest gap is feedback wants a baseline and a first week he can see. One whose widest gap is interpretation wants the straight answer on what can move. That answer is the Outcome Map, which sorts what adults can change and measure from what's debated and what's never claimed (Module 3).

### Three other buyers cost more than they pay

Three other buyers tempt a new operator, and each offers something real. The Optimizer has money, but positioning the brand around him shrinks the pool and demands polished proof a new brand can't show. Breathing-first looks gentler, because breathing carries less stigma than a jawline, yet its complaints sit next to sleep and snoring signs, which are referral triggers. And parents already pay for braces, so parent-funded teens look like a ready market, one the line on vulnerability rules out whoever holds the card.

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| The stalled adult, 19–32 | Always, as the buyer the brand speaks to | Proof has to be built from zero, and he pays from a monthly budget |
| Optimizer-first positioning | Never as the position; he gets his own lane from month 0 | A smaller pool, and polished proof a new brand doesn't have |
| Breathing-first entry | Never as the door; breathing habits stay one lever inside the program | Health framing on every page, and a door full of buyers whose right answer is a referral |
| Parent-funded teens | Never | A sale to someone who can't consent, paid for by someone who isn't the client |

**Default: the stalled adult.** He's the largest group that can pay from its own income, his stall is what judgment and measurement answer, and his need matches what you can honestly deliver without credentials. Switch the emphasis, not the default, once the Program, your 12-week flagship, climbs past about $3.2k and presses on what a 20–24 buyer can comfortably pay. Then keep a real tier under that price and let packaging, never income targeting, tilt the mix toward 25–34.

## 2. The Destination Ladder

What he asks for and what he'll pay for are different things, and the business is built on the second.

### He names a feature and wants a state

His surface goal is a feature: a sharper jaw, a better side profile, less puffiness in photos. It's real, and it's where he is. It's also the one thing you can't sell him, because in this category the feature is where the promises about bone live. Sell to it and you're pushed toward a structural promise you won't make, or a vague "we'll work on it" that a burned buyer hears as evasion. Ignore it and he feels talked past, which the category has done to him for months.

People name goals as features because features are easy to say. But they want the feature for what it would do, and they want that for how life would feel afterward. The jawline is a proxy he picked because the category told him it was the route. Ask what it would give him and the proxy gives way to a consequence; ask again and the consequence gives way to the state he's after. In this niche that state takes three recurring forms: being taken seriously, being at ease in photos and rooms, and knowing instead of hoping.

### Climb until the answer leaves his face

Watch the climb on a typical buyer: "a sharper jaw" → "I'd look better in photos" → "I'd stop dodging the camera at work" → "people there would take me seriously." Each arrow is one question, some version of "what would that give you?", and the last rung is about his life. That climb is the **Destination Ladder**: three or four "what would that give you?" questions that take a buyer from the feature he names to the destination he'd pay for, with a hard stop at the top.

**Climb until the answer leaves his face, then stop and say it back.**

The stop matters as much as the climb. Once the answer is about his life, you have what the sale needs. One more "why" climbs into his worth ("what would that say about you?"), where the answers are shame and none of them are yours to sell to. Sometimes the top rung is a person: "so she'd notice me." Name it without promising it. That's beyond any program's reach, and the part within reach is how at ease he is in the room.

### Say it back with the obstacle, the plan, and one line beyond his face

Say the destination back in his words, with three things in the same breath: the obstacle, the plan, and one perspective line that places the destination beyond his face. The obstacle and the plan keep a vivid destination an expectation, which sustains effort, instead of a fantasy, which drains it. That pairing is Fantasy to Expectation (Module 14). The perspective line keeps you honest about cause. Being taken seriously runs through how he holds himself, how he's photographed, what he knows about his own record, and parts of his life no program touches.

Name it vividly, because a destination said flatly moves no one. Future-pace the record. Take him to the next team photo, where he's in it without checking the angle. Then take him to his week-12 captures, the standardized photos, laid beside his baseline, where he can see what moved, what didn't, and what he'll do next.

What you never do is join the destination to the feature as cause and effect. "Once your jaw sharpens, they'll take you seriously" is a structural promise and a claim that his face is why they don't, and it fails him wherever his result lands.

### Adrian's ladder, run once

Here's the ladder on Adrian *(composite, Optimizer)*, 31, a consultant who opened his assessment call by asking about precision. His surface goal: "I want the jaw to look defined on camera." You ask what that would give him. "I'm on video with clients all day, and I catch myself checking my own thumbnail." You ask what would change if he weren't. "I'd be thinking about the client." The answer has left his face, so you stop, and you don't ask what the checking says about him.

You say it back: "So the goal is your attention on the client, and a straight answer about what's worth your time. Right now you're guessing, because nothing you've tried was measured. The plan is a baseline, a review on fixed days each week, and a written read at week 6. How you're lit and framed on calls is part of this; it can change this week, and we can measure it. Most of what a client reads on a call was never about your jaw." The destination is his, the obstacle is the guessing, and the perspective line placed the jaw among several things he can move.

Run the ladder once per buyer, early in the first conversation and before any recommendation. Three questions is the default and four the limit. If your door already asked what his goal would change, start a rung up. Write the destination down in his words, because it's the sentence your recommendation will argue from and your written recap will repeat. Stop early when a rung turns up a referral trigger (jaw pain, snoring, a bite concern) or distress, because those answers call for a referral instead.

## 3. Buyer States

Every buyer who reaches your door arrives in one of four states, and the state decides how you sell to him, not what you sell him.

A state is where he stands with the problem and the category right now. A segment is a trait you target and build a product for: young men, high earners, students. Building per segment multiplies offers, splits your proof across thin cohorts, and teaches you to change the product when the buyer only needed a different conversation. A state is temporary: the Struggler whose next gadget fails arrives burned, and the man who kept saying "later" arrives ready once something in his life shifts. **Buyer States**, the four situations a buyer can arrive in, give you the reading:

| State | Signals | Needs | Fears | Pays for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Struggler | Months of solo effort, nothing measured; a photo folder; "am I doing it wrong?" | Direction, feedback, interpretation | That it's genetics and the months were wasted | A plan he can check, and weekly review of his record |
| Burned Struggler | Names a device or course that promised structural change; asks for terms before prices; an early, calm "I need to think" | Verification before belief | Being sold to again, and feeling foolish for it | The same Program, once he has checked the samples and the guarantee |
| Optimizer | Asks about turnaround, privacy, and deliverables before price; short on time; usually 25–35 | Speed, precision as deliverables, privacy | Wasted time; being seen in a program | Speed and privacy: a faster paid assessment, then a premium seat while minutes are spare |
| Ambivalent | Agrees with everything; names a later start; no stake in his own words | A reason of his own, and a real date | Starting and failing again; bad timing | A start date he chose, and a first step small enough to begin |

### The Burned Struggler is a Struggler with a receipt

He's a Struggler who paid for a promise and watched it fail, usually a device or a course that sold structural change. The purchase taught him that confidence is the tell, so your confidence reads as the same pitch in better lighting. His skepticism is earned, and it's an opening: he audits, so a brand built to be audited is the one he'll end up trusting. Theo *(composite, Burned Struggler)*, 26, in sales, fits the pattern. Asked at the door what he has already bought or tried, he names two products and wants the guarantee terms before he'll book.

His "I need to think" is due diligence, so support it. Send the verification kit, a labeled sample written plan and a sample weekly review, with the guarantee terms. Make one firm recommendation and let him choose the decision date. Leave out stakes questions, and keep what he lost on the device far from your price, because a pitch that leans on money already spent is how he was sold the first time.

### The Optimizer is a state, not an age

He has income and little time, so he values speed and exactness over price. He's usually 25–35, and the range includes men over 30 because the state defines him. A man in his late twenties with money and no time is an Optimizer; a man past 30 with months of unmeasured effort is a Struggler. Precision, for him, means deliverables: a fixed turnaround, a written plan with a re-check date, reviews on stated days. It never means a reading or score of his face, which rating apps sell cheaply and the House Standard, the rules this playbook sells by, keeps out of your product.

He's often camera-shy about being seen in a program, so privacy is part of what he buys. His concern can also hide behind the language of optimization, which is why every check runs on him as on anyone. His lane opens at month 0 with the priority tier, a faster version of the paid written assessment, plus a founding Private seat, a premium seat with fixed deliverables, while your minutes are spare. He pays extra for speed and privacy, never for a better result.

### The Ambivalent buyer is waiting for a reason of his own

He agrees with everything and starts nothing, and the mechanism is ordinary deferral. A decision with no date defaults to "later", and in this niche later looks cheap, because outcomes are slow and one more month seems to cost only a month. Sam *(composite, Ambivalent)*, 22, an apprentice electrician, sees the need and keeps saying "after the busy season." More information won't move him; he's short of a reason that's his, said in his words, and a real date to attach it to, like the next monthly start with its real seat cap. A manufactured deadline would lose him, because seeing through it would confirm what the category taught him.

### States move, so read them at every contact

A state is a reading, not a label. It moves inside one conversation: an Ambivalent buyer who names his own stake is a Struggler ready to decide. It moves across months too. So write it in your notes in his words, date it, and read it again at every contact, because a reading that's weeks old will send him down a route that stopped fitting him.

How each state is run on a call is State Routing: cause and follow-through for the Struggler, method and guide for the Optimizer, verification and his own date for the burned buyer, and why now for the Ambivalent buyer (Module 19). The same promise and method wait at the end of all four routes, and the Optimizer's extra speed doesn't buy a different promise. That's the rule to carry: the route, not the offer.

### Women and men over 30 are welcome, not targeted

Women and men over 30 buy from this brand, and the door handles them exactly as it handles Dan: the same fork, question, ladder, and offer. They're welcome and not targeted, for practical reasons. The brand's language, proof, and examples are built for the core buyer, and aiming packaging at a second audience would split every asset between two vocabularies a solo operator's hours can't serve. Nothing in the method or the straight answer changes for them, so a separate pitch gains nothing. The over-30 buyer often brings the category's sharpest question, whether it's too late, and gets the same answer at its evidence tier.

## 4. The Eligibility Rule

Eligibility is two checks and nothing else: he's a legal adult where he lives, and he can pay from his own income or savings without new credit.

That's the Eligibility Rule. It's non-negotiable because each check protects him before it protects you, and because the buyer most likely to tempt you into bending it is the keen one whose fit looks perfect. Your door enforces it on every path.

### The age fork asks "legal adult where you live"

The first check is an age fork at the top of your self-assessment: one question that routes minors away before anything else happens. It asks whether he's a legal adult where he lives, and the wording is deliberate. The age of majority is 18 in most US states, 19 in two, and 21 in one state and one territory, and your viewers live in many countries. "Over 18" is wrong in some places you'll sell; "legal adult where you live" is right everywhere. The fork comes first because this audience skews young, and a door that asks anything before it ends up holding minors' answers.

### The affordability question is policy, word for word

The second check is the affordability question, asked before every paid step in every channel: a paid assessment, a checkout, a payment plan, a direct message carrying a checkout link. It's one sentence, and it doesn't change:

**"Is this comfortable from your own income or savings, without new credit or buy-now-pay-later?"**

Each phrase has a job. "Comfortable" asks more than "possible": a man can technically pay by draining next month's rent, and the question exists to catch exactly that. "Your own" rules out money belonging to someone who isn't in the conversation. "Income or savings" means money he has or earns, not money he expects. "Without new credit or buy-now-pay-later" closes the route buyers his age reach for at checkout, where a stretch quietly becomes a debt.

The answers route without discretion. On a yes, the step goes ahead. On a no, money talk ends: he gets the Starter Path, the free sequenced path for anyone who shouldn't buy now, handed over once. He also gets the pause route, a content-free tag that stops sales sequences and date announcements for 60–90 days (Module 5). Money that isn't his goes to the Starter Path too.

The wording never varies by channel, because a question that softens in DMs or vanishes at a warm checkout makes that channel the loophole, and automation can only honor an answer it can read. Module 19 teaches the question as spoken; here it's policy.

### He feels price in months of take-home

The question asks about comfort because the core buyer feels a price in months of take-home pay, and a yearly salary hides how large a program is to him. In the US, full-time men aged 20–24 earn about $42k a year, roughly $2.8k a month after tax. Full-time workers aged 25–34 earn about $59k, roughly $3.9k a month. The income figures are strong; the take-home figures are estimates built on them. Here's what that does to a price:

| Program price | Months of take-home at 20–24 | Months of take-home at 25–34 |
|---|---|---|
| $2.4k | ~0.86 | ~0.61 |
| $2.8k | ~1.0 | ~0.71 |
| $3.1k | ~1.1 | ~0.79 |
| $3.5k | ~1.25 | ~0.89 |
| $3.9k | ~1.4 | ~1.0 |

A price that looks small against a year's salary is most of a month's pay to Dan. That's why the core container stays under a cash ceiling of about 1–1.25 months of the core buyer's take-home (Module 7). It's why the same price lands differently on Dan and on Adrian. And it's why he should see the full path and its likely total cost before he pays anything.

### Student status and income are never the test

Two shortcuts look like screening and aren't. Student status predicts nothing the affordability question doesn't measure directly. A student with savings from summer work who answers yes is eligible; a salaried man who'd need buy-now-pay-later isn't. A status rule would turn away the first, let in the second, and hang a label on both. Income targeting fails the same way across a whole audience: it measures a proxy, drifts toward the Optimizer-first position you rejected, and suggests that price signals seriousness, when in this brand the standards do.

### Minors get an education lane

Under-18s get public education and a suggestion to see an orthodontist, and nothing else: no email capture, no intake answers, no calls, no offers, no parent-facing pitch. The reasons stack. Any sale to a minor crosses the line on vulnerability, whoever pays. Questions about a face that's still growing belong to clinicians, so the honest answer for a teenager is a referral. Keeping no data is how you make sure no sequence, tag, or ad ever reaches him, and the lane runs daily, because a large share of the category's most engaged viewers are under 18.

Jordan *(composite, minor)*, 16, finishes one of your videos and clicks through. The fork sends him to your public answers and a line suggesting he take his question to an orthodontist, and nothing about him is kept. No content is packaged for his age group and no ad is aimed at it; the lane serves the minors who arrive anyway, and your door implements it.

**When the signals disagree.** Say an adult passes the fork, reads as a Struggler on every signal, and ladders to a clear destination. Then, at the affordability question, he says, "My parents are covering it." Fit says yes and eligibility says no. Affordability comes first, because it's the check that protects him rather than the sale. The question asks whether the decision is comfortable for the person who carries it, and that person isn't in the conversation, so nobody present can answer it truthfully.

Money that isn't his goes to the Starter Path, with its re-entry point said plainly: when it's comfortable from his own income or savings, the door is open. If he adds that he couldn't pay it himself, that's a no, so money talk ends and the pause route is set. A partner with shared finances is a different case, because that money is his too, and a joint decision gets time and a written recap instead of probes. The rule costs you a sale this month. It saves you a dispute from a cardholder who never agreed to anything, and it keeps the brand well clear of parent-funded selling.

## 5. Buying Triggers

Most buyers don't decide because of a video; they decide when something in their life makes the stall expensive.

Those moments are buying triggers, events that turn a stall he can live with into one he wants ended. Five recur in this niche, and every one of them is his, which makes your job to be the obvious, honest next step when one arrives, and never to manufacture one.

| Trigger | What it looks like here | Why it moves him | Respect it by | Never |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pain spike | A tagged photo; a video call that shows his profile; a comment | The status quo suddenly costs something he can feel | A reply within hours, and the straight answer before any pitch | Reopening the moment in your copy; insecurity hooks |
| Failed DIY | The device in the drawer; month eight with nothing to show | Effort alone has visibly run out | Naming the missing measurement as the reason, not him | Recovery pitches built on money he already lost |
| Life event | A wedding, a new job, a move, a birthday | A dated moment with a camera or a first impression attached | Pinning a start near the date, and saying what 12 weeks can show by then | Promising he'll look different on the day |
| Fresh start | A new year, a new month, "after the busy season" | Goal pursuit rises after temporal landmarks | Monthly starts near real landmarks | Countdowns; a price that exists only this week |
| Someone similar succeeding | A friend's or a client's measured story, with its range | Seeing someone like him do it raises his belief that he can | Case stories with range and context; private referral asks | Borrowed proof; implying his result will match |

### A trigger speeds your reply and nothing else

A trigger is the moment a buyer is most willing, and sometimes the moment he's most fragile. The team photo that sent Dan to your door is, for another man, the edge of a fixation. A wedding next month can make a buyer want a promise no one can keep. So a trigger changes his timing and never your checks: the same fork, the same question, the same fit screening, the same honest timeline. A pain spike answered fast and calmly builds more trust than one answered with urgency, because he can tell which reply is using the moment.

### Respect them; never manufacture them

Respecting triggers is mostly being ready for them. That means content that answers the searches men run after one ("is it too late at 27?", "why isn't mewing working?"), a door that replies within hours, and a real start date to pin the decision to. Ask at the door what made him look for help now. The answer names the trigger in his words and tells you which conversation he needs. When Sam says "after the busy season", he's naming a fresh start of his own, so take it seriously: pin a real start near it and ask what changes then.

Manufacturing triggers is the category's habit. Insecurity hooks make the pain spike for him, "new year, new jawline" countdowns fake a fresh start, and comparison content invites him to rate himself against strangers. Each can lift clicks for a week. Each also recruits the buyer most likely to fixate or ask for a refund, trains your audience to wait for the next manufactured moment, and hands the category's harshest critic a screenshot. Buyers who arrive on their own trigger bring their own reasons, and a reason that's his survives the program's first hard week in a way borrowed urgency doesn't.

## 6. Vulnerability and Fit

Some adults who want to buy from you shouldn't. Finding them before anyone pays is what the House Standard requires, and good business would choose it anyway.

### Plan on meeting this buyer regularly

Serious appearance concern is the kind that takes hours of a day and crowds out work, sleep, or seeing people. The evidence on how often it occurs is strong, and it comes from clinical settings:

| Setting | Share with serious appearance concern | Evidence tier |
|---|---|---|
| General population | ~2% | Strong |
| People seeking appearance change | ~5–20%, by setting | Strong in clinical settings; applied to your buyers by analogy |

Read it at its tier. Nobody has measured the rate among men applying for appearance coaching. Your buyers are, by definition, people seeking appearance change, though, so plan on meeting this buyer regularly: at the analogy's range, between one in twenty and one in five applicants for a paid step. Your own fit-check counts, kept in aggregate and without names, will show your real rate.

### The signals are plain, and the product can make them worse

You never label anyone, and you don't need to, because the signals that matter are plain:

- preoccupation that eats hours of his day;
- checking mirrors and photos many times a day, or avoiding them entirely;
- distress crowding out work, sleep, or seeing people, or "everything is ruined" language;
- repeated appearance purchases, or a pull toward surgery;
- fixation on a feature nobody else can see.

In small doses, several of these sound like ordinary Struggler talk. That's why the check is a plain conversation and never a score.

This buyer is also the one your product can hurt. The program measures, and for a man caught in checking, capture tools feed the loop that's hurting him. A result that's real and inside the range you showed can still read to him as failure, because the measurement wasn't what troubled him. That's how a well-run program produces a refund, a dispute, and a screenshot from someone it shouldn't have enrolled.

So the checks run before money moves, and they stop the sale instead of arguing with it. The Fit Check, a short plain-language check before any paid step, turns a signal into a pause (Module 5). There's no payment that day, adjusted expectations go in writing, he gets at least 72 hours to cool off, and no payment plan is offered. Checking or fixation routes to a referral and reading-only content. Acute distress or "everything is ruined" means no sale and a referral conversation, before a program or in the middle of one.

Other signals call for a different professional. Sleep or snoring signs, jaw pain, and bite concerns go to a dentist or doctor, and none of them is yours to advise on. An insecurity-led buyer who isn't in distress can still buy, on the Dignity Route, a way of running the sale that keeps his face out of the stakes (Module 15).

### Caring for this buyer is good business

Every part of the business agrees with the ethics here. The buyer most likely to dispute a charge is the one no result could satisfy, and at solo volume a single dispute is something your processor notices. He's also the client least likely to see his own progress, give a testimonial, or refer a friend, so he spends review minutes and returns no proof.

Visible declines, meanwhile, are a signal a grifter can't afford to send. A brand that turns away minors, buyers who can't afford it, and buyers in distress earns belief from the ones it keeps, because its yes has started to mean something. Each no also has a destination: the education lane and a referral, the Starter Path, or a referral. That's the difference between a boundary and a brush-off.

## Worked Example: Four Buyers in One Week

Cole (composite operator) is in month [4], Early stage, holding [4] conversations a week. His founding group, every early client in one group with monthly entry, is open at [~$1.2–1.5k] for 12 weeks. In one week, four buyers who look eligible reach his door, each in a different state, and a fifth completes it on Friday.

**Monday. Dan: the read is Struggler.** His door: legal adult, about eight months of [his daily routine], a photo folder, one [jaw device], "a sharper jawline." What made him look now: a team photo at work. On a free first call, Cole finds the widest gap is feedback. Three ladder questions reach the destination: taken seriously at work, at ease on camera, and knowing whether the months did anything. Dan names what another year of this would cost him. Affordability: yes, from savings, and the founding price is well under a month of his take-home. No fit signals. Cole recommends the founding group from [the next monthly start], states the price once, and asks. Dan enrolls and books day one. Left alone: the device's cost, and any upsell.

**Tuesday. Theo: the read is Burned Struggler.** Asked what he has already bought or tried, he names a device and a course that both promised structural change. He wants the guarantee terms before booking, and his "I need to think" arrives early and calm. Destination: to stop paying for promises and know what's real for him. Affordability: yes, from income. Cole sends the verification kit and the terms, makes one firm recommendation (the same founding group), and asks Theo to choose his decision date. He picks [ten days out]. Left alone: stakes questions, a second probe of "I need to think", and any word about what the device cost. Follow-up runs on the fixed rule: a recap within 24 hours, one check-in on his date, one close-the-loop message.

**Wednesday. Adrian: the read is Optimizer.** He skips the free conversation and books the priority tier at [~$350–600] from his self-assessment result. Before paying, he clears the fork, the affordability question, and the Fit Check. On his assessment call, the ladder reaches attention on the client and a straight answer about what's worth his time, and he names the stake: more months of paying for things he can't evaluate. The written plan arrives in [48 hours]. It recommends a founding Private seat at [~$4–6k], sold on speed, privacy, and fixed deliverables, with the priority fee credited. He's past the core buyer's age band, and the state did the routing anyway. Left alone: any "precision" that meant reading his face. [He enrolls the following Tuesday.]

## Script: The Destination Ladder Question Set

Run it after he has said what he's tried and before any recommendation. The stop matters more than the count:

1. "What would [his surface goal] give you?"
2. "And if you had that, what would change?"
3. "Where would you notice it first?"

> **You:** "You said you want a sharper jawline. What would that give you?"
>
> **Dan** *(composite, Struggler)*: "Look better in photos, I guess. Less soft."
>
> **You:** "And if you looked the way you want in photos, what would change?"
>
> **Dan:** "I'd stop ducking out of the team photos. And people at work might take me more seriously."
>
> **You:** "Where would you notice it first?"
>
> **Dan:** "Meetings, probably. And I'd finally know if the last eight months did anything."
> *[Stop. The answer has left his face. Don't climb further.]*

> **You:** "So you want to be taken seriously at work, to be at ease when the camera comes out, and to know whether your effort is doing anything. You've spent eight months doing this without knowing whether it works. That's the problem, not you. Picture the next team photo: you're in it without checking the angle, and your week-12 captures sit beside your baseline, so you know what moved. What stands between you and that is the missing measurement, so the plan starts there: a baseline, a review every week, and a written read at week 6. Your jaw is one part of how you come across. How you hold yourself, how you're photographed, and plenty no program touches carry the rest."

Notice the two moves. Three questions climbed from the feature to the destination, and you stopped when the answer left his face; that's the Destination Ladder. Then you said the hard true thing, aimed it at the missing measurement, and put the destination, the obstacle, and the plan in one breath. That's Fantasy to Expectation, keeping a vivid picture from turning into a fantasy.

If he pushes back on the questions, keep his goal on the table and give the reason for asking:

> **Dan:** "Man, I just want the jaw. Can we skip the deep stuff?"
>
> **You:** "Fair. The jaw stays on the list, and I'll tell you straight what can and can't move there. I'm asking what it would give you because that decides what we measure. So where would you notice it first?"
> *[Stop. Let him answer.]*

What you never say is the bridge from feature to destination: "Once your jaw sharpens up, people at work will take you seriously." It promises structure and tells him his face is why they don't. At the low end of any range you could show him, it comes back as a refund request and a screenshot.

Then return to the decision. Let him name what another year of guessing would cost him, and restate one recommendation in the words of his destination: "You said you want to know; this is how you'll know." State the price once, run the affordability question as policy requires, and ask. A yes books day one before you hang up; a clear no ends the ask for this conversation.

## Checklist: Buyer-State Signals

Answer from his door answers and your call notes before recommending anything. Items 1–3 override every state; after them, route by the best fit and write it in his words.

1. Is he a legal adult where he lives (18 in most US states, 19 in two, 21 in one state and one territory)? → If not, the education lane: nothing kept, nothing offered.
2. Did the affordability question come back yes, from his own income or savings? → No: money talk ends, the Starter Path once, the pause route. Money that isn't his: the Starter Path.
3. Did any fixation or distress signal show? → No payment that day, written expectations, at least 72 hours to cool off, no plan. Checking or fixation goes to a referral; acute distress ends the sale.
4. Did he name a purchase that promised structural change and let him down? → Burned Struggler: the verification kit, one firm recommendation, his date.
5. Has he put in months of solo effort with nothing measured? → Struggler: name his widest gap and argue from his destination.
6. Did he ask about turnaround, privacy, or deliverables before price? → Optimizer: the priority tier, and a Private seat only while minutes are spare.
7. Has he named a later start more than once without naming a stake? → Ambivalent: his reasons in his words, and a real decision point within about 2–4 weeks.
8. Is today's state written in his words and dated? → If not, write it before the recap goes out, because states move between contacts.

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** You don't know your buyers yet; you know the ones you imagine. Talk to every eligible adult who books, and after each conversation write his state, destination, and trigger in his words. After about 30 conversations, that file is your state map, and it replaces your guesses. The trap is building content and offers for the buyer you'd like, usually the Optimizer who pays more, instead of the Struggler who books.

**Growing.** Conversations are capped, so the read has to start before the call. Tag states at the door with one or two plain questions ("what have you already bought or tried?", "when would you want to start?"), so his result and your call start on the right route. The trap is letting a tag harden into a label.

**Scaling.** Price nears the cash ceiling, and the state and age mix shifts with it: more Optimizers, more buyers aged 25–34. Keep a real tier under the 20–24 ceiling and move the mix through packaging by state and age, never income targeting; that's the Buyer-Mix Shift (Module 13). The trap is letting the core buyer drift out of the brand's language because the new mix pays more.

## Standard Check

- **Naming the stall.** The Struggler Gap Triad lets you say the hard true thing with force ("eight months without knowing whether it works; that's the problem, not you"), which is *Say the true thing*. The force lands on the missing structure, never on his worth or his face.
- **The destination, never the cause.** The Destination Ladder names what he wants vividly, which is *Name the destination boldly*. It stops at the destination, every destination you name carries one perspective line, and no sentence ties being taken seriously to his jaw.
- **Eligibility in every channel.** The age fork and the verbatim affordability question run before every paid step, DM checkout links and automation included. A no ends money talk and sets the pause route, and fixation signals get a referral before any payment (the line on vulnerability).
