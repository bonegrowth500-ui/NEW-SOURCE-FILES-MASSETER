# 13 · The Premium Lane and the Road to $50k
*Part III — Start, Extend, Run, Grow*

**The shift:** from *"$50k means more clients and more hours"* to *"$50k is price at the cash ceiling, two real tiers, a premium lane at parity, templated delivery, and a record no one can copy."*

At [$26k] a month in profit, with a waiting list for his next start and a week of about 20 hours, an operator decides to double. He writes the plan three ways.

The first version adds hours: a second start each month, more calls, reviews written late at night. It breaks around week 6. Review turnaround slips past its stated window, and the clients who trusted his clock start claiming the credit it promised. The second version adds prices: a VIP tier, a one-to-one upgrade, [a low-ticket course], a bundle. Each line looks like revenue on its own. Together they turn one recommendation into a menu, while the one-to-one seats quietly eat the minutes his flagship ran on.

The third version barely touches his week. It moves the flagship to an older buyer's cash ceiling and keeps a real tier under a younger buyer's. It sells a premium seat only where it earns what a flagship seat earns per hour of care. It templates review so the same minutes carry more clients, and it leans on a dated record no rival can back-fill. That version reaches the top of the range in about 21 hours a week, in year 2 or 3. Here are its math, its honest timing, and the guardrails that keep the core buyer safe.

---

## 1. What $50k Actually Requires

$50k a month in profit is one specific configuration of this business, and your care minutes decide whether you reach it. Care minutes are the review, group-call, and milestone minutes each client takes a week, and at $25k most of them are already spoken for. The Program, your 12-week flagship, enrolls about 8–9 clients a month at about $3.1k. That's near the cash ceiling for a buyer aged 20–24, the most a core container may cost the man it's built for.

The back end fills in the rest. Round Two is a lighter maintenance block for graduates with momentum, and the Hold is a measurement subscription. Add one Private seat a quarter, the premium seat with fixed deliverables, and fees from the Decision Assessment, the paid written plan non-buyers keep. That's roughly $31.5k of revenue and $24.5–26.5k of profit in a week of about 20 hours.

Enrollments can't double from there. At Scaling your care hours hold about 40–45 concurrent clients, which caps new enrollments near 12–15 a month, about half again what $25k needs. So the second $25k has to come from price, a second tier, a matured back end, and leverage.

### The configuration doubles profit without doubling the work

The $50k configuration is the steady state that reaches the top of the target range. Two of its lines don't exist at $25k. Program Async is the Program's weekly review without the live call. The Self-Serve System is a kit of tools for men keeping their own record.

| Line | At $25k | At $50k | What moved it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Program | ~8–9/month × ~$3.1k ≈ $26k | ~8/month × ~$3.9k ≈ $31k | Price at the 25–34 ceiling |
| Program Async | Not offered | ~5/month × ~$2.8k ≈ $14k | A real tier under the 20–24 ceiling |
| Round Two (~18% of graduates) | ≈ $1.5k | ≈ $2.8k | More graduates |
| The Hold | ~20 × $59 ≈ $1.2k | ~45 × $69 ≈ $3.1k | The ramp, and one step |
| Private | ~1 seat a quarter × ~$5k ≈ $1.7k | Only at parity; not counted | Minutes that bind |
| Self-Serve System | Not yet | ~15 × ~$197 ≈ $3k | Tools, after ~20 graduates |
| Decision Assessment fees | ≈ $1k | ≈ $2.2k | Async-first at volume |
| Revenue → profit | ≈ $31.5k → ≈ $24.5–26.5k | ≈ $56k → ≈ $44–47k | Small scheduled steps carry it toward ~$50k |
| Week | ~19.75 h | ~21 h | Templates, not hours |

Read the table by where the new money comes from. Of roughly $24.5k in new revenue, the second tier brings about $14k. The Program's move to the older buyer's ceiling brings about $5k. The back end and the leverage lines bring most of the rest, net of the Private revenue the configuration won't count until a seat earns its hours. Held conversations barely move, from about 8–17 a month to about 9–18, because about 65–70% of enrollments come without a call.

So the ceiling decision outweighs every other choice on this road. The second tier is the largest single line of growth, and it exists only because the Program crossed a ceiling.

**$50k is two tiers at their ceilings, a matured back end, and leverage, inside about 21 hours.**

### Care minutes set the path, and effort can't move them

Profit here is eligible adults × conversion × lifetime value × margin, capped by care minutes (Module 1). At $50k the cap binds. The terms left to move are the ones that raise profit per care minute: price, the back end inside lifetime value, and the minutes each client-week costs.

The week shows how tightly that runs. Of about 21 hours, the care lines take roughly 9. Weekly review covers about 36 concurrent clients at 6–7 minutes each. The group call takes about 2 hours, for the Program tier alone, and milestones about 2 more. Round Two and the Hold share about an hour. Selling takes about 2.8 hours at roughly an hour per enrollment, content keeps its minimum of about 5, and the build line and admin take the rest. Each care line rests on a build: templated review, templated milestones, async assessments, and a tier with no live call.

More hours is the version that fails first, for a reason specific to what you sell. Every review is a read of a man's logs and, at the measurement points, of captures taken to your standard. It's where you catch the lines that call for a referral: distress, fixation, jaw pain, a checking habit. Tired review misses those lines before it misses anything else. So the design caps the week at 20–25 hours, which keeps the review sharp enough to be the product.

### The honest timing is years 2–3, and reach decides which year

About 13 enrollments a month at a proven door's 3–7% means roughly 185–435 eligible leads a month, or about 870 at 1.5%. Eligible leads are adults who could pay from their own income or savings. That's above the month-9 range of Band B, the steady reach band, at 60–150, and inside Band C's 150–400. In engaged long-form views it's roughly 55–725k a month, at a 30–70% eligible share and 2–5 raw leads per 1,000 engaged views. Short-form and Instagram add reach those figures don't count. The eligible share swings the range most, because many of this category's most engaged viewers are under 18 and can't buy anything.

So $50k is the top of the range. It arrives in years 2–3 in Bands C–D, or in Band B once a paid reach lever works. That lever pushes proven pieces to adults, judged against a holdout and the most you'd pay for one eligible lead (Module 27). Plan on $25k, and count $50k as what the same system produces when reach keeps growing. It comes from price and leverage, never hours.

## 2. Price at the Ceiling and the Buyer-Mix Shift

Past $25k, price can rise only where a buyer can still pay it from his own pocket. So the Program crosses the younger buyer's ceiling only with a real tier left under it and a buyer mix that has already moved.

### Each tier sits under its own buyer's ceiling

The cash ceiling is about 1–1.25 months of the core buyer's take-home, payable without new credit (Module 7). Full-time take-home runs about $2.8k a month at 20–24 and $3.9k at 25–34, so the ceiling sits near $2.8–3.5k and $3.9–4.9k. Installments carry their own cap: at most three, each within about a third of monthly take-home. Together the caps place each tier:

| Tier | Price | Months of take-home, 20–24 / 25–34 | Plan cap | Built for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Program Async | ~$2.4–2.8k | ~0.86–1.0 / ~0.61–0.71 | Inside it at both ages | The core buyer aged 20–24 |
| The Program at $25k | ~$3.1k | ~1.1 / ~0.79 | Past it at 20–24 | The core buyer, near his ceiling |
| The Program at $50k | ~$3.5–3.9k | ~1.25–1.4 / ~0.89–1.0 | Inside it at 25–34, up to ~$3.9k | A 25–34 majority |

Read the rows as a design. Program Async gives the core buyer a container he can pay for without borrowing. The Program moves to the lower edge of the older buyer's ceiling, still inside three capped installments. Past about $3.9k even his plan needs a larger first payment from savings, so steps there come smaller and slower.

### Conversion falls as price climbs

Every paid step asks the affordability question: "Is this comfortable from your own income or savings, without new credit or buy-now-pay-later?" Each step toward a ceiling makes more buyers answer no, and for them no is the right outcome. So a step pays only while it loses a smaller share of buyers than its own percentage. At a 75–85% margin, a step that loses nobody lifts profit by about 1.2–1.3 times its size. While seats bind, the waiting list refills the seats a step empties; without one, each lost buyer takes his back end with him.

Nor does price sell itself. Buyers read it as only a weak hint of quality, and where a big price with nothing behind it is the grift's signature, it reads as a warning. What carries a price past the younger buyer's ceiling is what the older buyer can check: deliverables, terms, and your record.

### Two real tiers keep the core buyer inside the brand

Program Async is the tier that lets the Program cross: the same written review on the same clock, the week-6 read, the week-12 re-assessment, and captures on the same calendar. It drops the group call, the Program's one synchronous hour, which saves real care minutes and makes its lower price honest. It stays real while men buy it for its own sake: the man on rotating shifts, the one who'd rather skip calls, the one whose own pocket carries this price. A tier built to make another look good is a decoy. It fails the client at the low end of the range who later sees why it was there, and decoys seldom work when options are described in words anyway.

The order of checks keeps both tiers honest. Show them together before any paid step, premium first, with their delivery math and what his next [six] months are worth spent knowing, and recommend the one his week calls for. The affordability question runs at checkout for the tier he picks. A no, there or on the call, ends money talk. He gets the Starter Path, the free sequenced path for anyone who shouldn't buy now, and the pause route, a tag that stops every sales and date message for 60–90 days. Program Async never follows that no as a rescue.

It opens through the standing door: the result page, the written plan's options, and the next start's regular announcement, with no window price, since a tier sold as an event is a launch. It exists only at Scaling, so no default Early or Growing sample plan recommends it.

### The mix moves by packaging, never by income

Crossing works only if the buyers the new price was built for are already arriving, so the mix moves first. Keeping a real tier under the ceiling while the mix shifts by state and age, through what you publish and how you package it, is the **Buyer-Mix Shift**. It has three levers:

- **Age.** The Age-Up Dial's packaging choices lower the minors' share: adult contexts, calm thumbnails, decision-stage topics (Module 23).
- **State.** Topics like [what a week of review looks like] speak to the man who has tried and is choosing, and raise eligible leads per view.
- **The Optimizer.** The time-poor buyer who pays for speed and privacy needs a public premium page in working-life contexts he recognizes.

Income targeting stays out: no audiences built on income or wealth, no "for high earners" copy, no income question at the door, no tier picked by a guess at his pay. Eligibility is adulthood plus a question he answers himself, and income targeting swaps his answer for your guess. It also turns price into status in a category that sells to young men's insecurity.

**Keep a real tier under the ceiling you cross, and move the mix by packaging, never by income.**

The crossing runs on a test that fits on one card. Cross when all four hold:

1. The Program sits at the proof band's top, about $3.2k, with the proof milestone met: 10 or more graduates with consented testimonials, plus first ranges.
2. Recent starts fill with names waiting.
3. Your close rate over the last 30 held conversations sits inside 25–45%.
4. Your last 30 or so enrollments, read in aggregate from intake age bands, are mostly 25–34.

Then open Program Async from the next start and step the Program in ordinary Price Steps, scheduled raises that each name what was added: about 5–10% every second start, announced at least 30 days out. The guardrail is the affordability "no" share, read in 30-event windows against its pre-crossing level. Two windows above it mean price has outrun the mix. The announced step lands, the next one waits, and the work goes back to packaging, since no new buyer pays less than recent buyers paid.

| Option | Pick it when | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Hold the Program at the 20–24 ceiling | The mix isn't mostly 25–34, or the "no" share is climbing | Profit stops near $25k plus the back end and leverage |
| Two tiers under two ceilings | All four conditions hold | A second tier to present honestly; the live call in one tier |
| One tier past the 20–24 ceiling | Never; it re-declares the core buyer by price | The buyer the brand was built for |

**Default: two tiers.** Price rises for the buyers who can carry it, and the core buyer keeps a container he can pay for. Hold instead when a condition fails or the "no" share stays high for two windows.

## 3. The Parity Rule and the Premium Lane

At Scaling a premium seat earns its place only if it pays what a flagship seat pays for each hour of care it takes. By then, care hours are the one input you can't buy more of.

### A premium seat is priced by the hours it takes

The Optimizer is the buyer most ready to pay more, and he asks for what costs the most minutes: a weekly one-to-one call, same-day answers, "someone on this every day". In this category, precision has usually meant a closer read of the face, a score, a structural "analysis". That's exactly where the line on structural claims sits. And an operator with a waiting list feels a $4–6k seat as pure gain, one more sale in a month when the Program is full.

At Scaling a seat's real price is what it earns per hour of your time, because hours are what binds. A Program seat at the 25–34 ceiling takes about 13–15 care minutes a client-week, all-in. That's roughly 3 hours across 12 weeks, or about $1,250–1,400 per care hour. Standalone one-to-one at 45–75 minutes a client-week earns about $100–330 per delivery hour, and even a well-priced weekly-call seat lands far below the flagship. At equal weekly contact the format doesn't change the result. So the extra hours buy speed, privacy, and attention, and each one comes out of the care the Program's clients were promised.

So hold every premium and back-end seat to one line. It earns at least what a flagship seat earns per care hour, or it's sold only while minutes are spare. That's the **Parity Rule**:

**Seat price ÷ the hours it takes ≥ flagship price ÷ its care hours, or the seat waits for spare minutes.**

Count every operator hour the seat takes, including the call that sells it, since Private is sold on a call. Count the flagship by care hours alone, because most of its selling happens without one.

Reid *(composite operator)* reaches a waiting list at [~$27k] a month and opens Private at [$6k]: a weekly [45-minute] call, same-day replies, and a "precision plan". [Three] Optimizers buy within [five] weeks. By week [7] his turnaround has slipped past its [48]-hour promise twice, and [two] Program clients have claimed the credit his service guarantee pays for a late review. The calculator shows why. Each Private seat takes about [14] hours over 12 weeks, sales call included, so it earns about [$430] an hour against the Program's [~$1,300]. His three seats hold the care hours of about [14] Program seats and earn about a third of what those seats would.

Nothing in the offer was dishonest. Its price came from what felt premium instead of the minutes it spends. Reid honors every deliverable the three clients bought, to the end of their terms, and stops selling the seat. He rebuilds Private async-first: [a recorded plan review at weeks 0, 4, 8, and 12], [written replies within 24 hours on weekdays], and [a 20-minute call at baseline, week 6, and week 12]. That design takes about [7–8] hours, so it reaches parity near [$10k], and the page says so. The "precision plan" becomes a list of dated deliverables under one new line: "We never read or score your face."

Run the rule at every monthly Seat Math reset, when you set seat caps from measured care minutes, and before you open any premium or back-end seat. While minutes are spare, it lets 1–2 founding Private seats at $4–6k run below parity (Module 9). Those minutes would otherwise sit idle, and the seats teach you what the Optimizer wants. Once a waiting list says minutes bind, Private sells only at parity: in practice an async-first seat at about $10k or more, or none. At the $50k configuration the default is no Private until a design passes. Under-pricing fails the buyer too, since a seat priced below its minutes is one you can't deliver on time once the Program fills.

### The Premium Lane gives the Optimizer a path from month 0

The Optimizer needs a way in from the first month that doesn't spend minutes you won't have later. His first step is the priority tier, the Decision Assessment's faster version with a recorded walkthrough, at about $350–600 and credited to the Program or Private. It's a door step, judged like the rest of the door by profit per operator hour per 100 eligible leads. Next come the founding Private seats while minutes are spare, and at Scaling, Private at parity or not at all. That path is the **Premium Lane**.

Precision in this lane means deliverables he can count: a stated turnaround, recorded walkthroughs, replies inside a set window, and a fixed number of calls. His captures follow the same Measurement Calendar as every client's: baseline, around week 6, week 12, then quarterly. An Optimizer who asks for weekly photo reviews is asking for the checking habit that calendar exists to prevent, so Private never adds capture points. Every Private page says, in plain words, "We never read or score your face." It sells speed, privacy, and attention, never a better result.

Sell the lane directly on a public page: who it's for and who it isn't, the deliverables, the price, the seat count, and one next step. That step is the priority tier or a Private call. Keep his privacy visible throughout, with camera-optional calls, a pseudonymous handle if he wants one, and a billing name that says nothing about faces.

**When the signals disagree.** Say [four] Optimizers ask about Private in one month, your next [two] starts are full, and your page still lists a [$5k] seat sold while minutes were spare. Demand says open seats, and the waiting list says your minutes are spent. Go by the minutes. Close the sub-parity seat and say so on the page, listing the async-first seat at parity if it exists. Each Optimizer still gets the priority tier's walkthrough and one recommendation, often the Program at its next start. If the async-first seat isn't built, the surge is your reason to build its deliverables next. One more seat at the old price is out, because the Program clients whose turnaround slips would pay for it.

Held to the rule, the lane says yes to every Optimizer who fits, with a seat you can deliver on time. Today that's the priority tier, next month the Program, and Private once its design earns its hours.

## 4. Leverage Builds: Templated Review and Async Assessments

At Scaling the cheapest profit left is the minutes you stop spending. They come back fastest when you build leverage in the order that returns the most hours for each hour invested.

### The queue pays for itself in order

The Build Queue is that order: leverage builds ranked by hours saved per hour invested, worked on a protected build line of about 1–1.25 hours a week (Module 11). At the configuration, each build is worth this much:

| Build, in queue order | Minutes before → after | What it buys at $50k | Its condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Templated review | 12–20 per client-week → 6–10 | Room for about 13 enrollments a month inside the care hours | About 20 clients reviewed first; disclosed before payment |
| The Decision Assessment template | 1–1.5 h all-in → 0.5–0.75 h, async-first | Paid overflow without selling hours; fees ≈ $2.2k a month | Every plan still ends in one recommendation, "don't buy" included |
| The Async Arc: the call's arc without the call | ~1.3 h of selling per enrollment → ~1.0 | About 65–70% of enrollments with no call | Every check runs before any pay button |
| Round Two and Hold terms | Drafted once | Back-end seats offered at the peak without drafting | Deliverables carded |
| Private deliverables | Fixed and async-first | A seat that can pass parity | Parity, rerun monthly |
| The Self-Serve System | Almost no care minutes | ≈ $3k a month | After ~20 graduates; tools, never decision rules |

The order is the economics. Templated review sits first because it touches every client every week, so each minute it saves repeats across every seat. The assessment template saves hours only on the overflow. The Self-Serve System saves none and earns a little, which is why it waits at the end however good its revenue line looks.

**Build in the order of hours saved per hour invested, and template only what you've disclosed.**

### Templated review is the biggest saving, and disclosure is its entry price

Review at 12–20 minutes per client-week is how you learn what a good review says. At the configuration's roughly 36 concurrent clients, that pace would eat most of the week's 9–10.5 care hours by itself, and at the slow end all of them. At 6–10 minutes the same clients fit, with the group call and milestones still inside the budget.

The template holds what your first 20 or so clients taught you: the stall patterns and what to change for each, stock paragraphs for common weeks, and his markers, the two or three measures agreed at baseline, laid out so a slide shows at a glance. Timing is the whole risk. Template before about 20 clients and you freeze your weakest reviews into the product; template late and review eats the care hours your next start needed.

It shortens the writing, never the reading. You still read every log, because a template can't notice the line where a man mentions jaw pain or says he checked his face all week. Those lines call for a referral conversation. After you template, watch check-in completion and the non-responder share, the clients whose markers didn't move despite the work. If completion sits below its old level for two 30-event windows, the review has gotten too thin to keep men logging. Put minutes back before you add a seat.

Templating has one condition: the client knows before he pays. The Expectation Document, the fuller written terms sent before payment, says how reviews are made (Module 6). You read every log and write or approve every review from a structured template, and the document names any tool that helps and what it touches. Disclosed, the template is a standard a skeptic can check, which is how trust forms where credentials prove little (Module 15). Discovered later, it fails the client at the low end of the range, who would never have endorsed a review he thought was written for him alone.

### Async assessments and the arc move selling off the calendar

The other minutes worth buying back are selling minutes. At $25k about half of enrollments come without a call, at roughly 1.3 hours of selling each. At the configuration about 65–70% do, at roughly an hour each, which is how 13 enrollments fit inside about 2.8 selling hours a week.

Two builds carry that shift. The async-first assessment turns the overflow from the Call Cap, the weekly limit on free fit conversations, into paid written judgment. Its fees from non-buyers grow from about $1k a month to about $2.2k. The Async Arc carries the rest: a warm buyer moves from the result page through a walkthrough and emails to enrollment, with no call.

Neither build may skip a check. The written plan still ends in one recommendation that can be "don't buy", and the plan-usefulness refund still stands. The arc still runs the age attestation, the affordability question, and the Fit Check, the plain-language screen for signals that buying could hurt him, before any pay button. A pause tag still blocks checkout. Automation is where stop rules break most quietly, so every build that removes you from a step carries that step's checks with it.

## 5. Round Two and the Hold at Capacity

At capacity the back end raises profit and results together, as long as each seat stays at the intensity it was priced for.

Lifetime value here is the Program plus Round Two per graduate, plus Hold months, plus any premium seat, with each one's care minutes counted against it (Module 10). Round Two runs about $0.8–1.2k: biweekly async review, an optional group call, and re-captures at its own weeks 6 and 12. About 10–26% of graduates take it. The Hold runs $39–79 a month for the 20–40% who join, with a quarterly re-capture and written review, and it opens into the alumni room at about 30 alumni.

### The back end earns because its minutes are few and scheduled

At the configuration, Round Two brings about $2.8k a month on about 0.4 hours a week, and the Hold about $3.1k on about 0.6. Run both through the Parity Rule. Round Two at maintenance intensity clears the flagship's rate per care hour. The Hold, with one templated review per member per quarter, sits close to it, and its steps inside $39–79 plus the room's one-to-many prompts are what keep it there.

Neither needs a selling hour of its own, since both are offered at the week-12 measurement when his record shows progress. Seat Math caps both lines each month from measured minutes, so a full Hold line waits for next month's cap instead of thinning every member's review. Round Two still goes only to graduates with measured momentum, because a maintenance block sold on enthusiasm alone has nothing to maintain.

They matter more than their revenue suggests. In this category the months after week 12 are when a habit either holds or fades. The Hold's quarterly re-capture, never more often, is where he finds out which. Those re-captures also become the long-timeline part of your record, the part a new rival can't produce on demand.

**A back-end seat keeps its price only at the intensity it was priced for.**

### Intensity creep is how a back-end seat drops below parity

A back-end seat falls below the line one kindness at a time. A Round Two member who asked nicely gets weekly calls; a Hold review turns into monthly messages. Each turns a seat priced for maintenance into a Program seat sold at a maintenance price, and at capacity the clients whose turnaround slips pay for it.

Full intensity has its own rule. It follows only a "misdirected, now corrected" verdict, priced as a Program and shown on the Path and Timeline Card, the pre-payment view of the full path and its cost. When the misdirection was your own review error, the corrective weeks are free. Kept at their intensity, both seats add profit and carry clients into the months that answer "did it hold?"

## 6. The Moat at Scale

At the ceiling a rival can copy your prices, tiers, and pages in a weekend. So the price is defended by what he can't copy: a dated record, and the judgment it was built on.

### The Dated Record turns a high price into a checkable one

The Dated Record is your moat: a published pre-commitment plus a dated log of process metrics from month 1. Outcome ranges join at the proof milestone as a labeled small sample and become a standing log, non-responder share included, from 30 graduates (Module 3).

At $3.9k it does work the price can't. A burned buyer, one who already paid for a device or a course that promised structural change, reads a high price as the pattern he's escaping. He needs something he can check. Two years of turnaround kept, claims paid, fit declines counted, and ranges published beside the men who didn't respond are that something. A rival starting today can't back-fill them, however good his page is.

That's how the credibility ceiling keeps rising after the cash ceiling stops your steps. Every month adds dated entries a newcomer can't match, so the record grows more valuable just as the price stops moving.

Put the record where the price is. Link the dated log beside every tier's price, on the tier card and in the written plan, so a buyer who hesitates at $3.9k can check it in the same minute he doubts. When his question is whether the price is worth it, point him to the log once and let him read. When his answer is that he can't afford it, money talk ends there, record or not. The sign the record is carrying the price is a skeptic who asks about the non-responder share before he asks about the fee.

Scale also changes what the record can say. At about 13 enrollments a month, the standing log soon holds enough graduates to publish Program and Program Async ranges separately, each with its own denominator. Publish them apart. A blended number would hide whether the tier without the call serves the men who bought it, which is the first question a skeptic at the lower price will ask. Published apart, the ranges also keep you honest, since the cheaper tier can't quietly become the Program minus the care while its results sit in public.

**Price at the ceiling rests on the record, and the record rests on judgment you never sell as a kit.**

### The Self-Serve System sells tools and keeps the rules

The Self-Serve System sells logs, the Capture Standard's matched photo conditions, self-review prompts, and walkthroughs, and it stops where judgment starts (Module 4). At scale the reason for that line is economic as well as honest.

The stall taxonomy, your catalog of why men stall and what to change for each, is what makes a 6-minute review possible. It's also the working part behind every range in your record. Sold in a kit, those rules would hand a rival your leverage and hand a buyer a confident wrong verdict with no one reading his record. Kept, they make each of your reviews faster than a rival's and each recommendation harder to match.

The kit still earns about $3k a month at the configuration on almost no care minutes. Its optional review is a Decision Assessment, so a buyer who wants judgment on his record walks back through your door.

## Stage Notes

*Stages: Early < ~$8k/month · Growing ~$8–30k · Scaling ~$30k+, on a trailing 3-month average plus a volume signal.*

**Early.** Minutes are spare and Optimizers are rare, so the Premium Lane's only job is to exist from month 0. The default is the priority tier plus one founding Private seat at $4–6k, with fixed deliverables and its end stated. The trap is selling "precision" as a closer look at his face, the category's oldest premium and the one this lane never offers.

**Growing.** Conversion binds while the ceiling comes into view. The default is a protected build line worked from templated review down, while the Price Steps climb the proof band toward about $3.2k. The trap is opening Program Async early: a cheaper copy of the flagship at a price your core buyer can already carry.

**Scaling.** Care minutes bind, and the top of the range lives here. The default is two tiers at their ceilings, Private only at parity, and the queue's later builds. The trap is reading a waiting list as a reason for more hours or one more sub-parity seat, when it's the signal to price, tier, and template.

## Standard Check

- **Two tiers, both real.** Program Async stands beside the Program before any paid step, premium first, and never appears after "I can't afford it", because that answer ends money talk (*Present the price*; the line on vulnerability).
- **Packaging, never income.** The Buyer-Mix Shift moves the mix with adult contexts, decision-stage topics, and a premium page; no income audiences or "high earners" copy, and no tier worn as status (*Build identity on evidence*).
- **Precision as deliverables.** Every Private page says "we never read or score your face", keeps the standard capture calendar, and sells speed and privacy, never better results (the line on structural claims).
- **Steps that add.** Each step past the ceiling names what was added, and templated review cuts no published deliverable and is disclosed before anyone pays (*Present the price*).
